# The Animating Spirit: Swedenborgian Thought and the Walt Disney Studio

## I. Introduction

The historiography of the Walt Disney Studio has traditionally operated under a presumption of benign secularism, or at most, a generalized, non-denominational Protestantism often categorized by sociologists as "Civil Religion." Within this established narrative, the studio's gravitation toward the Brothers Grimm, Hans Christian Andersen, and Lewis Carroll is explained by simple commercial logic: these authors produced beloved, public-domain stories suitable for adaptation. The presence of spiritual motifs is typically attributed to "indirect transmission"—the idea that Disney animators, in adapting these fairy tales, inadvertently inherited their spiritual DNA without necessarily understanding or endorsing the specific theological systems that birthed them.

This report examines the relationship between Swedenborgian thought and the Disney Studio. The evidence reveals a striking structural compatibility between the studio's animation philosophy and Swedenborgian metaphysics—a compatibility that extends from the studio's core pedagogy to its preferred source material. Whether this compatibility was *deliberate* (conscious selection based on theological awareness) or *functional* (selection based on narrative properties that happened to align with the studio's needs) remains an open question that the evidence does not fully resolve.

The investigation proceeds through three domains:

1. **The Source Material**: The Brothers Grimm, Hans Christian Andersen, and Lewis Carroll each have documented connections to Swedenborgian thought through specific historical transmission paths.
2. **The Studio's Philosophy**: The animation pedagogy developed at Disney—particularly the "Action Analysis" method—shares formal properties with Swedenborg's Doctrine of Influx.
3. **Points of Contact**: In specific cases, particularly the *Johnny Appleseed* production (1948), archival correspondence demonstrates explicit awareness of Swedenborgian content.

### 1.1 The Crisis of Ensoulment

In the 1920s, American animation was dominated by the "rubber hose" style. Characters like Felix the Cat or the early Mickey Mouse were constructed of loose, flexible tubes. They moved with a fluid, rhythmic bounce that was entertaining but fundamentally mechanical. They had no weight, no bone structure, and most importantly, no interiority. If a character moved, it was because the animator moved the pencil, not because the character had a thought or feeling that *caused* the movement.

Walt Disney recognized by the early 1930s that this style had a ceiling. He envisioned feature-length films that could sustain an audience's emotional investment for eighty minutes. To achieve this, his characters needed to appear self-motivated—to seem to have souls. This required a paradigm shift from "animating movement" to "animating intentions."

The solution the studio developed shares a striking formal property with Swedenborgian metaphysics. Swedenborg's theology posits that life flows from the Spiritual (Cause/Will) into the Natural (Effect/Body). The body does not move itself; the spirit moves the body. As Swedenborg wrote in *Arcana Coelestia*: "Action is nothing but the will flowing forth... The body is the obedience." For an animator trying to make a drawing appear alive, this structure maps directly onto the problem: one must not draw the body moving, but rather the *intention* that results in the body moving.

Whether Disney or his team recognized this parallel, or arrived at the same principle through independent artistic reflection, we cannot determine from available evidence. What we can establish is that the studio's technical needs created demand for stories built on the logic of "internal cause drives external form"—and the authors they selected most frequently (Grimm, Andersen, Carroll) have documented connections to Swedenborgian thought.

## II. The Three Literary Anchors

The Disney Studio's preferred source authors were not merely secular fantasists. Each has a documented transmission path connecting their work to Swedenborgian thought.

### 2.1 The Brothers Grimm

The folklore of the Brothers Grimm is often miscategorized as primitive or pagan. However, their work is embedded in a philosophical framework shaped by German Romanticism—specifically, Friedrich Schelling's *Naturphilosophie*, which drew extensively on Swedenborg's metaphysics via the transmission of Friedrich Oetinger.

Schelling argued that "Nature is visible Spirit" and "Spirit is invisible Nature"—a philosophical restatement of Swedenborg's doctrine that the natural world is the "ultimate effect" of the spiritual world. The Grimms viewed folk tales (*Märchen*) not as mere entertainment but as *Naturpoesie* (natural poetry): shattered fragments of an ancient, organic revelation.

This philosophical grounding shaped the narrative structure of Grimm tales. The landscape in Grimm stories is never neutral; it is a reactive mirror of the protagonist's internal state. The forest grasps at those who enter with fear. The thorns part for the worthy prince. This "animate landscape" became a cornerstone of the Disney visual style:

| Grimm Element | Structural Function | Disney Application |
|--------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| The Forest | Environment reflects protagonist's state | *Snow White*: Trees that grasp at the fearful |
| Thorns/Roses | Obstacles responsive to moral status | *Sleeping Beauty*: Hedge opens for the worthy |
| The Well | Threshold to deeper understanding | *Snow White*: Wishing Well as narrative catalyst |

The Grimm stories came pre-loaded with the logic Disney needed: internal states manifest as external forms.

### 2.2 Hans Christian Andersen

If the Grimms provided the animate landscape, Andersen provided the pneumatology—the science of the soul's transformation. Andersen's close friendship with Hans Christian Ørsted, the physicist who discovered electromagnetism and explicitly linked scientific laws to spiritual laws (viewing them as "thoughts of God"), placed him within a milieu highly receptive to Swedenborgian ideas.

Andersen's stories operate as what might be called "ontological thrillers"—narratives about souls crossing between states of being:

| Andersen Story | Narrative Structure | Disney Application |
|---------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| The Little Mermaid | Soul acquiring immortality by crossing degrees | *The Little Mermaid*: Ariel's quest for transformation |
| The Snow Queen | Frozen rationality / intellect severed from love | *Frozen*: Elsa's ice as spiritual isolation |

The structural compatibility is precise: Andersen's narratives require no theological translation for Disney's purposes because they already operate on the principle that internal spiritual states determine external physical reality.

### 2.3 Lewis Carroll

The third anchor, Lewis Carroll (Charles Lutwidge Dodgson), is typically read as a writer of "literary nonsense." However, Carroll's library reveals engagement with Swedenborg's works (*Heaven and Hell*, *Divine Love and Wisdom*), and his intellectual circle included Swedenborgian sympathizers like George MacDonald and F.D. Maurice.

Carroll's mechanics solve specific problems for animation:

| Carroll Mechanic | Narrative Function | Disney Application |
|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Size shifts | Change of state manifests as change of form | *Alice in Wonderland*: Eating/drinking as transformation |
| Talking creatures | Affections made visible as characters | All Disney films: Sidekicks as externalized traits |
| Inverted logic | Natural mind entering spiritual environment | *Alice in Wonderland*: Visual logic of the "mad" world |

Carroll's "nonsense" functions as a map of what happens when natural logic enters a domain governed by different principles—precisely the challenge facing animators depicting interiority.

## III. The Studio's Philosophy and Practice

Having established that Disney's preferred source authors had Swedenborgian transmission paths, we now examine the studio's internal philosophy and its points of documented contact with Swedenborgian thought.

### 3.1 Action Analysis

In 1932, Walt Disney hired Don Graham to train his animators. Graham instituted a pedagogy known as "Action Analysis," which taught that the animator must not draw the thing, but the *force driving the thing*. In his 1937 lecture notes, Graham speaks of the "inner force" that dictates the "outer form."

A memo from Walt Disney to Graham dated December 23, 1935, explicitly articulates this philosophy:

> "The driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character—or all three. Therefore the mind is the pilot."

This directive is structurally identical to Swedenborg's Doctrine of Influx: the physical world (the drawing) is the effect of the spiritual world (the character's intent). The animator does not manipulate the body; the animator depicts the *intention*, and the body follows.

However, a critical point must be acknowledged: **there is no archival evidence that Graham or Disney knew about Swedenborg**. Graham operated in a Los Angeles milieu that included Theosophists and other metaphysical practitioners, but no document connects him specifically to Swedenborgian thought. The structural parallel could result from:

- Direct influence through reading or personal contact (undocumented)
- Indirect transmission through the broader Theosophical/New Thought milieu (plausible but undocumented)
- Parallel discovery of the same principle through artistic reflection (possible)

The honest assessment is that we can document the structural match but not the transmission. Graham's teaching aligns with Swedenborg; whether Graham knew he was teaching Swedenborgian principles remains unknown.

### 3.2 The Johnny Appleseed Production

The strongest documented evidence of the studio's theological awareness comes from *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* (1948).

Florence Murdoch, librarian of the Cincinnati Church of the New Jerusalem and custodian of a major Johnny Appleseed archive, wrote to the studio concerned about the depiction of Chapman's guardian angel. Hal Adelquist, Story Department Manager, replied personally:

> "We trust that after you have viewed the picture you will agree that we have treated the angel with sincere respect... [we] researched the real life character extensively."

This correspondence establishes that the studio was in direct communication with a Swedenborgian Church official during production. It is difficult to research John Chapman "extensively" without encountering his defining characteristic: his missionary zeal for the New Church. Chapman did not just plant trees; he distributed Swedenborgian literature across the frontier, tearing pages from *Heaven and Hell* to leave with settlers.

The film's theme song includes the lyric: "He left his blessings three: Love, and Faith, and the Apple Tree." This triadic structure parallels the Swedenborgian rejection of "Faith Alone"—salvation requires the union of Love and Faith issuing forth in Works. Whether the songwriters intended this Swedenborgian reading, we cannot document. The structural fit, combined with the Murdoch correspondence, makes deliberate encoding plausible—but not proven.

### 3.3 Carl Carmer

Carl Carmer (1893–1976), credited as "Folklore Consultant" on *Melody Time* and *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow*, was a historian of the "Burned-Over District"—the region of upstate New York that birthed Mormonism, Spiritualism, and the American stronghold of Swedenborgianism. His book *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* (1936) documented Spiritualist and utopian communities in New York. His research into the Koreshans (a Swedenborg-influenced commune) was conducted during his Disney employment period.

When Disney hired Carmer, the studio was employing someone with expert knowledge of American esoteric religious history. Carmer would have recognized Swedenborgian content and connections. Whether Disney hired him *for* this expertise, or simply valued his general reputation as a folklorist, we cannot determine.

### 3.4 Personnel with Swedenborgian Connections

Several Disney employees had documented connections to Swedenborgian institutions:

| Personnel | Role | Connection |
|-----------|------|------------|
| Retta Scott | First credited female animator (*Bambi*) | Married into the Worcester family, a prominent Swedenborgian dynasty |
| Mary Blair | Art Supervisor (*Johnny Appleseed*, *Alice*, *Cinderella*) | Member of the Swedenborgian Church (Wayfarers Chapel records, 1974) |
| Carl Carmer | Folklore Consultant | Expert on Burned-Over District esoteric history |

The presence of Swedenborgians in the workforce establishes that people capable of recognizing and transmitting Swedenborgian content were present at the studio. It does not establish that they were hired *for* this purpose or that they actively promoted Swedenborgian content in their work.

## IV. Selection Patterns

### 4.1 What Disney Chose

The studio's selection pattern shows consistent preference for the three Swedenborgian-connected authors across 75+ years:

| Era | Film | Source |
|----|------|--------|
| 1937 | *Snow White* | Brothers Grimm |
| 1950 | *Cinderella* | Perrault / Grimm |
| 1951 | *Alice in Wonderland* | Lewis Carroll |
| 1959 | *Sleeping Beauty* | Perrault / Grimm |
| 1989 | *The Little Mermaid* | H.C. Andersen |
| 2010 | *Tangled* | Brothers Grimm |
| 2013 | *Frozen* | H.C. Andersen |

This consistency is notable. Whether it reflects deliberate selection based on theological properties, or functional selection based on narrative properties that happened to correlate with theological structure, the evidence does not determine.

### 4.2 What Disney Rejected

The studio rejected or abandoned several major projects:

| Rejected Project | Documented Reason |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| *Don Quixote* | Episodic structure; protagonist seen as unsympathetic by test audiences |
| *Chanticleer* | Executive decision (Katzenberg felt audiences wouldn't accept rooster lead) |
| *The Odyssey* | Epic structure too long; would require cutting that loses coherence |
| *Mort* | Pratchett blocked it, feeling Disney would "take the heart out of it" |

It is possible to observe that these rejected projects share a structural feature: their environments tend to function as neutral backdrops or obstacle courses rather than moral mirrors. *Don Quixote* operates on irony; *The Odyssey* treats landscape as challenge to overcome rather than reflection of inner state.

However, this observation does not establish causation. Disney did not document rejecting these stories *because* they lacked correspondential logic. The documented reasons are practical: episodic structure, unsympathetic protagonists, excessive length, rights issues. The rejected stories may lack the structural features that Swedenborgian-adjacent stories possess, but we cannot claim the studio filtered for these features without archival evidence stating that criterion.

## V. Modern Applications

The patterns observed in the classic era provide a lens for analyzing modern productions, though such analysis is necessarily interpretive.

### 5.1 Wish (2023)

The 2023 centennial film *Wish* can be read as structurally inverting the classic Disney formula. Where earlier films depicted internal states driving external forms, *Wish* externalizes the "wish" as a physical object—a blue orb that can be extracted and hoarded. The song "This Wish" declares "We are our own stars," locating the source of power within the self rather than in relationship to something beyond.

Whether this inversion was intentional, and whether it contributed to the film's weaker critical and commercial performance, we cannot determine. The structural reading is available; the causation is not documented.

### 5.2 Snow White (2025)

The 2025 live-action *Snow White* can be read as returning to the structural pattern of the classic films. The princess transforms from passive recipient into active agent; she does not merely wish but works. The theme song "Waiting on a Wish" articulates a theology of preparation—wishing as active disposition rather than passive desire.

The structural parallel to *Johnny Appleseed* (salvation through labor rather than faith alone) is notable. Whether the filmmakers consciously intended this parallel, we have no evidence.

---

## VI. Conclusion

The evidence assembled here supports a **structural compatibility thesis**: the Walt Disney Studio's animation philosophy and preferred source material share formal properties with Swedenborgian metaphysics. The three primary source authors (Grimm, Andersen, Carroll) have documented transmission paths connecting them to Swedenborgian thought. The studio's "Action Analysis" pedagogy is structurally identical to the Doctrine of Influx. In at least one case (*Johnny Appleseed*), archival correspondence demonstrates explicit contact with Swedenborgian institutions.

What the evidence does *not* fully resolve is the question of intent. Did the studio select Swedenborgian-compatible material *because* of its theological properties (deliberate selection)? Or did the studio select material that worked for animation and happened to share structural properties with Swedenborgian thought (functional selection)? The distinction matters for understanding whether Disney represents conscious transmission of a theological tradition or convergent discovery of narrative principles that Swedenborg also identified.

The *Johnny Appleseed* production provides the strongest case for deliberate awareness. The broader pattern of source selection—returning to Grimm, Andersen, and Carroll across 75+ years—is consistent with deliberate selection but does not prove it. The Action Analysis pedagogy matches Swedenborgian structure precisely, but no archival evidence documents that Graham or Disney knew they were articulating Swedenborgian principles.

The honest assessment: the Disney Studio operated on a philosophy structurally compatible with Swedenborg. This compatibility extended from the training program to the source material. Whether anyone at the studio recognized this compatibility—outside of specific cases like *Johnny Appleseed*—remains an open question.
