# The Law of Correspondence in the Arts of Expression: From Swedenborg through Delsarte's American Expositors to the Disney Studio

## Tracing the Documented Lineage of the Studio's Method, from *Divine Love and Wisdom* to *The Art of Moana*

---

> **Abstract.** The Disney studio's method of animation, "the mind is the pilot" and "the artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force", has the structure of Swedenborg's doctrine of influx and correspondence. The library's Disney case had no documented route by which that structure reached the studio, and it filled the gap with an atmospheric milieu of Theosophy and New Thought. This thesis traces the route through the tradition of expression, link by link, in the primary and archival record. François Delsarte formulated a "law of correspondence" for the human person from a Catholic and hermetic inheritance, not from Swedenborg. A feature test against Aquinas, the Zohar and Swedenborg finds nothing in his system that only Swedenborg could have supplied. His American expositors laid a Swedenborgian layer over him. Monroe called him "Swedenborg geometrized". Stebbins's standard textbook quotes *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219 and defines correspondence in the words of a New Church minister. Northrop taught "the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg". Volkonsky built his Russian manual on Stebbins, and his one mention of Swedenborg follows her Swedenborg chapter. Stanislavsky attended Volkonsky's courses and recommended his book, and *An Actor Prepares* moves the actor by a triad of inner forces. In 1937 Don Graham's class recommended that book as "helpful reading along these lines", and it taught the law in one direction only, refusing the reverse. That is Swedenborg's direction, not Delsarte's two-way law. The central finding is a documented lineage for the studio's method. The central argument is that the expositors' layer acted as a filter: it set a hermetic inheritance in Swedenborg's order and direction, and passed that order on without the name. The method the studio teaches is the core of the science of correspondences Swedenborg restored, received as craft.

> **Keywords.** law of correspondence, François Delsarte, Genevieve Stebbins, Edward Madeley, Sergei Volkonsky, Stanislavsky, *An Actor Prepares*, Don Graham, Action Analysis, Walt Disney Studio, hermeticism, quality gradient, transmission, *Divine Love and Wisdom*

## 1. Introduction

The library's Disney case rests on a method. On 23 December 1935 Walt Disney asked his drawing teacher, Don Graham, for a course in "what the force behind the movement is", and he named that force: "the driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character—or all three. Therefore, the mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them" (Disney, 23 December 1935). Graham taught it in the studio's classes: "The artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force" (Graham, 21 June 1937); "it is the emotion and mood that come first, the action comes second" (Graham, 22 February 1937). Eighty years later the studio's artists still reason the same way. The producer of *Frozen II* explains that water "holds our history" because of what water does. The writer of *Encanto* says the family's gifts "didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality" (Julius 2019; Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022). The companion studies have shown that this method has the structure of Swedenborg's doctrine of influx and correspondence. *The Mechanics of the Soul* reads Graham's classes as a secularized doctrine of influx. *The Crisis of Ensoulment* shows why the studio needed such a method in 1932–1937. *The Parsimony Problem* counts the pedagogical strand among the five lines of its cumulative case. *The Diffracted Light* follows the method into the art books of the Revival. Each of them, however, has had to leave one question open, and *The Animating Spirit: A Cultural History* (§4.4) names it plainly: "we possess no document where Don Graham writes 'I am teaching Swedenborg'." The library filled the gap with atmosphere: Theosophy at the Chouinard Art Institute, New Thought in Los Angeles, Kandinsky's "inner meaning". It called the result "organic selection", a pedagogy that "happened to align" with Swedenborgian principles.

This thesis asks the question that the gap leaves. If the studio's method is the law of correspondence applied to the body, by what route did that law reach the studio's classroom? The question has a sharper form, which the author of the framework put after the art books had been read. The artists do not describe the commonplace that emotion is put into action. They describe an inner function expressed in an outer form, reached from what the thing does. That is not a commonplace. It is a specific doctrine, and a specific doctrine has a history.

The answer the record gives is a chain, and every link in it can be read in the documents. In the nineteenth century the French teacher of expression François Delsarte formulated a "law of correspondence" for the human person: "To each spiritual function responds a function of the body. To each grand function of the body corresponds a spiritual act" (Delsarte, in Stebbins 1901, 67). Delsarte's own sources, as the scholar of his manuscripts reads them, were Catholic and hermetic: Thomas Aquinas, the Kabbalah, the Emerald Tablet (Waille 2012; Waille 2015). He was not Swedenborg's disciple. His American expositors, however, read his law through Swedenborg and taught it that way. Lewis B. Monroe called Delsarte "Swedenborg geometrized". Genevieve Stebbins's standard textbook quotes *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219 on will, forces and action, and defines correspondence in the words of the New Church minister Edward Madeley: "no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure, but one founded alike on the inward and outward nature of the things". A popular handbook of 1896 calls it "the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg" (Stebbins 1901, 109–114, 139; Northrop 1896, 10).

From that textbook the law passed to Russia. Prince Sergei Volkonsky built his Delsarte manual of 1913 on Stebbins: he marks her book as used and translates her tables, and his one mention of Swedenborg, "from Pythagoras to Swedenborg", follows the order of her Swedenborg chapter (Volkonsky 1913, 49, 203–204). Stanislavsky attended seven of Volkonsky's courses and recommended his book to the students of his studios (Autant-Mathieu 2012). The first part of Stanislavsky's account of the actor's work, *An Actor Prepares* (1936), moves the actor's "spiritual and physical creative instrument" by three inner motive forces, "mind, will, and feelings", "a triumvirate, inextricably bound up together" (Stanislavski 1936, 229–230). In February 1937 the members of Graham's class recommended to one another, as "helpful reading along these lines", *An Actor Prepares* and Boleslavsky's *Acting: The First Six Lessons* (Graham, 22 February 1937). Decades later the studio's teachers named the tradition outright. Eric Larson, one of the Nine Old Men, set Stanislavsky's "There is no inner experience without external physical expression" beside Walt's memo. Walt Stanchfield taught Delsarte by name (Stanchfield 2009).

The central finding is therefore documentary. The studio's method has an ancestry that can be read in print and in archives, and the textbook through which it travelled taught it as Swedenborg's doctrine of correspondence applied to the body. The route is not the Theosophical atmosphere the library had assumed. It is the tradition of expression: oratory, acting and mime, the arts whose subject is the outward form of an inner state.

The central argument is interpretive, and it is the author's. The structure of the law came down a long road: the hermetic "as above, so below", the Kabbalistic map of the body, the Thomist union of soul and body. Delsarte inherited the structure from that road and observed the rest for himself. His American expositors did not edit him. They laid a Swedenborgian layer over his system, and the layer acted as a filter. It gave the order (will united to understanding, issuing through forces into action), the definition (correspondence is not arbitrary), the singular name (the Law of Correspondence) and the direction (from within outward). Whoever learned Delsarte from them learned him in Swedenborg's terms. Downstream the names fell away, and what travelled to Moscow and then to the studio on Hyperion Avenue was the filtered content without the names. This is why the studio teaches the law in Swedenborg's direction, from inner to outer, where Delsarte's own law ran both ways. It is also why a method taught as craft can be recognized, in the framework's terms, as the restored key working through a culture rather than as the fragment it replaced. The thesis does not claim that Delsarte drew on Swedenborg, that Graham read him, or that any artist of the studio knew the source. It claims what the record shows: the law the studio teaches came to it through a teaching that had been read through Swedenborg, and its impulse, in the form the studio received, is his.

The argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 sets out the method:
- the reading of correspondence (§2.1);
- the grades of evidence (§2.2);
- the framework's account of access to the science of correspondences, which supplies the terms of the argument (§2.3);
- the model of a layer that filters without naming (§2.4).

Section 3 states the law in Swedenborg's own words, so that every later link can be measured against it. Section 4 turns to Delsarte: his law in his words, his sources, and a feature test that asks whether anything in his system can only have come from Swedenborg. Section 5 documents the American layer (Monroe, Stebbins, Madeley, Northrop), the change from "correspondences" to "the Law of Correspondence", and the teaching of the law in Los Angeles two decades before the studio's classes. Section 6 follows the textbook to Russia: Volkonsky's manual, Stanislavsky's attendance at his courses, and the inner motive forces of *An Actor Prepares*. Section 7 reads the studio's own documents, from the memo of 1935 to the art books of 2016–2022, with attention to the direction in which the studio teaches the law. Section 8 weighs what the hermetic line preserved and what the Swedenborgian layer supplied, draws out the implications for the framework, and states the limits of the evidence. The Appendix sets out the chain link by link, with the strength of each link.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading

Correspondence, in the framework this thesis works within, is the organic relation between a natural form and the spiritual reality it expresses. It is not allegory, which joins a sign to a meaning by convention, and needs a key or codebook to decipher. It is not metaphor, which says that A is like B. It says that A is B at the natural degree. The library's working criterion comes from *The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* (§2.2): an allegory is "validated by a key or codebook", a correspondence "by the functional analogy of the object itself". Light corresponds to wisdom because light lets the eye distinguish forms, which is what understanding does. The correspondence is grounded in what the thing does.

Two features of the doctrine matter most for what follows. The first is the ground in function. Swedenborg locates correspondence in "the functions and uses of the bodily members" (*Heaven and Hell*, hereafter *HH*, §96): the inner reality flows into the use, and the use takes on a form in the natural world. The second is direction. The natural "springs from and has permanent existence from the spiritual world, precisely like an effect from its effecting cause" (*HH* §89). The body's action "is only an effect from the internal and the external of man's spirit; for the body is only an obedient instrument" (*Divine Providence*, hereafter *DP*, §103). Correspondence runs from within outward. A natural form can be *read* back to its cause, but it does not produce the cause.

These two features give the thesis two questions to put to every link in the chain. First, does it ground the outer form in the inner function, or in convention? Second, does it run from within outward, or both ways? A teaching that answers "function" and "from within" stands in the doctrine's own form. A teaching that answers otherwise may still carry the principle, but not in Swedenborg's form. The questions are asked of Delsarte, of his expositors, of Stanislavsky and of the studio, and they turn out to discriminate between them.

### 2.2 Source Tracing and the Grades of Evidence

The thesis is a transmission argument, and every link in it is held to the library's rule of source tracing: the claim is followed back to the original document, and the full path is kept. The sources were read in their own texts:
- Swedenborg in the Academy of the New Church edition, section by section;
- Delsarte's *Oratory* compilation (1893) in full text;
- Stebbins's textbook (6th ed., 1901) in the chapters that matter, with the book's page numbers;
- Northrop's handbook (1896) and Madeley's manual (1883) in archive.org full text;
- Volkonsky's manual (1913) in a scan of the printed book, read in Russian on the page;
- the Brazilian journal's 2012 special issue on Delsarte in full, including Waille's study of Delsarte's sources, Autant-Mathieu's study of Delsarte in Russian theatre, and Ruyter's study of Delsarte in America;
- Waille's article of 2015 paragraph by paragraph;
- Graham's class transcripts on the scan images of the typescripts;
- Stanchfield's lectures and Larson's notes in full text;
- the art books of *Moana*, *Frozen 2* and *Encanto* in full text.

Where a book could only be searched and not downloaded (Whyman 2008, *An Actor Prepares*), the quotations are exact but the page numbers are given only where the search returned them. Everything consulted, and everything searched for without result, is recorded in the research project for this thesis (`research/law-of-correspondence-in-expression/`).

The links are not all of one strength, and the argument depends on keeping them apart. Three grades are used throughout:
- **Documented**: a primary or archival source states the link. Stebbins quotes *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219; Volkonsky marks Stebbins's book as one he used; Graham's class names *An Actor Prepares*.
- **Strong inference**: two documented facts close a link between them that no single document states. Stanislavsky's triad of inner forces descends from the Delsartean trinity he learned through Volkonsky.
- **Reading**: the framework's interpretation of the documented facts. The expositors' Swedenborgian layer acted as a filter.

The Appendix gives each link its grade.

Two cautions from the library's rules apply with particular force. First, an objection is not owed. This thesis does not go looking for a counter-reading to set against the chain, and it does not inflate a counter-finding because it is adjacent. Where the record genuinely limits the claim, as it does on Delsarte's own sources, the limit is stated and the claim is drawn to fit it. Second, a scholar's judgement is weighed on its evidence. Franck Waille's conclusion that Delsarte was not Swedenborg's disciple rests on Delsarte's manuscripts, and it is accepted here because the manuscripts carry it, not because it is the consensus.

### 2.3 The Quality Gradient and the Problem of Access

The framework supplies the terms in which the chain is read. *The Transmission of the Ancient Word* argues that correspondence is ontological law, operating whether or not anyone reads it, while access to the knowledge of it varies across history (§II.A–B). Swedenborg says as much of his own age: "To them [the ancient people] the knowledge of correspondences was the chief of knowledges … But at this day that knowledge has been so completely lost that no one knows what correspondence is" (*HH* §87). The framework names three routes of access (*Transmission* §II.E):
- the intuitive faculty of the most ancient peoples, now lost;
- transmission of the codified system from those who held it;
- direct instruction, which is how Swedenborg describes his own.

From these it builds a quality gradient (§§VII, X), which places the traditions between the ancient world and Swedenborg in a third tier of fragmentary access:
- Hermeticism keeps "the principle itself", "as above, so below", but "not the dictionary" (§VII.B).
- Kabbalah is a "parallel development with different organization": a detailed key, organized on the Tree of Life and overlapping the Swedenborgian system "significantly but not completely" (§VII.D).
- The quality marker of the tier is the inability to construct: the principle can be invoked but not consistently applied (§VII.G).

Swedenborg's restoration supplies "the systematic, transmittable key that restores the ability to read correspondence at the spiritual and natural degrees" (§VIII.D). The fourth tier is the re-emergence of the system in culture, in Grimm, Andersen, Carroll and Disney (§IX).

*The Golden Chain of Prisca Theologia* (Part IV.4.1) adds the distinction that matters most for an art of expression. Hermeticism tends toward sympathy, the manipulation of the lower to draw the higher; Swedenborg's doctrine toward signification, the reading of the lower as the expression of the higher. *The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* (§5.2) treats the Zohar's Adam Kadmon and Swedenborg's Greatest Man as convergent witnesses to one structure.

Delsarte's system sits exactly on the boundary these documents draw. Its structure came to him from the hermetic and Kabbalistic tier, and his American expositors read it with the restored key. The question of this thesis is therefore also a question about the gradient. When a Tier 3 inheritance is read through the Tier 4 key and taught onward as a craft, which of the two does the craft carry? The answer the record gives in Sections 5–7 is that it carries the key's order and direction, and drops the inheritance's theology.

The framework's own Disney section names two vectors of transmission, source material and personnel (*Transmission* §IX.D). This thesis documents a third, pedagogy, which the framework's pedagogical implication anticipates: "the Science of Correspondences can be taught again" (§XI.F).

### 2.4 Layer, Filter and Name: Transmission Without Attribution

A doctrine can travel without its name, and a transmission argument has to say how. The model used here has three terms.

A **layer** is an interpretive frame that an expositor sets over a received system without altering the system's text. The original remains legible beneath it, but the reader meets it through the frame. A **filter** is what a layer becomes downstream. Readers who learn the system from the expositor learn it in the expositor's terms. When they in turn teach it, they pass on what the frame let through: its order, its definitions, its direction. They do not necessarily pass on the frame's name or source. A **name** is the explicit attribution, "Swedenborg says", which is the part of a teaching most easily dropped when it crosses a language, a discipline or a culture.

The model distinguishes transmission from two alternatives that the evidence must be tested against. One is **convergence**: two sources describe the same structure because both have seen it, without any line between them. The framework allows for convergence, since correspondence is held to be real and therefore discoverable in part by observation (*Transmission* §II.E). Waille's title, *The Two Sources of Delsarte's Teachings*, observation and metaphysics, names the convergent element in Delsarte's own work (Waille 2012). The other alternative is **independent inheritance**: two systems share a structure because both received it from a common older source. Delsarte and Swedenborg may share features because both stand downstream of the hermetic and Kabbalistic tradition.

To separate transmission from these alternatives, the thesis applies a **feature test** (§4.3). Does the receiving system carry a feature found *only* in the proposed source, a detail that neither observation nor the common inheritance would supply? If it does, transmission from that source is shown. If it does not, the source is not shown to be the origin, though it may still be the frame through which the system was read and taught. The test is an argument from resolution: detail that exists only in a high-resolution source cannot be recovered from a low-resolution one, so its presence downstream marks the route. It is applied twice:
- to Delsarte's own system, against Aquinas, the Zohar and Swedenborg;
- to what the studio received, against Delsarte's system and the American layer over it.

The two applications give different answers, and the difference is the thesis.

## 3. The Law as Swedenborg States It

Swedenborg states the law that the expression tradition would apply to the body in three places that belong together. The first is the general statement in *Heaven and Hell*, where the person is the case nearest to hand:

> "Since man is both a heaven and a world in least form after the image of the greatest, there is in him both a spiritual and a natural world. The interior things that belong to his mind, and that have relation to understanding and will, constitute his spiritual world; while the exterior things that belong to his body, and that have relation to its senses and activities, constitute his natural world. Consequently, everything in his natural world (that is, in his body and its senses and activities), that has its existence from his spiritual world (that is, from his mind and its understanding and will) is called a correspondent." (*HH* §90; Ager translation throughout)

The second gives the ground of correspondence in the body: not resemblance but use. "This inflow of heaven is into the functions and uses of the bodily members; and the uses, since they are from the spiritual world, take on a form by means of such things as are in the natural world, and thus present themselves in effect. From this is the correspondence" (*HH* §96). The form follows the function, and the function follows the inner reality.

The third is the most exact, and it is the one the American expositors would quote. In *Divine Love and Wisdom* (hereafter *DLW*) Swedenborg confirms the doctrine of discrete degrees "by the conatus, forces and motions in dead and in living subjects":

> "It is known that conatus does nothing of itself, but acts through forces corresponding to it, thereby producing motion; consequently that conatus is the all in forces, and through forces is the all in motion; and since motion is the outmost degree of conatus, through motion conatus exerts its power. Conatus, force, and motion are no otherwise conjoined than according to degrees of height, conjunction of which is not by continuity, for they are discrete, but by correspondences." (*DLW* §218)

He then applies it to the living person:

> "Living conatus in man, who is a living subject, is his will united to his understanding; living forces in man are the interior constituents of his body, in all of which there are motor fibers interlacing in various ways; and living motion in man is action, which is produced through these forces by the will united to the understanding. … These three do not act by what is continuous, but by what is discrete; and to act by what is discrete is to act by correspondences. The interiors of the mind correspond to the interiors of the body, and the interiors of the body correspond to the exteriors, through which actions come forth." (*DLW* §219)

Read as a teaching for an art of expression, the passage contains the whole method the studio would later call its own. There are three degrees, and they are discrete. The first is the will united to the understanding: in the studio's words, "the mood, the personality, the attitude". The second is the forces of the body, the "force behind the movement" that Graham taught the animators to draw before the form. The third is the action, the outward form in which the first two are seen. The conjunction between them is "by correspondences", and power lies in the outmost, "through motion conatus exerts its power", which is why the animator's whole art has to be put into the drawn action. The direction is one way: "the body is only an obedient instrument" (*DP* §103).

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Action, motion of the body | The outmost degree, in which will and understanding exert their power (*DLW* §§218–219) |
| Forces in the body (motor fibres, the interiors of the body) | The middle degree: conatus made active (*DLW* §218) |
| Will united to understanding | Living conatus: the first degree, the cause (*DLW* §219) |
| The functions and uses of the members | The point at which inflow takes on natural form (*HH* §96) |
| The body as a whole | "Only an obedient instrument" of the spirit (*DP* §103) |

Two further passages fix the background against which the later links stand. Swedenborg holds that the knowledge of correspondences was once "the chief of knowledges" and "flourished among the Eastern nations and in Egypt", and that it has since been lost (*HH* §87). He holds also that threefoldness is in everything created: "In every thing created, greatest as well as least, there are these three, end, cause and effect" (*DLW* §154). Both claims will be met again: the first in the expositors' account of the law as an ancient and lost principle of art, the second in Delsarte's trinity "present in all Creation". The law, in short, was stated in full in 1763, with its order, its ground in function, its discreteness and its direction. The question for the rest of the thesis is how much of that full statement reached the studio, and by what hands.

## 4. Delsarte and the Hermetic Inheritance

### 4.1 The Law in Delsarte's Words

François Delsarte (1811–1871) was a singer and teacher in Paris who built, out of his own failures as a performer and years of watching people, a system of the expressive human being. He taught it in courses and lectures but never published it. What survives is his manuscripts, now in the Delsarte Collection at Louisiana State University, and the records his pupils made. In English these were gathered in the *Delsarte System of Oratory* (1893), which brings together the expositions of the Abbé Delaumosne and Angélique Arnaud with Delsarte's own *Literary Remains*.

The law appears in his address to the Philotechnic Society of Paris, which Stebbins prints:

> "Man considered from the point of view of art presents three orders of essential functions, each one depending upon a proper and determined organic apparatus. These apparatuses engender three orders of corresponding products. Thus, there results in the phenomena three states, three species of acts, three languages, each one of which should be studied in itself and in its relations of association, of succession and of hierarchy. These three orders of functions, of apparatuses and of languages constitute the natural division of the phenomena of the human personality and of its triple causality. This manner of looking at man shows us the role of his two natures in all their manifestations. To each spiritual function responds a function of the body. To each grand function of the body corresponds a spiritual act." (Delsarte, in Stebbins 1901, 67)

The French is « À chaque fonction spirituelle répond une fonction du corps; à chaque grande fonction du corps répond un acte spirituel » (Waille 2012, n. 31). The law is a law of functions. It does not say that a gesture resembles a feeling. It says that every inner function has its bodily function, and that the body's great functions answer to inner acts. In this respect it stands in the doctrine's own form (§2.1). It grounds the outer in the inner function, and it is, in Swedenborg's words, the inflow "into the functions and uses of the bodily members" (*HH* §96) seen from the side of the performer.

The rest of Delsarte's system is built on a threefold division of the person into life, soul and mind, the vital, the moral and the mental. Each part of the body is a zone in which one of the three is best expressed, and each zone is in turn divided by three, so that the three "interpenetrate" and give a ninefold accord. Delsarte's "thermometers" mark the zones: "the eyebrow is the thermometer of the mind; the shoulder is the thermometer of the life; the thumb is the thermometer of the will" (*Delsarte System of Oratory* 1893). Art, in his definition as Arnaud reports it, is "the appropriation of the sign to the thing" (Stebbins 1901, 111–112), or as Volkonsky would set it at the head of his book, "the finding of the sign corresponding to the essence" (Volkonsky 1913, 6).

Two observations follow from his own words. First, the law runs both ways. Its second clause makes the body's great functions answer to spiritual acts, and Waille draws the consequence: if psycho-spiritual phenomena flow into the body, "bodily manifestations also have a direct influence on the psycho-spiritual sphere" (Waille 2012, 312). When Dalcroze opened his *Rhythmic Gymnastics* (1906) with Delsarte's law, he gave it as "to each spiritual function corresponds a bodily function, and vice versa" (Autant-Mathieu 2012, n. 7). Second, nothing in the compilation names Swedenborg. A full-text search of the *Oratory*, in both its archive.org and Project Gutenberg texts, finds no mention. The frame of the compilation is Catholic. It speaks of "three worlds, of the nature of which man partakes … The *natural*, the *intellectual*, and the *supernatural*". Man "fulfils his course by the light of the senses, the reason, or the light of grace". "This marvelous correspondence of the organs with the sentiment arises from the close union of soul and body" (*Delsarte System of Oratory* 1893). His manuscripts define the Trinity as "present in all Creation" (Delsarte 1839, cited in Waille 2012, 311).

### 4.2 Delsarte's Own Sources

The scholar closest to Delsarte's manuscripts is Franck Waille, whose doctoral thesis (Lyon 3, 2009) worked through the Delsarte Collection. His account of Delsarte's sources is the base on which this section stands. Delsarte, he writes, "is at once a fervent and convinced Catholic and an avowed member of the nebulous milieus of the Christian esotericism of his time. His referents are at once the great Western theologians and the great referents of esotericism: Hermes Trismegistus, the Kabbalah, *Illuminisme*" (Waille 2012, 308). Among these one figure dominates, Thomas Aquinas, in whom Delsarte found an experimental cast of mind like his own and a matrix for formulating what he had observed. The two pillars of the system are the Trinity and "the theory of universal correspondences": correspondences between macrocosm and microcosm, God and man, the spiritual and the material (Waille 2012, 311).

The hermetic root is explicit. "Delsarte bases himself unambiguously on one of the principal reference texts of hermetic literature, the Emerald Tablet", whose opening is quoted exactly in the notebook of his American pupil Steele MacKaye: "That which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below, to accomplish the miracles of one thing only" (Waille 2012, 311, citing the Delsarte Collection, box 12b, folder 6). Delsarte's training in Christian and Hebrew Kabbalah came, Waille shows in a later study, through "illuminist freemasonry" (Waille 2015, ¶36). On the question this thesis most needs answered, Waille is direct: « Delsarte n'était pas plus un disciple éloigné du mystique suédois Emmanuel Swedenborg qu'un tenant de l'occultisme » (Delsarte was no more a distant disciple of the Swedish mystic Emmanuel Swedenborg than an adherent of occultism) (Waille 2015, ¶62). His note to that sentence names the pages where the filiation had been asserted: Stebbins's chapter on the Trinity, which is examined below (§5.1), and Ruyter.

One misreading must be set aside. The Portuguese translation of Waille's 2012 article glosses *Illuminisme* as "a movement inspired by the doctrines of mystics such as Swedenborg and Böhme". That gloss is the translator's note, marked N. T. and citing the *Petit Robert*. It is not Waille's statement, and it cannot be used to place Swedenborg among Delsarte's referents.

So, on the best evidence, Delsarte's law reached him down the long road the framework calls the third tier: the hermetic principle, the Kabbalistic system and Thomist theology. His own observation of people, Waille's second source, filled it out. That is the inheritance. The question is whether anything in it is nevertheless Swedenborg's.

### 4.3 The Feature Test

The feature test (§2.4) asks whether Delsarte's system carries a detail found only in Swedenborg, one that neither observation nor the hermetic, Kabbalistic and Thomist inheritance would have supplied. Delsarte's formulations were set against three bodies of text:
- Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae*;
- the body map of the *Tikkunei Zohar*, in the passage known as "Patach Eliyahu";
- Swedenborg.

The result is in the table.

| Delsarte's formulation | Aquinas | Zohar | Swedenborg | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Trinity "present in all Creation" | "in all creatures there is found the trace of the Trinity" (*ST* I q.45 a.7) | — | "In every thing created … there are these three, end, cause and effect" (*DLW* §154) | Shared; Delsarte's wording is Thomist |
| "The soul being the form of the body" | "the soul is the form of the body" (*ST* I q.76 a.1) | — | Not his formula | Thomist marker |
| Three worlds, natural, intellectual, supernatural; light of the senses, reason, grace | Nature and grace; natural and supernatural | — | Natural, spiritual, celestial | Thomist marker |
| Correspondence through the function of the organ | Sensitive operations "through a corporeal organ" (*ST* I q.78 a.1) | Each sefirah in its limb | Inflow "into the functions and uses of the bodily members" (*HH* §96) | Shared by all three |
| Life, soul, mind, with the moral (love) highest | Intellect above will "simply and absolutely" (*ST* I q.82 a.3) | — | Celestial love above spiritual wisdom | Shared with Swedenborg, against Aquinas, but also Augustinian and Franciscan |
| The three "interpenetrate", giving a ninefold accord | Perichoresis: the Persons in one another | — | Discrete degrees, which "do not act by what is continuous" (*DLW* §219) | Not Swedenborg's; Trinitarian |
| Mind in the head, the moral in the torso, life in the limbs | — | "The brain is Ḥokhmah, it is inner thought. Binah is the heart"; Ḥesed and Gevurah the arms; Netzaḥ and Hod the thighs | The head holds "love, peace, innocence, wisdom"; the breast "the good of charity and of faith" (*HH* §96) | Matches the Zohar; differs from Swedenborg on the head |

No feature of Delsarte's own system is found only in Swedenborg:
- **Thomist markers.** The soul as the form of the body; nature and grace.
- **Trinitarian and Kabbalistic structure.** The interpenetration of the three, which is Trinitarian, and the placing of thought in the head and understanding in the heart, which is the Zohar's.
- **Points shared with Swedenborg.** The trinity in all things, correspondence by function, and the primacy of love. These are also shared with older traditions, and none of them requires him.

The test supports Waille: Delsarte's detail does not come from Swedenborg.

In the framework's terms, Delsarte's system is a fine example of the third tier. It is a detailed key, systematic within its own frame, with "different organization" from Swedenborg's (*Transmission* §VII.D). It has one notable strength: it constructs. Delsarte did not merely invoke "as above, so below"; he applied it to every part of the body and built an art on it. The Zohar's body and Swedenborg's Greatest Man stand in the library as two witnesses to one body-correspondence knowledge (*Mundus Imaginalis* §5.2), and Delsarte's map belongs to the first of them. What remains to be seen is what happened when that map was read, in America, through the second.

## 5. The American Layer

### 5.1 Monroe and Stebbins

Delsarte's system crossed the Atlantic with Steele MacKaye, the American actor who worked with him in Paris from October 1869 until the Franco-Prussian War drove him home in July 1870 (Ruyter 2012, 459–460). In Boston it was taken up by Lewis B. Monroe, founder and director of the Boston University School of Oratory, who put it into the school's curriculum and taught it himself. In the second phase of American Delsartism the representative figure was Genevieve Stebbins (1857–after 1933), teacher, performer and writer (Ruyter 2012, 460–461). Her *Delsarte System of Expression*, first published in 1885 and in its sixth enlarged edition by 1901, became the standard English textbook. It is on her pages that the Swedenborgian layer can be read.

It is laid over the system at the foundation, in the chapter on the principle of the Trinity. Stebbins first surveys the threefold principle across the ancient world:
- "The Druidical triad";
- the Egyptian, Greek and Persian figures of divine goodness, wisdom and power;
- "the oracle of Serapis";
- "Pythagoras, the Samian philosopher";
- the trinities of "the Hindoos" and of Chinese philosophy;
- the Platonic hypothesis.

Then she gives the frame in which the whole is to be read:

> "The above ideas agree with Swedenborg. He defines trinity as consisting of 'love, wisdom and power. Love, being the origin and parent of all existence, is called Father; wisdom, which is the form of love, is named the Son, and the only begotten; while the power, consisting in the perfect union of love and wisdom, going forth in creative energy and life-imparting influence, is the Holy Spirit.'" (Stebbins 1901, 109)

She draws the principle of expression straight from it: "Every created thing is composed of two parts: life-power or energy, and a form to show this power in effect. This demonstrates to us an important fact, namely, that there must be a union of two forms — one spiritual, the other material; the spiritual form being the life-power or energy, and the material form being the one which appears to the senses. The latter is the form by which life-power or energy is brought into action." And then she quotes the passage of *Divine Love and Wisdom* on which the studio's method would turn:

> "Swedenborg writes: 'Three degrees: living endeavor, living power, and living motion. The endeavor in a man who is a living subject is his will united to his understanding; the living powers in him are what constitute the interior of his body, in all of which there are moving fibres variously interwoven; and living motion in him is action which is produced through those powers by the will united to the understanding. Powers have no potency but by action of the body.'" (Stebbins 1901, 109–110; cf. *DLW* §219)

The application to the performer's body follows on the next page. "Now, according to our philosophy, matter has no form of itself. It is being that forms the matter. That head, torso and limbs, which you have just named, are but the covering of a spiritual head, torso and limbs." Delsarte's own definition of the trinity is quoted after this, already framed: "The triune in deific essence is Love, Wisdom, Power" (Stebbins 1901, 110–111). Three pages later she records the verdict of the Boston teacher who had brought Delsarte to his own pupils. Monroe called the chart of the ninefold accord "the key of the universe", and "He also called Delsarte 'Swedenborg geometrized'" (Stebbins 1901, 113–114).

The layer lies over the system and does not replace it. Stebbins prints Delsarte's fragments, his Philotechnic address, his charts and his terms; the text is his. What she sets around it is Swedenborg's:
- the order of will, forces and action;
- the trinity of love, wisdom and power;
- the principle that "it is being that forms the matter".

She is not uncritical of Delsarte's metaphysics. In the later chapters of the same book she traces his philosophy, unsparingly, to "the cabalism of the hermetic writers". It is "an esoteric Christianity; a compound of cabalistic lore and Platonism filtered through the imaginative brain of an artistic French student", which she judges "of small value" to the practical artist (Stebbins 1901, 384–386). His system of expression, by contrast, she judges "true, so far as it goes", resting on "self-evident principles in nature". She names its principle:

> "This fixed principle is the great Law of Correspondence, a law old almost as man. … although he may have borrowed ideas from Swedenborg, Delsarte was the first among modern students to unearth this lost principle of art, which gave to antiquity its artistic greatness and which furnishes the cornerstone of all the important systems of expression of to-day." (Stebbins 1901, 389–390)

Stebbins thus holds both lines at once, and she keeps them apart. Historically she traces the inheritance through the cabala and the hermetic writers ("the so-called hermetic doctrine of correspondences", p. 420). Doctrinally she reads it through Swedenborg, whose statement of the law she quotes and whose definition of correspondence she adopts. That is exactly the relation the framework would predict between a third-tier inheritance and the restored key (§2.3). The structure came down the old road, and it was read by the new light.

**Correspondential key** (Stebbins's reading of the performer's body):

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| The performer's head, torso and limbs | "The covering of a spiritual head, torso and limbs" (Stebbins 1901, 110) |
| Living motion: the visible action | Will united to understanding, acting through the body's powers (*DLW* §219, as Stebbins quotes it) |
| The material form of any created thing | "The form by which life-power or energy is brought into action" |
| The ninefold chart of expression | "The key of the universe"; Delsarte as "Swedenborg geometrized" (Monroe) |

### 5.2 Madeley's Definition

In the chapter on the legs, Stebbins stops to define the term on which the whole system rests. "The science of semeiotics is the science of signs, of correspondences. Correspondence is derived from three Latin words, cor-re-spondeo, and it means literally to answer again from the heart." She gives the source of her etymology in a footnote, "Science of Correspondences: Rev. Edward Madeley", and then quotes the same author for the definition itself:

> "The material form should correspond to the inner form, should answer 'thought to thought, heart to heart.' 'Correspondence is no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure, but one founded alike on the inward and outward nature of the things by which we are surrounded.'" (Stebbins 1901, 139)

The quoted sentence is from Edward Madeley's *The Science of Correspondences Elucidated: The Key to the Heavenly and True Meaning of the Sacred Scriptures*, issued by the Swedenborg Publishing Association at Germantown in 1883: "Correspondence, then, is no arbitrary relationship, like metaphor or figure, but one founded alike on the inward and outward nature of the things by which we are surrounded" (Madeley 1883). Madeley was a minister of the New Church, and his book is a manual of Swedenborg's correspondences for reading the Bible. At this point a Swedenborgian definition of correspondence enters the standard textbook of the art of expression word for word, as the answer to the question what correspondence is.

The definition matters beyond its source, because it is the criterion. It excludes the arbitrary ("like metaphor or figure") and grounds the relation in "the inward and outward nature of the things". That is the same line the library draws between correspondence and allegory (*Mundus Imaginalis* §2.2). It is also the line the Revival filmmakers would draw for themselves, when they said that the powers of the Madrigals "didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality" (Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022). Stebbins's next sentences apply it at once to the body: "I see an outward manifestation, viz., a child laughs. I infer that the child is pleased; it cries, I know it is displeased."

### 5.3 Northrop and the American Tradition

What Stebbins taught her readers became, within a decade, what the general public was told. Henry Davenport Northrop's *The Delsarte Method of Expression and Oratory* (Chicago, 1896) was a popular compilation: an exposition of the method followed by recitations for "schools, lodges, public entertainments". Its account of Delsarte's philosophy reads:

> "Delsarte was a man of religious feeling and knew religious books better than other books. He was acquainted with the lore of the priests, to which he was indebted for his philosophy. Tradition affirms that he was much interested in, and studied Swedenborg. This theory is confirmed by the fact that certain fundamental ideas in his philosophy and the expression of them are intrinsically Swedenborgian. One of the fundamental principles of Delsarte's philosophy is the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg, who held that the material world corresponds to the spiritual world and is the manifestation of man's mental being. In other words, that the spiritual world is symbolized in the physical world. Applied to expression, the interpretation of this law is: 'Every expression of the face, every posture of the body, corresponds to, or is but an outward expression of, an inner emotion or condition of the mind.'" (Northrop 1896, 10)

Under the next heading, "Law of Correspondence", the body is mapped: "the head in its poise, etc., represents intellect better than any other part of the body; the trunk, affection; the limbs, power."

Northrop reports two things, and they should be weighed separately. The first is a "tradition" that Delsarte studied Swedenborg. The manuscript scholarship does not bear this out (§4.2), and the thesis does not rely on it. The second is the substance of what American readers were taught: the law of correspondence "was discovered by Swedenborg", and its meaning for the body is that "every posture of the body … is but an outward expression of an inner emotion or condition of the mind". That second report is documentary evidence of the layer. By the 1890s the law of expression was taught in America under Swedenborg's name, and in Swedenborg's direction, from the inner condition to the outer posture. Northrop's formula is the one the studio would state in 1935 as "the mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them".

### 5.4 From Correspondences to the Law of Correspondence

A small change of grammar marks the layer. Waille, discussing the formula he calls "the law of human correspondences", records how the American disciples named it: "Delsarte's American disciples call 'the law of correspondence' (Law of Correspondence) what we propose to call 'the law of human correspondences'. In hermetic milieus it is 'the theory of correspondences' or 'the idea of universal correspondences', always in the plural" (Waille 2012, n. 30, confirmed by Antoine Faivre). In the hermetic tradition, correspondences are many: sympathies and analogies between this and that, macrocosm and microcosm. In the American line, correspondence became one law.

The singular is not a neutral choice in English of the period. "Correspondence" in the singular, as a law of the relation between spirit and nature, was the form in which Swedenborg's doctrine was read. Madeley speaks of "the science of correspondences" in his title but defines "correspondence" as a single relation. Northrop says "the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg". Stebbins writes "this fixed principle is the great Law of Correspondence". The difference corresponds to the one *The Golden Chain of Prisca Theologia* draws between hermetic sympathy and Swedenborgian signification (§2.3). A plurality of correspondences is a field for operation. A law of correspondence is a principle for reading: every outer form answers to an inner state, and the form is to be read for it. When Waille sees in the American singular a sign that Delsarte "elaborated a formulation of his own" of the hermetic theory, he identifies the place where the formulation was given its American, Swedenborgian reading.

The renaming does not prove that each American user of the phrase had Swedenborg in mind. It shows the frame in which the law was taught: as one law, with one direction, read from the outer form to the inner cause. That is the frame the following links carry forward.

### 5.5 The Law in Los Angeles

The American line reached Los Angeles before the studio did. Henrietta Hovey (1849–1918), who published under her earlier name Henrietta Russell, learned Delsarte in the 1870s at the Boston University School of Oratory in Monroe's own classes, and from MacKaye's lectures. Monroe is the teacher who called Delsarte "Swedenborg geometrized". Hovey studied further in Paris with Delsarte's son Gustave and taught for nearly forty years. At the end of her life she settled in the Los Angeles region, came into contact with Ted Shawn and Ruth St. Denis, and lectured on Delsartism at their Denishawn school. Shawn took private lessons with her in the summers of 1915, 1916 and 1917. She became, in Nancy Lee Chalfa Ruyter's judgement, "the most important source of Delsartean knowledge" for Shawn, whose *Every Little Movement* (1954) would carry Delsarte into the history of American modern dance (Ruyter 2012, 463–465).

This is milieu, not transmission. No document found links Denishawn to the Disney studio, and the thesis does not claim one. It establishes that the Monroe line was taught in Los Angeles, in the city's leading school of dance, some twenty years before Graham's classes. Hovey also stands at the head of the Russian branch of the chain, which is how the law in fact reached the studio. Volkonsky quotes "the well-known American 'Delsartean', Mrs. Russell" in his manual (§6.1). The same Monroe pupil thus taught in Los Angeles in person and reached Moscow in print.

## 6. The Russian Passage

### 6.1 Volkonsky's *Expressive Man*

Prince Sergei Mikhailovich Volkonsky (1860–1937) had been director of the Imperial Theatres and became, after 1910, the leading Russian advocate of both Dalcroze and Delsarte. In 1913 he published *Vyrazitel'nyi chelovek: Stsenicheskoe vospitanie zhesta (po Del'sartu)* (The Expressive Man: The Stage Training of Gesture, after Delsarte). His bibliography marks with an asterisk "the works which the author used in composing the present book". There are five:
- Arnaud's *François del Sarte* (1882);
- Delaumosne's *Pratique de l'art oratoire* (1874);
- Giraudet's *Mimique, physionomie et geste* (1895);
- Werner's *Delsarte System of Oratory* (1893);
- Stebbins's *Del Sarte System of Expression* (New York, 1892).

He lists Henrietta Russell's *Yawning* without an asterisk (Volkonsky 1913, 203–204). In the body of the book he draws on Stebbins openly. His tables of gesture are "taken from the book of Mrs. Stebbins". He quotes "the well-known American 'Delsartean', Mrs. Russell". The historian of Russian theatre Marie-Christine Autant-Mathieu, working from the archives of the Moscow Art Theatre, concludes that Volkonsky "drew his inspiration essentially from the works of Genevieve Stebbins and Alfred Giraudet" (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 374).

Volkonsky saw the Swedenborgian layer in his American source, named it, and set it aside. Chapter V, "The System", opens:

> "Delsarte's followers, in setting out his system, like to begin not only with philosophy but even with religion. The threefold principle he laid at the base of the system is carried through all times — Chaldeans, Egyptians, Druids; through all the philosophical teachings from Pythagoras to Swedenborg; through all religious doctrines, ending with the doctrine of the Holy Trinity. True, Delsarte himself saw the strength and infallibility of his system precisely in its being built on a principle equally applicable to everything that exists in this world. But since we are interested in the science of the expressive man, and interested only from the point of view of the manifestation of the inner man in his outward form [проявленія внутренняго человѣка во внѣшнемъ его образѣ], then, to narrow the frame and not to leave the ground on which we already stand, I shall lead the exposition not from religion, not from philosophy, but from man." (Volkonsky 1913, 49)

The sentence summarizes Stebbins's chapter on the Trinity. Its order is hers:
- the ancient peoples (the Druids, the Egyptians, the Chaldeans of her later chapter);
- Pythagoras;
- the philosophers;
- the Trinity.

Its terminus, "to Swedenborg", is the point at which she wrote "The above ideas agree with Swedenborg" and quoted *Divine Love and Wisdom*. Volkonsky's single mention of Swedenborg is a summary of the American layer, written by a reader who had it in front of him. Autant-Mathieu describes what followed: Volkonsky set out the triad and its bodily correspondences "ignoring the religious dimension, that is, the philosophy of the system" (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 377).

What he kept is the content the layer had framed. His book announces its subject as "the manifestation of the inner man in his outward form". He sets at its head Delsarte's "Art is the finding of the sign corresponding to the essence" (Volkonsky 1913, 6). He wrote to Stanislavsky, in the letter that accompanied the book, that their two systems aimed at "the same thing: the truth of life in performance". "How can the face express what I feel if the muscles … do not submit to my feeling?" (letter in the MAT archives, quoted in Autant-Mathieu 2012, nn. 32–33). The name was dropped at this link, and the order of the law was kept: the inner man first, the outward form his manifestation.

### 6.2 Stanislavsky at Volkonsky's Courses

The link from Volkonsky to Stanislavsky is the best documented in the chain, because the Moscow Art Theatre kept records:
- **The courses.** On 7 October 1911, during the last rehearsals of Craig's *Hamlet*, Volkonsky began courses in rhythmic gymnastics for the theatre's actors. Stanislavsky, Kachalov, Moskvin, Knipper-Chekhova and others attended (Autant-Mathieu 2012, n. 8, citing Vinogradskaja's chronicle of Stanislavsky's life). Volkonsky became a shareholder of the theatre the same year.
- **Rome, winter 1911.** He read Stanislavsky his notes on the laws of stage speech, while Stanislavsky showed him fragments of his System (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 376).
- **The opera studio.** Stanislavsky entrusted him with training in stage speech in his opera studio, and "attended seven of his courses, taking notes on them" (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 374).
- **The book.** Volkonsky's *The Expressive Word* was often cited by Stanislavsky. But "it is in fact *The Expressive Man*, the book 'after Delsarte', that the director-teacher recommends to the students of his studios". The source is Stanislavsky's own materials for *The Actor's Work on Himself* (*Sobranie sočinenij*, vol. 3, 1990, pp. 76–77; Autant-Mathieu 2012, 374).

Autant-Mathieu gives the reason. Stanislavsky "found parallels between his own search for inner truth and Delsarte's aspiration to 'justify his effects logically; psychologically, as much in gesture as in the inflections of his voice'" (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 374–375). The English-language scholarship agrees. Rose Whyman's Cambridge study of the System states that "Stanislavsky investigated and made use of the work of Delsarte and Dalcroze", identifies Volkonsky as "an expert on Delsarte and Dalcroze" who taught at the MAT from 1911, and compares exercises in the System's line with "Delsarte's decomposing exercises, as described by Genevieve Stebbins in *Delsarte System of Expression* (pp. 83–6)" (Whyman 2008).

Two independent historians, one working from the Moscow archives and one from the System's texts, thus place Stebbins's textbook inside the formation of Stanislavsky's System: through Volkonsky's courses, through the book Stanislavsky recommended, and through specific exercises. The religious and philosophical frame did not travel. As §6.1 showed, Volkonsky had set it aside, keeping only its single sentence. What travelled was the law in the form the layer had given it: the inner man manifested in the outward form.

### 6.3 The Inner Motive Forces

Stanislavsky's account of the actor's work on himself reached English readers in 1936, in Elizabeth Reynolds Hapgood's translation of its first part, *An Actor Prepares* (cited here from the Theatre Arts printing of 1948). This is the book the Disney animators would recommend to one another the following year (§7.2). Its twelfth chapter is "Inner Motive Forces" (Stanislavski 1936, 229). The actor, it teaches, "turns to his spiritual and physical creative instrument. His mind, will, and feelings combine to mobilize all of" his powers. These three are not separable faculties but "a triumvirate, inextricably bound up together". The actor must study their "reciprocal interaction", and he can work on the whole by way of a part: "we can turn to one member of the triumvirate, perhaps the mind" (Stanislavski 1936, 229–230). The book's whole teaching is framed by the relation of "inner and outer": only "when an actor feels that his inner and outer life on the stage is flowing naturally" is the performance true.

The triad has a history. Thomas Leabhart, writing on Delsarte in the *Mime Journal*, finds an equivalence between Stanislavsky's three inner forces (the centres of thought, of emotion and of action, as Bella Merlin describes them) and the Delsartean trinity of the mental, the emotional-spiritual and the physical. Autant-Mathieu reports the comparison approvingly (2012, n. 3). The comparison is a strong inference, not a documented derivation: Stanislavsky does not name Delsarte in *An Actor Prepares*, and a full-text probe for the name returns nothing. But the route by which the trinity could reach him is documented in §6.2, and the shape is the same. Three inner forces, bound into one, issue through the body as action.

Set beside the source of the American layer, the triad acquires its depth. Stebbins had taught the Delsartean trinity through *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219: "The endeavor in a man who is a living subject is his will united to his understanding; the living powers in him are what constitute the interior of his body … and living motion in him is action which is produced through those powers by the will united to the understanding." Stanislavsky's "mind, will, and feelings" issuing through "the spiritual and physical creative instrument" is that order, stated by a director for actors, without the vocabulary of degrees. The library's earlier comparison of the inner motive forces to the "ruling love" (*The Mechanics of the Soul* §V) points in the right direction but lands on the wrong term. The match is not with the ruling love, which is single, but with the threefold order Swedenborg gives for the living person: will, understanding, and their issue in action through the body.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| The actor's body, "the spiritual and physical creative instrument" | The body through whose forces the inner degree acts (*DLW* §219) |
| Mind, will and feeling as one "triumvirate" | Will united to understanding: the first degree, living conatus (*DLW* §219) |
| The action on stage | Living motion, the outmost degree, in which the inner exerts its power (*DLW* §218) |
| "Inner and outer life … flowing naturally" | Correspondence of the interiors of the mind with the interiors and exteriors of the body (*DLW* §219) |

## 7. The Studio

### 7.1 The Memo of 1935

By 1935 the Disney studio had a problem that *The Crisis of Ensoulment* has described in full. The "rubber hose" figures of the 1920s moved because the music or the gag moved them. A feature film needed characters who seemed to move because they thought and felt. Walt Disney's memorandum to Don Graham of 23 December 1935 is the studio's statement of the solution, and it is a statement about causes:

> "Many men do not realize what really makes things move - why they move - what the force behind the movement is. I think a course along that line, accompanied by practical examples of analysis and planning, would be very good. In other words, in most instances, the driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character - or all three. Therefore, the mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them." (Disney, 23 December 1935)

The memo asks the classes to "show the men ways of visualizing action in their minds" before they draw. Walt insists that "there is a scientific approach to this business". He also says that "the first duty of the cartoon is not to picture or duplicate real action or things as they actually happen, but give a caricature of life and action". The cartoon is not to copy the outer event. It is to draw the outer event from its inner cause, and to draw it so that the cause shows.

Measured against §3, the memo has every element of the law. It names three things, and their order is Swedenborg's:
- an inner cause in three aspects, "the mood, the personality, the attitude";
- a "force behind the movement";
- the movement itself.

The direction is one way: "the mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them." It names no source, and it does not need to. A studio head asking for a course does not cite its authorities. What the memo shows is that by 1935 the order of the law was available to Walt as plain sense. It was also the order American readers had been taught for two generations under the name of the Law of Correspondence: "every posture of the body … is but an outward expression of an inner emotion or condition of the mind" (Northrop 1896, 10).

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| "The driving force behind the action" | Living force: conatus made active (*DLW* §218) |
| "The mood, the personality, the attitude of the character" | Will united to understanding, the living conatus (*DLW* §219) |
| The drawn action | The outmost degree, in which conatus "exerts its power" (*DLW* §218) |
| "The mind is the pilot" | "The body is only an obedient instrument" (*DP* §103) |

### 7.2 Graham's Old Philosophy, Adapted

Don Graham taught at the studio from 1932. The classes expanded in 1934 as *Snow White* went into production. The transcripts of his Action Analysis classes, typed by the studio and preserved in the collection of Hans Perk, are the only record of what was actually said in the room. The class of 22 February 1937, "Introduction to Study of Action: Primary Actions", sets out the principle:

> "every primary action must be the result of a thought process either subconscious or conscious … it is the emotion and mood that come first, the action comes second. The action is a secondary thing - it is a result of the thought or feeling … it is the thought process that determines primary actions, not the action that determines the thought." (Graham, 22 February 1937, 6, 11)

His example is a man who "walks angrily across the room. Before he can walk angrily, he must be angry, - he has to have that thought, that feeling." Three figures, "all tall and thin", walking down the same street can express "three entirely different things" because "inner feelings have a lot to do with the way they act" (Graham, 22 February 1937, 11).

At the end of that class the transcript records something no previous study in the library has used. "Suggestions offered by various members of the class on helpful reading along these lines" included:
- *Theatre Workshop*, "a quarterly - the first two issues, and more especially the first, with articles on acting that offered practical guidance to animators who had read them";
- "AN ACTOR PREPARES, by Stanislawski";
- "FIRST SIX LESSONS IN ACTING, by Boleslavski".

(Graham, 22 February 1937, 20.) The animators had found for themselves the books that taught what Graham was teaching, and the books they named were the books of the tradition traced in §6. *An Actor Prepares* had been published in English the year before. Its chapter on the inner motive forces states the triad and its issue through the "creative instrument". Its "inner and outer" is the relation the class was studying. Boleslavsky had been a member of the Art Theatre's First Studio, where he directed in 1913 (Whyman 2008); his *Acting: The First Six Lessons* (1933) teaches the System in English.

The class of 21 June 1937, "Animation of Forces vs. Animation of Forms", gives the method its graphic form. "In drawing lines representing a leg, a symbol for the force or thrust, is first drawn. The artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force." Of Bill Tytla, Graham says: "The actual form is something which eventually is necessary but which is really a by-product of animation. The vital element is force." And in the same class Graham says what kind of thing he is teaching. "There is a world of difference in the mental approach - there is an entirely new philosophy of animation wrapped up in this concept of animating forces." When an animator objects, "I think if you put it just as forces, it sounds too much like philosophy", Graham answers: "It *is* a philosophy." He then places it:

> "This is merely a new way of looking at old material … Review and study the different philosophies, the different big steps forward, the progress made in science, history, art. Rarely will one find these forward steps entirely new - merely old material given a new twist. … It is putting a new twist to an old subject - a new philosophy of approach … not really new, but adapted to Studio uses." (Graham, 21 June 1937, 2–5)

Graham names no philosopher. A full search of the 183 pages of his published classes, from January 1936 to October 1938, finds no Swedenborg, no Delsarte and no named source for the method. But he tells his students three things that the chain explains:
- the method is a philosophy;
- the philosophy is old;
- the studio has adapted it.

The old philosophy is the law the expression tradition had carried for half a century, and the adaptation is from the actor's body to the animator's line. In "a symbol for the force or thrust, is first drawn", conatus is drawn as force before it is drawn as form.

The studio received it as craft, and said so. A week later Tytla addressed the same class: "This proves to me that the ultimate thing is the mood or sensation you get into a piece of work." And: "We can't be mystical about our work … we cannot turn out the lights and have confessions. This is a business" (Tytla, in Graham, 28 June 1937, 11–12). The law entered the studio as a working method. It was neither a doctrine nor a key of fixed meanings, and its inner logic was intact.

### 7.3 The Direction of the Law

The links of the chain differ on one point that the documents allow to be checked exactly: the direction in which the law runs. Swedenborg's law runs one way. The body "is only an obedient instrument" (*DP* §103), and the natural springs from the spiritual "precisely like an effect from its effecting cause" (*HH* §89). Delsarte's law runs both ways. Its second clause makes the body's great functions answer to spiritual acts, and Waille draws the consequence that bodily manifestations act directly on the psycho-spiritual sphere (§4.1). Dalcroze cited it with "and vice versa". The American layer runs one way. Stebbins writes "The material form should correspond to the inner form" and "it is being that forms the matter"; Northrop writes that "every posture of the body … is but an outward expression of an inner emotion" (§§5.1–5.3). Stanislavsky's System, in the sentence Eric Larson would later quote, runs both ways: "In every physical action … there is always something psychological and vice versa." "There is no inner experience without external physical expression" (Larson, in Stanchfield 2009).

The 1937 studio chose, and the transcript shows it choosing. In the class of 22 February an animator, Hilberman, proposed the other direction: "Or else the thought processes can be started by an action." The answer recorded on the next page does not accept it. "Thought processes are started by the things around the actor - the things he experiences are the things that generate the thoughts within him", and the class returns to the rule: "it is the thought process that determines primary actions, not the action that determines the thought" (Graham, 22 February 1937, 10–11). The perceived situation may start a thought, but the action does not. In the 21 June class Graham puts the same choice as the whole of the method: what matters is "whether an artist starts with the conception of force … and whether he sees the force as the by-product of the drawing, or vice versa. It is the approach that is important" (Graham, 21 June 1937, 5).

On direction, then, the studio's law is Swedenborg's and the American expositors'. It is not Delsarte's own, and not Stanislavsky's two-way "and vice versa". This is the second application of the feature test (§2.4). Delsarte's system carried no feature found only in Swedenborg (§4.3). The studio's method carries one feature that Delsarte's law lacks and the Swedenborgian layer supplied: a strictly one-way order, from inner cause through force to outer form. The thesis claims this as a structural observation and no more. No document says the studio chose the direction because of Swedenborg. But the direction the studio chose is the direction of the frame through which the law had been read, and not of the system that frame was laid over.

### 7.4 Larson and Stanchfield

The law stayed in the studio's teaching, and in the later generation the tradition behind it was named. Eric Larson joined the studio in 1933, the year after Graham's classes began, became one of the Nine Old Men, and with Walt Stanchfield set up the studio's training programme in the 1970s (Stanchfield 2009, Hahn's foreword). Stanchfield quotes Larson's "lecture notes on entertainment" at length in one of his own lectures:

> "As we begin the 'ruffing out' of our scene, we become concerned with the believability of the character and the action we've planned and we give some thought to the observation of Constantin Stanislavsky. 'In every physical action,' he wrote, 'there is always something psychological and vice versa. There is no inner experience without external physical expression.' In other words, what is our character thinking to make it act, behave, and move as it does? … Leonardo da Vinci wrote: 'Build a figure in such a way that its pose tells what is in the soul of it. A gesture is a movement not of a body but of a soul.' Walt (Disney) reminded us of this when he spoke of the driving force behind the action: 'In other words, in most instances, the driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character — or all three.' Let's think of ourselves as pantomimists because animation is really a pantomime art." (Larson, in Stanchfield 2009)

Here an animator of the studio's founding generation sets Stanislavsky's law of inner and outer beside Walt's memo, as one teaching. His source for both quotations is most likely Sonia Moore's *The Stanislavski System*. There Leonardo's sentence is followed by "And Feodor Chaliapin, the great singer and actor, said, 'A gesture is a movement not of a body but of a soul'" (Moore 1976, 16). The second sentence of the "Leonardo" quotation is therefore Chaliapin's. Larson's own notes of the 1980s also cite Stanislavsky and the memo's "driving force" side by side (Larson 1983).

Stanchfield, who taught the studio's artists from the 1970s until 2000, named the rest of the line. When the mime Ken Martin posed and lectured for his evening class, Stanchfield reported the lecture under the title "Mime" (Stanchfield 2009, lecture 103):
- "Francis Delsarte (The Delsarte Method) said the body is divided into three basic centers. First the intellect, (the head) … The torso is the emotional center … The physical center is in the hips and pelvis area and from there down."
- "Delsarte said the shoulders and the elbows are the thermometer of the will."
- Delsarte's three "great orders of movement": oppositions, parallelisms and successions. Of the third: "In animation this would be called overlapping action."

He quotes *An Actor Prepares* directly: "never allow yourself externally to portray anything that you have not inwardly experienced". He quotes Robert Henri's *The Art Spirit*: "The look of the eye has its correspondence in every part of the body." His lecture titles carry the law as the class received it: "The Inner Force", "Gestural Symbolism", "Draw Verbs Not Nouns" (Stanchfield 2009).

Two details show the form in which the tradition arrived. The "thermometer of the will" in shoulders and elbows is the mime's version. Delsarte's own text puts the will in the thumb and the life in the shoulder (*Delsarte System of Oratory* 1893). And "overlapping action", one of the studio's own principles of animation, is here identified by the studio's teacher as Delsarte's "successions". The expression tradition did not merely parallel the studio's method. By Stanchfield's time the studio recognized it as part of its own lineage, and taught it by name.

### 7.5 The Art Books

The continuity from Larson and Stanchfield to the present is documented:
- In 1995 the studio reissued Walt's memo to its artists, with Stanchfield's foreword: "It is management's wish that the 'Disney Tradition' be revitalized and maintained … we need but to refer to the memo Walt wrote to Don Graham in 1935" (Stanchfield 1995).
- Stanchfield's students included Ron Clements and John Musker, the directors of *Moana* (Stanchfield 2009, Hahn's foreword).

The art books of the studio's recent features record how its artists now explain their images, and *The Diffracted Light* (§§IV–V) has read them in full. What matters here is that the art books state the law in its own terms: function, force, inner state, and the rejection of the arbitrary.

**Function to inner quality.** Peter Del Vecho on *Frozen II*: "As we looked into the science of water, we learned about the concept that water has memory. Water dissolves other substances and absorbs part of everything that passes through it, so in a sense it really holds our history" (Julius 2019). This is correspondence reached through the use of the thing, as in *HH* §96. It arrives where Swedenborg placed it: "waters" signify "knowledges" (*Arcana Coelestia* §28).

**Force known by its effects.** The wind spirit Gale: "It's not anthropomorphic; it's nature, it's wind. We only 'see' it by how it causes other things to react, like hair or fabric rippling" (Julius 2019). This is Graham's "the artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force".

**The inner state governs the outer event.** Dale Mayeda: "What is the emotional tone of this sequence, and how can effects support that? It's not just about what would naturally happen in the real world" (Julius 2019). This is the memo's "caricature of life and action" rather than duplication.

**Not arbitrary.** Jared Bush on *Encanto*: the powers given to the family "didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality". The house is "a literal representation of the family and their emotional connections. If the family is happy, the house is healthy, but if the family is going through struggles, the house too will crack" (Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022). This is Madeley's definition in a screenwriter's words: "no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure, but one founded alike on the inward and outward nature of the things".

**Truth to the thing.** Ian Gooding on *Moana*: "we didn't want to mess with the physics". Andy Harkness: the design rules "evolved from what we noticed in nature … we stylized what was already there" (Julius and Malone 2016). This is the observation that Waille names as Delsarte's second source, and that Graham's classes practised frame by frame.

None of these artists names Swedenborg, Delsarte, Stanislavsky or Graham. They do not need to. The law has become the way the studio's artists think about an image: what does the thing do, what inner state does it carry, and how does that state show in the form? It is taught as craft, without a key of fixed meanings. That is why, as *The Diffracted Light* shows, it can meet a Polynesian or a Colombian perception of living nature and recognize it as kin.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Water that "holds our history" | Knowledges held in memory ("waters" signify "knowledges", *Arcana Coelestia* §28) |
| The wind seen only in what it moves | A force known by its effects: conatus seen in motion (*DLW* §218) |
| The house that cracks when the family does | The outer form of an inner bond: everything in the natural "that has its existence from" the spiritual "is called a correspondent" (*HH* §90) |
| Gifts "dictated by emotions and personality" | Use given form by the inner state (*HH* §96) |

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 What the Hermetic Line Preserved

The hermetic and Kabbalistic line, as it reached Delsarte, preserved more than the framework's account of the third tier might lead one to expect, and it is worth stating exactly what. It preserved the principle: "that which is below is like that which is above" stood in MacKaye's notebook as the Emerald Tablet's first sentence (Waille 2012, 311). It preserved a body map: thought in the head, understanding in the heart, power in the limbs. This is the Zohar's arrangement, and Delsarte's (§4.3). Through Aquinas it preserved the union of soul and body as form and matter. And in Delsarte's hands it preserved the capacity to construct. He did not merely invoke the principle. He applied it to every zone of the body and to every inflection of the voice, and built an art on the result that his pupils could learn and teach. By the framework's own quality marker, construction (*Transmission* §§VII.G, X.A), Delsarte's system is at the upper edge of its tier.

What the line did not preserve, or did not hold firmly, is equally clear from the comparison:
- **The discreteness of the degrees.** Delsarte's three "interpenetrate" into a ninefold accord, where Swedenborg's three "do not act by what is continuous, but by what is discrete" (*DLW* §219).
- **The one-way direction.** Delsarte's law runs both ways.
- **A single name for the relation.** In the hermetic milieus correspondences were "always in the plural" (Waille 2012, n. 30).

The inheritance was a key in a different organization. It was rich enough to build an art on, but not set in the order and direction in which Swedenborg states the law. That is the condition the framework describes for Kabbalah: "a detailed key (though organized differently)", "systematic within its own framework", overlapping the Swedenborgian system "significantly but not completely" (*Transmission* §§VII.D, X.B).

### 8.2 What the Swedenborgian Layer Supplied

The American layer supplied what the inheritance lacked. The layer can be itemized, because Stebbins's pages and Northrop's show exactly what was laid over the system.

1. **The order.** Will united to understanding, issuing through the forces of the body into action, quoted from *DLW* §219 (Stebbins 1901, 109–110).
2. **The definition.** Correspondence as "no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure, but one founded alike on the inward and outward nature of the things" (Madeley 1883, in Stebbins 1901, 139).
3. **The name.** "The Law of Correspondence" in the singular, taught as Swedenborg's discovery (Northrop 1896, 10; Stebbins 1901, 389–390; Waille 2012, n. 30).
4. **The direction.** "The material form should correspond to the inner form"; "it is being that forms the matter"; every posture "but an outward expression of an inner emotion" (Stebbins 1901, 110, 139; Northrop 1896, 10).
5. **The trinity read through Swedenborg.** "Love, wisdom and power" (Stebbins 1901, 109–111).

The downstream links received exactly this:
- **The order.** The triad of *An Actor Prepares* and the three aspects of the memo, "the mood, the personality, the attitude", issuing as force and then form.
- **The direction.** Graham's class kept it against an explicit challenge (§7.3).
- **The definition.** The art books restate the rejection of the arbitrary.
- **The trinity and the name.** These did not travel. Volkonsky dropped them at the Russian link, and the studio never heard them.

This is the filter at work. The frame passed on its content and lost its name. What it let through was not Delsarte's Thomist theology or his Kabbalistic body map. It was the Swedenborgian part: the order, the direction, and the ground in what the thing is and does.

The author's reading can therefore be stated with the precision the record allows. The expositors recognized in Delsarte's law the structure Swedenborg had stated. The structure had come down the hermetic road, and the work of carrying it was already done. They did not rewrite Delsarte. They read him through Swedenborg and taught him that way. Their reading set the inheritance in the restored order, and whoever learned the law from them learned it in that order. The comparison the author draws with the Nag Hammadi texts holds with one difference. Those texts were written in correspondence and can be read in their own terms once the key is in hand. Delsarte's system approached correspondence and was read into its proper order by the key.

**Correspondential key** (the layer and the filter):

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Delsarte's system as received | A Tier 3 key: the principle and a detailed body map, in a different organization (*Transmission* §VII.D) |
| Stebbins's pages on Swedenborg, Madeley and Monroe | The restored key laid over the inheritance (*Transmission* §VIII.D) |
| The name dropped at Volkonsky | The form that varies, while the state (the order of the law) is kept |
| The studio's craft | The law working in a culture without its doctrine (*Transmission* §IX.G) |

### 8.3 Implications for the Framework Hypothesis

The chain bears on the framework's account of the science of correspondences in three ways.

**It documents a route of the fourth tier.** *The Transmission of the Ancient Word* describes the cultural re-emergence of the science after Swedenborg through two channels at the Disney studio, source material and personnel (§IX.D). It also states as an implication that "the Science of Correspondences can be taught again" (§XI.F). The expression tradition is that implication realized in history. Here the science was taught again, as an art of the body and the line, to actors in Boston and Moscow and to animators in Los Angeles, without its theological vocabulary. The framework's claim that "the system doesn't require conscious knowledge to function. It requires only that someone with access creates the structure" (§IX.G) takes a sharper form. Someone with access, Stebbins, set the structure in order; a teaching carried it; and those who learned it built with it.

**It shows the restored key working on a fragment.** The framework treats the third tier as fragmentary because it cannot construct with the restored key's precision (§VII.G). Delsarte's case refines this. His inheritance could construct, but in a different organization, and the layer reorganized it by reading it through Swedenborg. It added no new detail; it set the existing detail in order and direction. If the framework is right that Swedenborg's key is the key the older traditions once had (§VIII.E), this is what one would expect to see when the key meets a surviving fragment. The fragment is not replaced. It is read, and in being read it is set in order. The author's reading of the Nag Hammadi texts is the same operation applied to texts written in correspondence; the Delsarte case applies it to a system that approached it.

**It confirms constant state, variable form at the level of method.** The law the studio teaches is constant across five languages of expression:
- Swedenborg's degrees;
- Delsarte's functions;
- Stanislavsky's motive forces;
- Graham's forces;
- the art books' "science of water".

The names change at every link; the order does not. The framework predicts that the state of a correspondence is constant while its form varies with the receiver (*The Diffracted Light* §5.2). Here that holds for the method of correspondence itself, carried through cultures that named it differently or not at all.

These implications are interpretation, not documentation. The chain is documented; that it is the restored key at work is the framework's reading of it, and the thesis holds the two apart (§2.2).

### 8.4 The Evidentiary Gap in the Disney Case

*The Parsimony Problem* counts the pedagogical strand as "documented fact" in its structure: the materials exist and say what they say. It leaves open whether the structure "represents conscious transmission or parallel invention" (§3.2), and its coincidence hypothesis must posit "that Graham independently invented a methodology that happens to mirror a theological framework nearly two centuries old, without any transmission pathway". *A Cultural History* (§4.4) prefers "organic selection", Graham formalizing "intuitions that happened to align", because no document shows him reading Swedenborg. The library supplied a route: the Theosophical milieu of Chouinard and of Los Angeles, Kandinsky's "inner meaning", New Thought (*The Mechanics of the Soul* §4.2; *The Invisible Influx* §5.2). No document places that milieu in Graham's classroom.

This thesis replaces the atmospheric route with a documented one, and changes the terms of the parsimony argument accordingly. The coincidence hypothesis no longer has to explain a parallel invention without a pathway, because the pathway is in the record:
- the class names *An Actor Prepares* as reading "along these lines" (Graham, 22 February 1937, 20);
- *An Actor Prepares* is the work of a director who learned Delsarte from Volkonsky and recommended Volkonsky's book (Autant-Mathieu 2012, 374);
- Volkonsky built his book on Stebbins (Volkonsky 1913, 203–204);
- Stebbins taught Delsarte's law through *Divine Love and Wisdom* and a New Church definition of correspondence (Stebbins 1901, 109–110, 139).

Graham's own words fit the route: "not really new, but adapted to Studio uses". The pedagogical strand moves from isomorphism to lineage.

Two consequences follow for the cumulative case. First, the pedagogical strand is now partly dependent on a documented source line. Its independence from the other four strands is unchanged, since none of them runs through the expression tradition. But it no longer stands on structure alone. Second, the milieu strand (*Parsimony* §3.5) gains a documented member that is not Theosophical: Hovey's teaching of the Monroe line in Los Angeles from 1915. This is opportunity, not transmission, and *Parsimony* already counts the milieu strand as opportunity.

What the chain does not do is close the question *Parsimony* treats as the hardest: whether the studio's narrative choices of 1934–1937, such as the cloud castle, reflect conscious Swedenborgian design. The method can travel without the name, and this thesis shows that it did. It says nothing either way about whether anyone at the studio knew the name.

### 8.5 Limitations and Lacunae

The limits of the evidence are specific, and they bound the claim.

**Delsarte's own sources.** The thesis accepts Waille's conclusion, from the manuscripts, that Delsarte was not Swedenborg's disciple, and its feature test supports him. Northrop's "tradition" that Delsarte studied Swedenborg is reported and not relied on. The manuscripts are mostly unpublished. Neither Waille's thesis (Lyon 3, 2009) nor Porte's anthology of Delsarte's writings (1992) was read. The French original of the "Love, Wisdom, Power" fragment Stebbins prints was not seen, so it cannot be said whether those three words are Delsarte's or her rendering.

**The Russian link.** Volkonsky's one sentence naming Swedenborg is read here as a summary of Stebbins's chapter, because it follows that chapter's order and he marks her book as used. He does not cite the page. That he passed on the content of *DLW* §219 or of Madeley's definition is not shown: he set the philosophy aside. The descent of Stanislavsky's triad of inner forces from the Delsartean trinity is a strong inference (§6.3), reported by Leabhart and Autant-Mathieu, not a statement of Stanislavsky's. Stanislavsky does not name Delsarte in *An Actor Prepares*. Taylor and Whyman's article on Delsarte, Volkonsky and Michael Chekhov in the *Mime Journal* (2004–5) was not read.

**The studio link.** The class of 22 February 1937 shows that animators read *An Actor Prepares* and Boleslavsky. It does not show that Graham himself took the method from them. Graham's own source for "a new philosophy of approach … not really new" is unnamed in all 183 published pages of his classes. His unpublished transcripts in the Walt Disney Archives, his *Composing Pictures* (1970) and his years at Chouinard were not reached. The first two issues of *Theatre Workshop* (1936–37), which the class singled out, are not online. It is possible that Graham reached the law by another route, or partly by his own observation, as Delsarte did. The thesis claims the documented route into the classroom, not the only one.

**The direction.** That the studio's one-way law matches the Swedenborgian layer rather than Delsarte (§7.3) is a structural observation. No document says the studio chose the direction because of its source. Swedenborg is not the only thinker who places the inner before the outer.

**The art books.** That the art-book artists stand in the line of Graham's classes is the corpus's reading of a documented continuity: the 1995 reissue of the memo, and Stanchfield's classes and students. None of the artists names the line.

**What cannot be concluded.** The thesis does not show that anyone at the studio in 1935–1937 knew the law had been taught as Swedenborg's. It does not bear on the narrative strand of the Disney case. And it does not decide between transmission and convergence for the law itself. The framework holds that correspondence is real and in part observable (*Transmission* §II.E), so the same order could be found by observation as well as received by teaching. What the thesis establishes is narrower and firmer: the teaching by which the order reached the studio is documented, and at its American source it was taught as Swedenborg's.

## 9. Conclusion: The Law Without the Name

The law of correspondence for the human person was stated in full by Swedenborg in 1763. Will united to understanding is the living conatus. The forces of the body are that conatus made active. Action is the outmost degree, in which the inner exerts its power. The three are joined "not by what is continuous, but by what is discrete", that is, "by correspondences" (*DLW* §§218–219). The inflow is "into the functions and uses of the bodily members" (*HH* §96), and "the body is only an obedient instrument" (*DP* §103).

Delsarte, a century later, formulated his own law of correspondence for the art of expression, "To each spiritual function responds a function of the body". He did so from a Catholic and hermetic inheritance and from his own observation, not from Swedenborg. His system was a detailed key in a different organization: it was systematic, it constructed, its map of the body was the Zohar's, and its law ran both ways.

His American expositors laid Swedenborg over him. Monroe called him "Swedenborg geometrized". Stebbins, in the standard textbook, read his trinity as Swedenborg's "love, wisdom and power", quoted *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219 for the order of will, forces and action, and defined correspondence in the words of a New Church minister as "no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure". The popular handbooks taught "the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg", and taught it one way, from the inner condition to the outer posture. The hermetic plural became the American singular, the Law of Correspondence.

From that textbook the law went to Russia. Volkonsky built his manual on Stebbins, saw her Swedenborg chapter and summarized it in one sentence, "from Pythagoras to Swedenborg", and set the philosophy aside to keep "the manifestation of the inner man in his outward form". Stanislavsky attended his courses, recommended his book, and wrote, in *An Actor Prepares*, of three inner motive forces, mind, will and feeling, moving the "spiritual and physical creative instrument".

In February 1937 the animators in Don Graham's class at the Disney studio recommended *An Actor Prepares* to one another as "helpful reading along these lines". In that class they were taught that "the emotion and mood … come first, the action comes second", and they refused the reverse. That June Graham told them that what they were learning was "a philosophy … not really new, but adapted to Studio uses". The studio's later teachers named the line, Stanislavsky beside Walt's memo, Delsarte's centres and successions in the life class. Its artists still reason by it: water "holds our history" because of what water does, and the family's gifts "didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality".

The library had called the gap in the Disney case an absence of documents. It was an absence of the right documents. The route of the studio's method lies through the expression tradition, and every link of it can be read: the textbook, the manual, the archives of the Moscow Art Theatre, the class transcript, the lectures, the art books. At its American source the teaching that travelled this route was taught as Swedenborg's doctrine of correspondence applied to the body. The name was dropped in Russia; the order was kept.

The thesis does not claim that Delsarte drew on Swedenborg, that Graham read him, or that any artist of the studio knew the source. It claims what the record shows, and what the author of the framework proposed. The method the studio teaches is the core of the science of correspondences that Swedenborg restored. It reached the studio through a teaching that had been read through his doctrine and set in his order. It is taught as craft, not as a key of meanings, and that is why it travels. In the framework's terms, a fragment preserved on the hermetic road was read by the restored key, set in order, and passed on as an art. Those who received it built with it, as the framework says the system does when it is working.

Swedenborg wrote that the knowledge of correspondences had been "so completely lost that no one knows what correspondence is" (*HH* §87). A century and a half later it was being taught in every drawing class of the studio that made *Snow White*, by men who did not know its name. The law came without the name, the order without the doctrine, the key without the keeper.
