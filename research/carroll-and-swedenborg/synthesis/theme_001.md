# Theme 001 — Carroll: the record and the reading

Sources: store/source_001–012. Library context: context.md.

## 1. What the record shows

**No direct line from Swedenborg to Carroll is documented.** Carroll never names Swedenborg in any of his writings the session could search: Lancelyn Green's edition of the diaries (source_002), Collingwood's *Life and Letters* (source_003), the *Selected Letters* (source_003), the *Picture Book* including "Eternal Punishment" (source_004), the *Alice* books and both volumes of *Sylvie and Bruno* (source_012). His library, as far as it is recorded, held three books in which Swedenborg appears through other authors — Emerson's *Representative Men*, Mahan's hostile *Modern Mysteries*, Garth Wilkinson's *The Human Body* — and none of Swedenborg's own (source_001). The record of the library is about half complete by Lovett's own account, so absence there proves nothing; but presence is all that can be claimed.

**Two indirect lines are documented, and both are strong.**

1. *George MacDonald.* Friend from 1859; the first audience of the *Alice* manuscript was MacDonald's family; MacDonald persuaded Carroll to publish (sources 002, 003). MacDonald knew Swedenborg's writings, stated the correspondence idea as his own ("Such symbols are the true bodies of the true ideas … nature was called into being … to furnish forms for truths") and named Swedenborg "the European master of the order" (source_005). Scholarship reads Swedenborgian thought-structures in his fantasies (sources 006, 007).
2. *F. D. Maurice.* Carroll corresponded with him in February 1863, helped at his Vere Street church, lunched with him and went on to the MacDonalds the same day (source_002). Gardiner's thesis, built on Maurice's letters to Carroll, shows Maurice's eschatology — eternity as "a state which is unrelated to time or space", the rejection of endless punishment — running through the *Alice* books and *Sylvie and Bruno* (source_008). Carroll's own essay turns on the same word, αἰών (source_004).

**Carroll's own named sources for the spirit-world are psychical research and Theosophy**: a charter member of the Society for Psychical Research, owner of its *Journal* and *Proceedings*, and of Sinnett's *Esoteric Buddhism*, which he names in the 1893 preface as the model for the "immaterial essence" that migrates (sources 001, 009).

## 2. What the reading shows

The ideas the library reads in Carroll are in Swedenborg's text, verbatim:
- time replaced by state (*HH* §§162–163) — the Hatter's stopped time; Maurice's "fetters of Time";
- space as state, the way "lengthened and shortened in accordance with the desire" (*HH* §§191–192, 195) — walking away to arrive, running to stay in place, Gardiner's "space … depending on the spiritual state of the individual";
- the self-chosen hell (*HH* §547) — Carroll's case (C), "continuous suffering as punishment for continuous sin";
- the two kinds of visionary state (*HH* §§440–441) — Carroll's states (b) and (c).

The convergence is exact in content and documented in route — but the route runs through Maurice, MacDonald, the SPR and Sinnett, not through Swedenborg's books.

## 3. The argument the library can make

The library's own framework holds that correspondence is ontological law, open to more than one route of access (*The Transmission of the Ancient Word*, Thesis), and that content grounded in reality "does not drift because each generation's lived experience re-confirms the structure" (*The River and the Vessel*). On that framework Carroll is not a transmitter of Swedenborg's system. He is a convergent witness: a logician and clergyman who, through the Broad Church eschatology of his friends and his own reasoning about eternity, arrived at the same account of time, space and judgement that Swedenborg gives, and built his fantasies on it. That is a stronger and more honest use of Carroll than the transmission claim, because the evidence for it is in Carroll's own words and those of his friends.

What the library may no longer say: that Carroll owned or read Swedenborg's works; that he was a "systematic Swedenborgian thinker"; that he encoded Swedenborg's doctrine; that the *Sylvie and Bruno* preface transliterates Swedenborg; that the raven riddle conceals a theological answer by design. None of these has a record; several are contradicted by the record.

What stays: the correspondential readings of the texts, where they rest on Carroll's words and Swedenborg's, offered as readings; and the documented circle (MacDonald, Maurice) that carried the same content.
