# What the Apparatus Cannot See: Translation, Selection, and the Concretising Drift Beneath the Christological Text
## A Text-Critical Audit Across Hebrew, Greek, and Samaritan Witnesses Through the Correspondential Framework

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis reports a 126-item audit of the principal christological, interpolation, resurrection, infancy, title, prophecy, canon, and authorship loci across *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, *Novum Testamentum Graece* 27 and 28, the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and the graded Marcionite reconstruction. Three questions were kept separate: what the source-language text says, whether its wording is secure, and whether the passage is compositionally integral. The manuscript record does not support progressive scribal manufacture of Jesus's divinity. Within the audited sample, the Comma Johanneum is the unambiguous late intrusion of direct trinitarian identity-language, 1 Timothy 3:16 preserves a clear change from “who” to “God,” ordinary additions are predominantly harmonising or explanatory, and difficult sayings remain substantially intact. The positive finding is more consequential: the secure christological texts carry their weight through relation, reception, manifestation, image, indwelling, non-seizure, and shared predicates rather than through the later binary of human promotion or numerical divine identity. Translation, citation form, source selection, compositional expansion, and later reading explain how this grammar could be concretised without producing New Testament variants. Primary-text queries were source-gated from framework documents; the correspondential framework was applied only after the readings and witnesses were fixed. Because that framework has independent predictive support, its precise fit to the recovered source–expression grammar is corroborative evidence, not an optional theological gloss. Ordinary textual mechanisms describe how the forms changed; the framework explains both what the secure forms preserve and why later reception repeatedly converted relation into identity. The apparatus does not reveal a man becoming God. It preserves a receiver through whom the divine source becomes fully expressed.

> **Keywords.** textual criticism, Nestle-Aland, *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, Comma Johanneum, 1 Timothy 3:16, Western Non-Interpolations, *nomina sacra*, *qere perpetuum*, Psalm 110, Granville Sharp, μονογενής, correspondences, literalization, Marcionite witness, christology

## 1. Introduction: The Argument Is Not Where the Argument Is Looking

The public argument about what the New Testament says concerning Jesus is conducted almost entirely in the register of textual criticism. One side holds that the high christological material is late — that a Galilean teacher was progressively elevated by scribes who added, altered, and interpolated until a man became God. The other holds that the manuscripts are secure and the high material original, and that the sceptic's case is an ideological construction laid over an unbroken transmission. Both sides appeal to the apparatus. Both sides can quote it accurately. Neither side is looking where the evidence is.

This thesis reports a 126-item audit conducted across *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, *Novum Testamentum Graece* in its twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth editions, the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and Dieter Roth's graded reconstruction of the Marcionite gospel. The sample comprises the principal passages used in christological argument, commonly alleged additions, resurrection and infancy loci, titles, prophecy citations, and representative questions of canon and authorship. Three questions were kept separate throughout: what does the text say in its source language; is that wording secure in the surviving witnesses; and is the passage integral to its literary context. The third question was added under pressure from passages that are unanimous in the manuscripts but visibly interrupt, duplicate, or extend their compositions.

The first central finding is text-critical. **The audited manuscript record does not preserve a progressive manufacture of Jesus's divinity.** The passages usually called “high” are predominantly early and stable. The audit found one unambiguous late intrusion of direct trinitarian identity-language, the Comma Johanneum, and one exceptionally clear doctrinal alteration, ΟΣ to ΘΣ at 1 Timothy 3:16. It also found ordinary harmonisation, glossing, liturgical completion, and changes in more than one theological direction. The difficult sayings remain: the Son does not know the hour, Jesus asks why he is called good, Mark says he could not act, and the imminence sayings survive. Within the audited sample, the claim of systematic scribal elevation is therefore not merely unproved; it is contradicted by the distribution of the evidence.

The second central finding lies in the secure text. **Its christological grammar is relational before it is identificatory.** John 1 distinguishes the Word from *ho theos* while predicating *theos* of the Word; John 1:18 defines the unique relation by saying that the one in the Father's bosom makes the unseen God known; John 10:30 uses the neuter *hen* for unity, the same form applied to the disciples at John 17:21–22; and John 10:34 answers the charge of self-deification by appealing to those “to whom the word of God came.” Philippians 2 speaks in the sequence of prior form, non-seizure, emptying, service, and exaltation. Colossians calls the Son the image of the invisible God. Revelation places shared divine titles in the mouths of the one on the throne and of Jesus without narrating rivalry between them. These are not remnants of a “low” position awaiting promotion. They are a coherent source–expression grammar: distinction without separation, reception without possession, and the invisible source present through its visible expression.

The third finding concerns what the apparatus cannot register. Translation, citation form, source selection, compositional expansion, and later reading can all alter what a secure passage is taken to say without producing a New Testament variant. The eight examined Hebrew–Greek source divergences are directionally mixed; they cannot support a universal law of concretisation. A recurrent subset nevertheless moves from an idiomatic, spatial, or relational form toward a more concrete or sensory one. More important than that subset is the larger text-critical pattern: later reception repeatedly treats relation as identity, function as title, and expression as possession. The Comma and 1 Timothy 3:16 display the operation in the manuscript record; the later identity-metaphysical reading of secure passages displays it without changing their words.

The framework enters only after the primary findings have been established under source gating, but it does not enter without evidential standing. [*The Epistemic Architecture of Post-Materialist Inquiry*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Epistemic%20Architecture%20of%20Post-Materialist%20Inquiry_%20A%20Methodological%20Thesis%20on%20Hypothesis-Testing%20with%20the%20Swedenborgian%20Framework.md) treats correspondences, influx, and constant-state/variable-form as hypotheses tested across independent empirical domains. Their predictive successes give the framework rational weight before it reaches this corpus. Source gating prevents circularity: framework documents did not determine readings, witnesses, or grammatical observations. Once those observations were fixed, however, model comparison became appropriate. Ordinary scribal, translational, and editorial habits identify the proximate mechanisms of change. They do not explain why the secure christological text itself is organized by source, reception, indwelling, manifestation, image, and non-claiming, or why later reception repeatedly has difficulty preserving that architecture. The correspondential model explains both levels with one account.

The argument of this thesis is therefore stronger than compatibility. The text-critical evidence independently recovers the structure the framework predicts: the Father as source, the Son as the full human expression of divine love in the form of wisdom, and Jesus as the receiver whose transparency allows the source to be present in ultimates without turning the receiver into a second source. The later binary — either a man promoted into deity or a divine entity numerically identical with God — is not the grammar of the earliest secure material. It is what appears when reception is converted into possession and relation into identity.

The thesis proceeds as follows. **§2** establishes the three-question method, source gating, the prior epistemic standing of the framework, and the distinction between receiving and claiming. **§3** defines the narrow class of demonstrable textual corruption without generalising it into a programme. **§4** separates textual security from compositional integrity. **§§5–7** examine translation, selection, and later reading as mechanisms invisible to the New Testament apparatus. **§8** uses the Samaritan Pentateuch as a control specimen for ordinary scribal expansion and harmonisation. **§9** turns to the central positive result: shared titles, the reception argument of John 10:34, the secure source–expression grammar, and its distribution across the corpus. **§10** brings the text-critical and framework findings together while retaining only limitations that bear on those findings. **§11** states the resulting reconstruction.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading: Expression, Not Allegory

Correspondence, as this library uses the term, is not a literary device. It is a claim about how reality is layered. Emanuel Swedenborg's position, argued across *Arcana Coelestia*, *Heaven and Hell* and *Divine Love and Wisdom*, is that every natural object, act and relation exists because an interior reality flows into it and takes form there. The natural thing does not *stand for* the spiritual thing; it *is* the spiritual thing at the outermost degree, in the only shape that degree can hold. The relation is therefore one of expression, not of resemblance, and it runs in one direction only: from inside outward. A correspondence can be read upward, from the form to what expresses itself in it, but the form is never the cause of what it carries.

Three distinctions follow, and they are load-bearing for everything in this thesis.

**Correspondence is not allegory.** An allegory is a story constructed to carry a meaning assigned to it by its author; the assignment is arbitrary and could have been made otherwise. A correspondence is not assigned. It is a function-relation that obtains whether or not anyone notices it. Water cleanses; truth cleanses; the correspondence of water to truth is not a decision but an observation about what each does at its own degree.

**Correspondence is not symbolism.** A symbol is a token agreed upon within a culture and legible only inside it. Correspondences, on this account, are stable across cultures precisely because they are grounded in function rather than convention, which is why the same relations surface in Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Israelite and Iranian material without contact sufficient to explain borrowing.

**Correspondence is not metaphor.** A metaphor asserts a likeness between two things that remain two. A correspondence asserts that one reality is being perceived at two degrees. When Paul writes ἡ πέτρα ἦν ὁ Χριστός — "the rock *was* Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4) — he does not write that the rock resembled Christ or prefigured him. He uses the copula. That is the grammar of correspondence rather than the grammar of simile, and it appears in the undisputed Pauline corpus in a textually secure verse.

A worked example, from a passage that will recur in §9:

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural form | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Rock in the wilderness (1 Cor 10:4) | The stable ground of truth on which a people in transit subsists |
| Water from the rock | Truth received as sustenance in a state of privation |
| The rock "following" them | The same source present at every stage of a passage through desolation |
| Light (John 1:4–9) | Divine wisdom in its illuminating aspect, received according to the state of the receiver |
| The bosom of the Father (John 1:18, εἰς τὸν κόλπον) | Interior conjunction — the state of being within what one expresses, not adjacent to it |

The relevance of this to a textual-critical audit is precise. If a corpus is correspondentially composed, then its statements about a central figure should tend to be built as **through-language**: expression, image, manifestation, indwelling, exegesis of the invisible. They should point *through* the figure to what is expressed in him. Material received after this architecture has been lost should tend to convert relation into identity, reception into possession, and function into title-predication. This is not a prediction invented from the passages under examination. The framework entered the audit with independent evidential standing established through prior tests of influx, constant-state/variable-form, functional differentiation, and correspondential pattern-fit in other domains. The present audit therefore has two tasks: to establish the textual facts without framework contamination, and then to ask whether those independently established facts add to or subtract from the framework's cumulative support. It cannot by itself validate the entire ontology, but neither is it methodologically entitled to treat a precise predicted fit as weightless.

### 2.2 The Three Questions

Every passage in the audit was put to three questions, and the discipline of the method consists in refusing to let them collapse into one another. In ordinary discussion they routinely do, with the result that a claim established at one level is defended with evidence drawn from another.

**Question one: what does the text say?** This means the wording, the grammar and the lexical range in the source script — Hebrew, Greek, or Samaritan Hebrew — before any inherited translation choice is permitted to settle the matter. It is asked first because the other two cannot be asked coherently until it is answered. A great deal of the argument about the New Testament is conducted over English renderings whose decisions were made centuries ago and are no longer visible as decisions: *only-begotten* for μονογενής, *hell* for three unrelated Greek words, *virgin* for עַלְמָה.

**Question two: is the text secure?** This means the manuscript support, the significant variants, the earliest attestation, and whether the reading is a known or suspected interpolation. It is the question the popular argument treats as decisive, and the audit's most consistent result is that it decides far less than either side supposes.

**Question three: is the passage integral to its context?** This question was added during the audit, under pressure from the evidence, and it changed the shape of the results. A passage can stand in every surviving manuscript without variation and still be a later insertion, detectable only by the seam it leaves: an argument broken and then resumed, vocabulary the author uses nowhere else, an editorial voice in the first person plural, a conclusion given twice. **Manuscript unanimity proves that a passage was in place before our oldest surviving copy. It does not prove that it stood in the author's text**, and the gap between composition and earliest extant witness is, for every New Testament book, at least a century. Questions two and three are therefore evidentially independent, and a passage can be secure on one and secondary on the other. §4 presents four such cases.

The separation matters in both directions. When 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1 is defended on the ground that no manuscript omits it, an answer to question two is being offered to an objection raised at question three, and it is not responsive. When Matthew 28:19 is alleged to be an interpolation on the ground that Eusebius cites a shorter form, an argument proper to question three is being dressed as an answer to question two, for which it has no manuscript support whatever. The rule has to run both ways or it is not a rule.

### 2.3 The Primary Editions and the Source-Gating Protocol

The audit was conducted against four bodies of primary text, and the first methodological point to register is that two of them are made on opposite principles.

*Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* is a **diplomatic** edition. It reproduces a single manuscript — the Leningrad Codex, B19A, sigel **L**, copied in the eleventh century — and confines every judgement to the apparatus beneath. What is printed is what one scribe wrote. *Novum Testamentum Graece* is an **eclectic** edition. What is printed is a reconstruction that exists in no manuscript at all, assembled reading by reading from the whole tradition. The Hebrew edition prints an artefact; the Greek edition prints an inference. This asymmetry is invisible in translation and is quietly responsible for a good deal of confusion, because a reader who compares "the Hebrew" with "the Greek" is comparing one eleventh-century manuscript with a twentieth-century committee's best judgement about a first-century original.

The BHS apparatus operates on its own conventions, and two of them bear directly on the findings below. The **Ketiv/Qere** system preserves the received consonants (כְּתִיב, *what is written*) while placing on them the vowels of the word the Masoretes intended to be read (קְרֵי, *what is read*), with the replacement consonants in the margin; the form standing in the text is therefore a hybrid never meant to be pronounced as it appears. The *qere perpetuum* does this silently, without marginal note, for high-frequency words. Its most consequential instance here is that **יהוה is pointed with the vowels of אֲדֹנָי**. That pointing records an inherited avoidance convention; it postdates the Septuagint and did not cause its use of κύριος. §5.3 traces the historically ordered chain.

On the Greek side, the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth editions of Nestle-Aland were consulted together, with explicit confirmation demanded at each locus of whether they agree. They almost always do: the text of NA27 was identical to NA26 and stood unchanged for over thirty years, and NA28 altered it in **exactly thirty-four places, all in the Catholic Epistles**, under the Coherence-Based Genealogical Method developed for the *Editio Critica Maior*. CBGM abandons the family-tree model of classical stemmatics — which fails because scribes contaminated, copying from several exemplars at once — in favour of comparing whole texts across thousands of variant units, measuring how consistently the direction of development runs between any two witnesses, and reconstructing an *Ausgangstext* that is then entered into the database as a further witness. NA28 also abolished the square brackets in the Catholic Epistles, replacing them with split guiding lines where the editors judge two readings equally possible: an admission that the older brackets were recording editorial doubt rather than evidence.

For the Marcionite material the audit used Dieter T. Roth's *The Text of Marcion's Gospel*, and adopted his standard of certainty as the standard for the audit as a whole. Roth works from three hostile witnesses. Tertullian, *Adversus Marcionem* IV, attests roughly ninety per cent of the 486 verses known to have stood in the gospel and is the **sole** witness for about two-thirds of them; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 42, supplies numbered *scholia*; the *Dialogue of Adamantius* is third and least trusted, its author working from anti-Marcionite secondary literature and its Latin transmission conforming readings toward the Vulgate. Roth's discipline lies in analysing the citation habits themselves — Tertullian quoted loosely, from memory, drifted into Matthean and Markan wording, and paraphrased for rhetorical effect, so a Latin synonym is not evidence of a Greek variant — and above all in his typographic grading, introduced to correct the speculative confidence of Harnack's reconstruction:

| Marking | Evidential status |
|---|---|
| **Bold** | Secure: citation habit and attestation agree, or multiple independent sources |
| ***Bold italic*** | Very likely: citation habit clear but attestation lacking, or the reverse |
| Regular | Probable: some evidence, not definitive |
| *Italic* | Possible: attested, carrying no strong historical confidence |
| *(Italic in parentheses)* | The verse element is attested; the wording is unrecoverable |
| {Curly brackets} | Words attested, word order uncertain |
| … | Unattested |

The value of this scale is that it separates three claims that ordinary discussion fuses: *this text existed*, *this wording existed*, and *we cannot know*. Every judgement in §§3–9 below is intended to be readable against it.

Finally, a protocol point that is procedural but was decisive for the reliability of the results. The primary editions were consulted in an environment that also holds this library's own framework theses, and the two were **never enabled together for a primary-text question**. The single weakest result produced early in the audit — a New Testament reading cited to a framework reconstruction rather than to a critical edition — was caused by leaving everything switched on at once. Primary-text questions receive primary sources only. This is what keeps `[P]` evidence and `[T]` reconstruction from being cited in the same register, and it is the reason the findings below can be checked independently of anything this library argues.

### 2.4 Receiving and Claiming: The Distinction Identity-Christology Cannot Represent

The popular argument runs on a shared and unexamined premise: that a christological statement is an **identity statement** — a claim about which entity a name denotes. Once that premise is in place the whole dispute becomes an argument about how many entities there are and which one Jesus is, and both sides can quote the same secure text indefinitely without moving. It is worth noticing that the premise is not in the sources. It is a conceptual apparatus that arrives later, with the fourth-century controversies, and is then read back into first-century documents that were not built to answer it. The Greek New Testament contains no word for a threefold godhead: τριάς, or any abstract noun for such a thing, occurs nowhere in it.

The position from which this audit reads is a third thing, and it is not a compromise between the two poles. It holds that the figure in question is a soul of the celestial degree who **descended**; that what is expressed through him is the Father, without remainder, because he is transparent to what flows through him; and that the relation between them is **correspondential** — the Father as divine love, the Son as that love expressed in the form of divine wisdom, so that mention of the Son points *through* him to what he expresses. This is not a second entity standing alongside the first, and it is not a man later promoted. The library's treatments of discrete degrees, the operation of the divine through a vessel, and Jesus's human reception set out the underlying architecture: see [*Asking the Skin to Be the Heart*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/Asking%20the%20Skin%20to%20Be%20the%20Heart_%20The%20Category%20Error%20of%20Physical%20Miracle%20Theology%20and%20the%20Architecture%20of%20Discrete%20Degrees.md), [*The Bridge That Became the Path*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Bridge%20That%20Became%20the%20Path_%20The%20Lord's%20Operation%20Through%20the%20Vessel,%20and%20the%20Error%20of%20Making%20One%20Soul%20the%20Exception.md), and [*The Human Who Showed the Way*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Human%20Who%20Showed%20the%20Way_%20The%20Soul%20Whose%20Heart%20Belonged%20to%20the%20Lord.md).

The operative distinction is between **receiving** and **claiming**. A vessel perfectly transparent to what fills it can truthfully say "the Father in me" and would never say "I am the Father" — not because the second is forbidden but because continuous receiving knows itself as receiving. Only the self, taking what flows through it to be its own possession, produces the second sentence. Identity-statement christology has no way to represent this at all: it must resolve the case into either two Gods or one man, because its only available operator is the equals sign.

This matters methodologically, and not only theologically, for a reason that becomes visible in the data. **If the sources are composed in through-language and are read by an apparatus that can only register identity-predication, then every genuinely correspondential statement will be mis-scored** — assigned to one pole or the other, and cited by whichever side finds it convenient. John 10:30 is the clearest instance. The Greek reads ἐγὼ καὶ ὁ πατὴρ ἕν ἐσμεν: **ἕν is neuter**, "one thing," not the masculine εἷς, "one person," and the same neuter is applied to the disciples at John 17:21–22. The verse is textually secure in P45, P66, P75, ℵ, B, A, W and the Byzantine tradition; nothing in the apparatus is in dispute. Read as an identity statement it is either a proof of deity or an embarrassment to be explained away. Read as it stands, it says something the identity frame has no slot for.

The framework claim is therefore used in the way this library uses Swedenborg generally — as a hypothesis evaluated by predictive accuracy and explanatory scope rather than as a doctrine protected from evidence. The neutral position is accuracy, not equal distance between a framework with accumulated successful predictions and an untested alternative. Its prediction here is specific: the secure source material should carry christological weight through expression, reception, indwelling, and functional relation; literalized reception should increasingly make those relations land as identities, possessed attributes, and stacked titles. Section 9 tests that prediction against readings established under source gating. Section 10 then compares explanatory levels: textual criticism identifies the wording and the material mechanisms of alteration, while the correspondential framework identifies the architecture preserved in the wording and the semantic direction of its later misreading.

## 3. What the Manuscripts Convict: The Narrow Class of Real Corruption

The audit began with the expectation that question two — is the text secure — would carry most of the argumentative load. It does not. Of the passages on which the standard christological case is built, the overwhelming majority are transmitted without any variant that changes what is being claimed. John 1:1 is uniform in P66, P75, ℵ, B, C, W and the Byzantine tradition. John 8:58, John 10:30, John 10:33, John 20:28 and John 5:18 are stable across every early papyrus and uncial. Philippians 2:6–11, Colossians 1:15–20, Colossians 2:9, Hebrews 1:6, Hebrews 1:10 and Revelation 3:14 have no variant of substance among them: an article present or absent, a word transposed, a tense form. Whatever is argued from the christological hymns is argued from a stable text, and it follows that the dispute at those passages is lexical and grammatical rather than text-critical and should be conducted as such.

What this section presents is therefore the residue: the small, well-defined class of places where the manuscripts genuinely convict the printed tradition. The class is smaller than the sceptical account requires, and much of its content is ordinary. But it is not empty. Its clearest doctrinally relevant cases either add an identity formula, as at the Comma Johanneum, or convert a statement of manifestation into an explicit identity statement, as at 1 Timothy 3:16.

### 3.1 The Comma Johanneum: A Demonstrable Late Intrusion

The words at 1 John 5:7–8 — "there are three that bear witness in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit, and these three are one" — are the only item in the audited set that states a three-party identity formula this directly. They have no ancient Greek attestation.

The reading stands in the running Greek text of **exactly four manuscripts**, and all four are late and all four are under Latin influence:

| Manuscript | Date | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 629, Codex Ottobonianus | 14th c. | Greek–Latin bilingual; the Greek column reworked to match the facing Latin |
| 61, Codex Montfortianus | c. 1520 | The manuscript associated with Erasmus's inclusion of the reading; **omits the closing clause "and these three are one"** |
| 918 | 16th c. | Escorial, paper |
| 2318 | 18th c. | Bucharest, written under printed Vulgate influence |

Every papyrus, every uncial and every early minuscule omits it. Where the words appear elsewhere in Greek — in 221, 88, 429, 636 — they are marginal additions entered by hands centuries later than the manuscripts they annotate. The versional evidence is equally decisive: the Comma is absent from the Coptic in both dialects and from the Syriac tradition. Even the Latin evidence is split, the reading being absent from the critical editions of the Vulgate and appearing only in late Vulgate manuscripts, the Clementine edition, the *Speculum*, and the late Latin fathers Priscillian and Fulgentius.

Two features of this deserve to be held together. The first is that the earliest Greek witness with the words *in its text* is fourteenth-century, and it is a bilingual harmonised to its own Latin column: the Greek reading entered under Latin influence, after the doctrine it supports had already been settled for centuries. The second is Montfortianus. The manuscript associated with Erasmus's inclusion of the reading **drops the final clause** and does not reproduce the Latin form whole. The most explicit trinitarian identity statement in the received text thus has a late Latin transmission history rather than an ancient Greek one.

### 3.2 1 Timothy 3:16: One Crossbar

The verse reads either "**who** was manifested in flesh" or "**God** was manifested in flesh." The difference is between a relative clause with no stated antecedent and a flat assertion of incarnation, and it is produced by a single stroke of ink.

**ὅς, "who", is the earlier reading**, supported by the *original hands* of the great uncials — Sinaiticus (ℵ*, 4th c.), Alexandrinus (A*, 5th c.), Ephraemi Rescriptus (C*, 5th c.) — together with Augiensis (F) and Boernerianus (G), both 9th c., minuscules 33 and 365, and the fourth-century fathers Didymus and Epiphanius.

**θεός, "God", is the later reading**, and in the oldest manuscripts it appears *only as correction*, and late: the Ephraemi corrector (C²) and the Claromontanus corrector (D²), both working in the ninth century, four hundred years after the pages they were altering were first written. Otherwise it is Ψ (9th–10th c.), 088, 1739, 1881, and the Byzantine majority from the ninth century onward. A third reading, the neuter ὅ, stands in the original hand of Claromontanus (D*, 6th c.) and behind the Latin *quod*, agreeing grammatically with "mystery" and almost certainly a scribal tidying.

The mechanism is visible on the page. In uncial script "who" is **ΟΣ**; "God", written as a *nomen sacrum*, is **ΘΣ** with an overline. Omicron is a plain circle and theta is the same circle with one horizontal stroke through it. The entire difference between "who was manifested" and "God was manifested" is a short bar inside a circle plus a line above two letters — an amount of ink that faded exemplars, show-through from the other side of a leaf, or a scribe's settled conviction about what the verse ought to say could each supply.

This is the clearest case in the audit of a doctrinal reading entering the text through a graphically easy alteration reinforced by scribal expectation. The direction is directly visible in the principal corrected witnesses: Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and Ephraemi read "who" in their first hands, while later intervention supplies "God." No cited correction in this transmission history runs from "God" to "who."

### 3.3 The Fifteen Absent Verses: The Banality of Textual Growth

Fifteen verses stand in the Textus Receptus and are absent from the modern critical text. They are the most frequently cited evidence that the Bible has been tampered with, deployed with equal confidence by those who think modern editors removed them and by those who think medieval scribes added them. Sorted by mechanism, they turn out to be neither a conspiracy nor an outrage but a catalogue of ordinary scribal habit.

| Mechanism | Verses |
|---|---|
| Harmonisation to a synoptic parallel | Matthew 17:21 (from Mark 9:29), Matthew 18:11 (from Luke 19:10), Matthew 23:14 (from Mark 12:40 / Luke 20:47), Mark 11:26 (from Matthew 6:15), Mark 15:28 (from Luke 22:37), Luke 17:36 (from Matthew 24:40), Luke 23:17 (from Mark 15:6) |
| Internal duplication within the same passage | Mark 9:44 and 9:46, both repeating the Isaiah 66:24 line already standing at 9:48 |
| Explanatory gloss absorbed from the margin | John 5:4 (the legend of the troubled pool), Acts 15:34, 24:7, 28:29 — all Western, all smoothing a narrative seam |
| Liturgical formula | Mark 7:16 ("let him who has ears"), Romans 16:24 (a benediction duplicating v. 20) |

The omission is supported by ℵ and B throughout, and by P45 at Matthew 17:21, P75 at Luke 17:36 and 23:17, P66 and P75 at John 5:4, and **P46 at Romans 16:24**. The additions are Byzantine and Western. Two further passages belong to the same class and are likewise absent from the printed text without even a bracket: **Acts 8:37**, the Ethiopian's confession of faith, missing from P45, P74, ℵ, A, B and C; and **Matthew 6:13b**, the doxology of the Lord's Prayer, missing from ℵ, B, D, Z, and from Origen and Cyprian. Both are still recited liturgically by millions of people every week.

**Not one of the fifteen changes a christological claim.** This is the datum, and it cuts in both directions. Any argument that the tradition was systematically doctored has to explain why the overwhelming majority of its additions are of this harmless kind — a copyist matching a parallel, completing a familiar formula, or absorbing a reader's explanatory note. Any argument that the tradition was pristine has to explain why fifteen verses accumulated at all. What the class actually demonstrates is that **the ordinary machinery of textual growth is banal**, and that the burden on anyone claiming a doctrinally motivated corruption is to show that a given case is not an instance of this machinery.

The two most famous disputed passages belong here too, and are the two about which the editions are most confident. Mark 16:9–20, the Longer Ending, is omitted by ℵ and B, by the Sinaitic Syriac and the early Coptic, and by Eusebius and Jerome; it is present in A, C, W, D, the Byzantine tradition and Irenaeus in the second century. John 7:53–8:11, the woman taken in adultery, is omitted by P66, P75, ℵ, B, C, L and W, and appears first in D in the fifth century. Both are printed in double brackets: the editors regard them as certainly not original and print them for their history in the church. The decisive datum on the second is not its absence from early witnesses but its **mobility**. It appears after John 7:52, after 7:36, after 21:25, and in some manuscripts in Luke. A passage that floats is a passage that was inserted, because a passage that belongs to a text does not travel through it.

### 3.4 The Western Non-Interpolations and the Reversal at P75

Eight short passages in Luke 22–24 are present in the great Alexandrian manuscripts and absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin. Westcott and Hort, in 1881, named them **Western Non-Interpolations** and judged the shorter text original — the one place in their edition where they departed from their own Alexandrian preference.

| Passage | Words at issue | Omitted by | NA27/28 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luke 22:19b–20 | "which is given for you … poured out for you" | D, Old Latin a d ff2 i l | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:3 | "of the Lord Jesus" | D, Old Latin a b d e ff2 l r1 | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:6 | "He is not here, but has risen" | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:12 | the whole verse: Peter runs to the tomb | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:36 | "and said to them, Peace be with you" | D, Old Latin a b d e f l r1 | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:40 | the whole verse: he showed them his hands and feet | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic and Curetonian Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:51 | "and was carried up into heaven" | ℵ* (original hand), D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:52 | "worshipped him" | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |

**The omissions form a coherent Western cluster.** Codex Bezae and Old Latin witnesses carry the shorter readings throughout the set; the Sinaitic and Curetonian Syriac support several individual omissions, and the original hand of Sinaiticus omits the ascension clause at Luke 24:51. This clustering is why Westcott and Hort treated the readings as a class rather than as eight unrelated accidents. Several items make the resurrection or ascension more tangible — the body given, the empty-tomb sequence, hands and feet displayed, the ascent, and worship — although not every item has the same semantic force.

**P75 changes the chronology of the problem.** The third-century papyrus contains all eight passages and predates Bezae by two to three centuries. Its evidence was central to the reversal from NA26 onward, which restored all eight and bracketed four. P75 is not the sole support for the longer readings; its importance is that it demonstrates that the complete cluster already existed substantially earlier than the principal Western witnesses that omit it.

Two observations follow, and they should be kept apart. First, editors reversed a judgement inherited from Westcott and Hort when materially earlier evidence altered the external balance; this is evidence against treating the critical text as a fixed ideological product. Second, the reversal **does not settle the direction of change**. Addition and omission remain internally explicable at several loci, and the surviving witnesses preserve both a very early longer form and a coherent shorter tradition. At Luke 24:51 the words are absent from the *original hand* of Sinaiticus and supplied by a corrector, a local intervention in the longer direction; that local fact does not determine the history of all eight passages.

The physicalising material in Luke 24 is discussed further in §4 and §9, and its relation to the reconstructed Proto-Lukan tradition is treated at length in [*The Forensic Stratigraphy of the Resurrection*](../04_Early_Christian_History/The%20Forensic%20Stratigraphy%20of%20the%20Resurrection_%20A%20Critical%20Reconstruction%20of%20the%20Tomb%20Narrative%20in%20Proto-Luke%20and%20the%20Marcionite%20Witness.md).

### 3.5 The Corrections That Run Both Ways

A tidy theory of unidirectional scribal expansion is available, attractive, and refuted by the manuscripts. Three sets of data refuse it.

**The Lukan variants divide early witnesses in different ways.** At Luke 22:43–44 the *original hand* of Sinaiticus contains the angel and the sweat like blood, and a corrector removed them; the passage is also absent from P69 and P75 and from Vaticanus and the Sinaitic Syriac, and present in D, L and the Byzantine tradition, and cited by Justin in the second century. At Luke 23:34a, however, the original hand of Sinaiticus stands on the **inclusion** side with A, C, L and the Byzantine tradition; P75, Vaticanus, D* and W omit "Father, forgive them." The second locus therefore does not furnish an opposite Sinaiticus correction. What the two passages show is an early tradition divided differently at adjacent sayings, not two contrary interventions demonstrable within one codex.

**The variant at John 1:18 runs against the expected drift.** The reading μονογενὴς θεός is carried by the *earliest* witnesses — P66 (c. 200), P75, ℵ*, B, C*, L — while ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός is carried by the later ones: A, C³, K, W, Θ, Ψ, f¹, f¹³, the Byzantine majority, and the Latin and Syriac versions. Between them stands a third reading, the articular ὁ μονογενὴς θεός in the Sinaiticus corrector and in 33. That is two distinct movements at one verse: a scribe first making the anarthrous reading more definite, and the tradition subsequently swinging to υἱός instead. Any account claiming a single uniform elevation of christology by copyists has to deal with a first-rank variant where the elevation is in the oldest witnesses and the deflation is later.

**The modern editors moved a reading upward.** Of the thirty-four changes NA28 introduced, the most consequential is at **Jude 5**, where NA27's [ὁ] κύριος becomes **Ἰησοῦς** — so that the current critical edition prints the statement that *Jesus* saved a people out of Egypt. The editors moved the text toward the more christologically loaded reading, on the evidence and against the expectation. Anyone arguing that modern critical scholarship systematically deflates christology has to account for this, and the account cannot be that the editors were following a programme, because the programme would have forbidden it.

Two further data belong here because they are so often mis-filed. **1 Corinthians 14:34–35 is not omitted by any known Greek manuscript**; D, F and G place it after verse 40. That displacement is consistent with a marginal block entering the text at different locations and therefore supplies a serious compositional argument for secondary origin. It does not prove that history by itself; relocation or deliberate rearrangement must remain possible. **Matthew 28:19, by contrast, has no extant Greek manuscript variant at the formula**: the claim that it is an interpolation rests on Eusebius citing a shorter form in some pre-Nicene works. A patristic citation habit is evidence about citation and reception, not manuscript evidence for a shorter Greek text.

## 4. What the Manuscripts Cannot Settle: Compositional Seams in Unanimous Text

The third question — is the passage integral to its context — was not in the original design of this audit. It was forced by a recurring result: passages that every surviving manuscript transmits without variation can nonetheless contain literary features that raise a question about compositional history. Because textual attestation and compositional integrity are evidentially independent, this class has to be handled on its own terms. **Manuscript unanimity establishes an early textual state; it does not by itself establish original composition.**

The logic is straightforward once stated. Our earliest continuous witnesses to any New Testament book postdate its composition by a century or more. Manuscript unanimity therefore establishes only that a passage was in place before the earliest surviving copy — a period that comfortably accommodates the entire formative history of the tradition. Everything that happened in that window is invisible to the apparatus by construction.

**John 21.** No manuscript ends the gospel at 20:31. But 20:30–31 is a formal conclusion — "many other signs … these are written so that you may believe" — and it is followed by a further chapter that ends with a second conclusion at 21:25. Within that chapter the narrative voice changes twice: at 21:24 it becomes an editorial first person plural, "we know that his testimony is true," and at 21:25 an authorial first person singular, "I suppose." The doubled conclusion and changed voice are strong evidence of editorial extension, but the apparatus cannot display the earlier state if the extension predates every surviving witness.

**2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1.** Without the passage, 6:13 runs directly into 7:2 — "widen your hearts also … make room for us" — continuing an argument the intervening unit interrupts. The vocabulary is significant: the paragraph contains several words Paul uses nowhere else, among them **Βελιάρ** and **παντοκράτωρ**, and its sharp dualism of light against darkness, righteousness against lawlessness, believer against unbeliever has close analogues in the Qumran material. No manuscript omits it. The evidence therefore raises a compositional question but cannot turn that question into a directly observed interpolation.

**Matthew 27:52–53.** At the death, tombs open and "many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised"; then, in the same sentence, they enter the holy city **"after his resurrection."** The events are separated by three days inside a single clause, and the raised bodies are left in an unnarrated interval in between. The verses are uniform in the surviving witnesses. Their temporal discontinuity and the direct narrative movement available from the death to the centurion's confession raise a compositional question, but those features alone do not demonstrate insertion.

**Romans 16:25–27.** Here the manuscripts do speak, and what they show is that the doxology **will not stay put**. It stands at the end of chapter 16 in ℵ, B, C, D and 1739; at the end of chapter 14 in the Byzantine tradition; in *both* places in A and 33; and, in the earliest witness of all, **at the end of chapter 15 in P46**. Three positions and a doubling establish unstable attachment. Mobility makes a secondary relocation or attachment highly probable, although it does not by itself identify the first position or the exact compositional history.

**Ephesians 1:1.** The words ἐν Ἐφέσῳ are absent from P46, from the original hand of Sinaiticus, and from the original hand of Vaticanus, and Basil states that the oldest copies known to him lacked them. Without the place-name the letter has no addressee, which for a Pauline letter is an anomaly of the first order. This is a case where questions two and three converge rather than conflict, and §10.4 returns to it, because the same letter is attested in an independent line of transmission under a different name altogether.

**Mark 1:1.** The words υἱοῦ θεοῦ are bracketed in the critical text. They are omitted by the original hand of Sinaiticus, by Θ, by 28, and repeatedly by Origen; they are present in B, D, W, L and the Byzantine tradition. The mechanism is mechanical rather than doctrinal: in a manuscript using *nomina sacra*, the sequence runs ΙΥ ΧΥ ΥΥ ΘΥ — four contracted words in the genitive, every one of them ending in upsilon. Loss by eye-skip in copying, or addition to fill out a title, are equally easy. This one is genuinely undecided and is recorded as undecided.

The class as a whole delivers a necessary methodological result. **Any editorial work completed before the common ancestor of the surviving witnesses is invisible to manuscript comparison and can be approached only through composition-internal argument or an independent textual tradition.** Composition-internal analysis is less determinate than direct variant evidence and cannot quantify how much shaping occurred in that inaccessible interval. It nevertheless addresses a real evidential blind spot: manuscript unanimity and compositional integrity are different findings.

## 5. The First Silent Mechanism: Translation

The audit was designed to test the security of the Greek New Testament. It found instead that a large fraction of the load-bearing christological argument does not depend on the Greek New Testament's security at all, because the argument was already carried by the time the New Testament authors picked up their sources. They read the Hebrew Bible in Greek, and the Greek says things the Hebrew does not say.

Three properties of this mechanism should be fixed before the evidence is presented, because each of them is routinely mishandled in the popular argument.

**It is not corruption.** The Septuagint translators were not producing a defective text. They were doing what translators do: rendering figurative, elliptical and idiomatic Hebrew into a language with different resources, and choosing, at each ambiguity, the sense that was available to them. Several of the divergences are demonstrably attempts to make sense of consonantal sequences that are genuinely difficult, and at least one — Psalm 22:17 — is now known to reflect a real Hebrew variant rather than a translator's guess.

**It has no christological motive, because it predates the dispute by centuries.** The Pentateuch was rendered into Greek in Alexandria in the third century BCE and the rest over the following two centuries, by Jewish translators for Jewish readers, in a setting where the questions that would later be argued about Jesus did not exist. Whatever one concludes about the direction of the divergences, no one can allege intent. This is the single most important property of the mechanism, and it is what makes the result in §5.2 worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as motivated reasoning at the source.

**It leaves no trace in the New Testament apparatus.** A New Testament author quoting a Greek source form can produce a quotation that is textually secure in every New Testament manuscript even where that source differs from the Hebrew. The divergence is invisible unless the Hebrew and Greek editions are opened together. Eight source-level divergences are examined below. Psalm 40:7 at Hebrews 10:5 is a separate case: the New Testament reading is stable, but it differs from the Rahlfs–Hanhart Septuagint as well as from the Hebrew.

### 5.1 Eight Source Divergences and One Citation Variant

**Psalm 40:7 at Hebrews 10:5.** The Hebrew reads אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי, *ears you have dug for me*. Rahlfs–Hanhart Psalm 39:7 reads ὠτία δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι, *ears you prepared for me*. Hebrews 10:5 instead reads σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι, *a body you prepared for me*, and 10:10 develops that wording into the offering of the **body** of Jesus Christ. The decisive divergence is therefore not Hebrew against the printed Septuagint but the critical Septuagint against the citation in Hebrews. Because Rahlfs–Hanhart prints no apparatus, this edition alone cannot establish whether σῶμα reflects an older Greek Psalm form, the epistle's own adaptation, or later inner-Greek transmission.

**Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23.** The Hebrew is הָעַלְמָה — *the young woman*, with the definite article, a term that does not itself assert virginity. Biblical Hebrew also has בְּתוּלָה, a term more directly associated with virginity, and Isaiah does not use it here. The Septuagint renders ἡ παρθένος, whose usual range makes virginity available and prominent, although the Greek word is not an exceptionless technical term for sexual status. Matthew quotes the Greek. The surrounding verses are as important as the noun: Isaiah 7:15–16 attaches a deadline to the sign — before the child knows to refuse evil and choose good, the land of the two kings Ahaz fears will be abandoned — locating the sign in the crisis addressed to Ahaz.

**Psalm 19:5 at Romans 10:18.** The Hebrew has קַוָּם, *their measuring line*, going out through all the earth — the heavens as a surveyor's cord, silently laying out order. The Septuagint has ὁ φθόγγος αὐτῶν, *their sound*, most likely reading the consonants as קוֹלָם. Paul quotes the Greek to establish that Israel has heard and therefore has no excuse. A measuring line cannot be heard. The argument requires the noise.

**Psalm 16:10 at Acts 2:27 and 13:35.** The Hebrew has שַׁחַת in parallel with שְׁאוֹל, most directly “the pit” or destruction; the Septuagint has διαφθορά, *decay* or corruption. Acts builds its resurrection argument on the Greek noun — David died and saw corruption, therefore the psalm speaks beyond David of one who did not. The Hebrew can sustain deliverance from death or destruction, but it does not state bodily decomposition with the lexical explicitness of διαφθορά. The specific incorruption argument in Acts therefore depends on the Greek form.

**Isaiah 61:1 at Luke 4:18.** The Hebrew reads פְּקַח־קוֹחַ לַאֲסוּרִים, *the opening of the prison to those who are bound*. The Septuagint reads τυφλοῖς ἀνάβλεψιν, *recovery of sight to the blind*, having taken the doubled root of opening as opening of eyes. Luke quotes the Greek, and the restoration of sight enters the programmatic statement of the ministry from a phrase that in Hebrew is about release from confinement.

**Genesis 3:15.** The Hebrew זֶרַע is a masculine singular noun with collective sense, and the pronouns that follow agree with it grammatically — הוּא … תְּשׁוּפֶנּוּ — without implying an individual. The Septuagint's σπέρμα is **neuter**, so the grammatically expected pronoun is αὐτό. The Greek has **αὐτός**, masculine. A pronoun that does not agree with its antecedent in gender has stopped tracking the noun and started tracking a person, and the effect is to produce a single male descendant where the Hebrew has a line of offspring. (The later Latin *ipsa*, *she*, has no support in the Hebrew, the Greek or the Samaritan.)

**Amos 9:12 at Acts 15:17.** The Hebrew reads לְמַעַן יִירְשׁוּ אֶת־שְׁאֵרִית אֱדוֹם, *that they may possess the remnant of Edom* — a statement of territorial restoration. The Septuagint reads ὅπως ἐκζητήσωσιν οἱ κατάλοιποι τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *that the remnant of men may seek*, having read אדם as *adam*, humanity, rather than *Edom*, and יירשו, *possess*, as ידרשו, *seek*. Two consonantal readings, and a promise of conquest becomes a promise of Gentile inclusion. **James cites the Greek at the Jerusalem council, as the scriptural warrant for admitting Gentiles without circumcision.** The decision that made the movement a world religion rests on a verse that in Hebrew says the opposite kind of thing.

**Daniel 9:25–26.** The Hebrew distinguishes two figures: at v. 25 a מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד, an *anointed leader*, and at v. 26 a מָשִׁיחַ standing alone. It also divides the period, by the accent placed immediately after שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעָה: the text as pointed reads **seven weeks, and then sixty-two weeks**, not sixty-nine. The Greek recensions differ from each other as well as from the Hebrew. Rahlfs–Hanhart's Old Greek has no χριστός in v. 25 and reads ἀποσταθήσεται χρῖσμα καὶ οὐκ ἔσται in v. 26, *an anointing shall be removed and will not be*. The Theodotionic text that displaced Old Greek in church usage has ἕως χριστοῦ ἡγουμένου in v. 25 but remains **impersonal** in v. 26: ἐξολεθρευθήσεται χρῖσμα, *an anointing shall be destroyed*, not *an anointed one shall be cut off*. Neither Greek recension preserves the later construction of one individual arriving after a combined sixty-nine weeks and then being cut off.

**Isaiah 9:5–6.** The Hebrew gives a four-part throne-name: פֶּלֶא יוֹעֵץ, אֵל גִּבּוֹר, אֲבִיעַד, שַׂר־שָׁלוֹם. The Septuagint **does not translate it at all**, substituting Μεγάλης βουλῆς ἄγγελος, *messenger of great counsel*. This too runs away from the concrete. Two further points belong to question one rather than question two: the verb וַיִּקְרָא is third masculine singular, so the naming is done *to* the child rather than by him about himself; and Israelite theophoric names routinely predicate something of God rather than of their bearer, as Elijah does not claim to be Yahweh.

### 5.2 A Recurrent but Mixed Direction

Set out together, the loci do not form a uniform series:

| Source form | Greek or citation form | Evidential movement |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew and Rahlfs–Hanhart: **ears** | Hebrews: a **body** prepared | citation-level change; physicalising direction |
| the young woman | the **virgin** | unmarked → marked |
| their measuring line | their **voice** | spatial → audible |
| the pit | **decay** | place → bodily process |
| the opening of the prison | **sight** to the blind | release → sensory restoration |
| seed, masculine collective | seed, neuter, with a **masculine** pronoun | grammatical mismatch; individualising potential, not proof |
| may possess Edom | may **seek** the Lord | territorial/ethnic → universal/religious |
| the anointed *leader*, then an anointed one | Old Greek: no χριστός at v. 25, then the anointing removed; Theodotion: ἕως χριστοῦ, then the anointing destroyed | personal → impersonal |
| Wonderful-Counsellor / Mighty-God / Father-of-Eternity / Prince-of-Peace | messenger of great counsel | one naming construction replaced by another |

Four of the eight source-level divergences plausibly move from something less specified, spatial or idiomatic toward something more specific, sensory or physical: the young woman, measuring line, pit, and prison opening. Psalm 40 shows the same kind of movement only at the later citation level; it cannot be attributed to the printed Septuagint. The remaining loci are not reducible to that axis. The masculine pronoun after neuter σπέρμα permits an individualising reading but does not by itself convert a collective into one male person; Amos 9 is a wider semantic recasting; Daniel 9 becomes less personal; and Isaiah 9 replaces one naming construction with another.

The selection problem remains substantial. These passages were chosen because they are load-bearing in later arguments, not sampled independently from the Septuagint. Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9:5–6, the clearest counter-patterns, are absent from the New Testament (§7). The defensible result is therefore limited: **concretisation recurs within the selected set, but neither a corpus-wide rate nor a uniform direction has been established.**

The local mechanism and the framework interpretation should not be posed as rival explanations. Translators and later citers can render difficult or figurative phrases into nearer, more graspable senses; that explains the production of each wording at the textual level. The correspondential claim concerns the larger semantic pattern in which states, relations, and functions are repeatedly received as concrete things. This selected and mixed set cannot establish the frequency or generality of that pattern across the Septuagint. It can identify instances of it, while the independently source-gated analysis of the secure christological grammar supplies the stronger framework test in §9.4.

What the audit can decide without either interpretation is stated in §5.3, and it is a structural fact rather than a directional theory.

### 5.3 The Psalm 110 Chain: Reverence, Not Editing

One finding in this audit outweighs the rest, and it is not a variant, not an interpolation, and not attributable to anyone. It is a chain of five steps, each of them innocent, whose combined effect shaped the vocabulary in which the entire New Testament speaks of Jesus.

**Step one.** Jewish avoidance of pronouncing the divine name predates both the Masoretic vowel points and the Septuagint. The Masoretes later encoded that inherited convention as a *qere perpetuum*: they left יהוה standing and placed on it the vowel points of אֲדֹנָי, producing the hybrid form יְהוָה — a spelling nobody said aloud, whose purpose is to instruct the reader to say a different word. This is reverence, exactly and only. No editorial intention is involved, and the same vocalisation mechanism operates elsewhere on words of no theological interest, such as הִוא, נַעֲרָה and יְרוּשָׁלַיִם.

**Step two.** The Greek translators reflect the older avoidance convention by representing the divine name as **κύριος** — the same word they use for אֲדֹנָי and for its ordinary human counterpart אֲדֹנִי, "my lord," a term of address for a superior. Hebrew distinguishes these lexically. Greek does not distinguish them at all.

**Step three.** At Psalm 110:1 the Hebrew reads נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי — an oracle of *Yahweh* to *my lord*, two lexically distinct words denoting two plainly distinct parties, the second of them a human superior. The Septuagint renders both with the same word: **εἶπεν ὁ κύριος τῷ κυρίῳ μου**. One Greek word, twice, for two different Hebrew words with two different referents.

**Step four.** Matthew 22:44 and Mark 12:36 quote the Greek, and quote it accurately, and build the argument on it. In Mark 12:36 the two occurrences of κύριος stand side by side in a single sentence with two different referents, and the puzzle Jesus poses — how can the Messiah be David's son if David calls him lord — is a puzzle that lives in that sentence.

**Step five.** κύριος is the standing New Testament address for Jesus, in every stratum of the corpus, from the Aramaic acclamation preserved at 1 Corinthians 16:22 to the latest of the Pastorals.

**The result: a distinction the Hebrew makes lexically is invisible in Greek, and the entire New Testament is in Greek.** This is not a scribal alteration, not a doctrinal insertion, and not a variant that any apparatus can display. It is a translation effect whose historical background is the older Jewish avoidance of pronouncing the divine name; the later Masoretic pointing documents that convention but did not originate the Greek rendering. The effect removes the ability of a Greek reader to see, at any given occurrence of κύριος, which Hebrew expression stands behind it. Whatever else is true, no participant in the later christological argument working only from the Greek had access to the lexical distinction preserved in Hebrew. The collapse begins in Jewish reverence, before there is a Christian dispute for it to serve.

## 6. The Second Silent Mechanism: Selection

The translation layer supplies a bilingual environment in which two authoritative forms of the same book say different things. The second mechanism is what the New Testament authors did with that environment. They moved between the Hebrew and the Greek — not at random, and not consistently, but **according to which form yielded the reading the argument required**. Like translation, this leaves no variants: the resulting quotation is transmitted faithfully by every manuscript. Unlike translation, it is a decision made by the New Testament author himself, and it is visible only when both source forms are placed beside the citation.

The clearest demonstration is a single author moving in both directions within two chapters. **At Matthew 1:23 the Hebrew of Isaiah 7:14 would have blocked the application, so the citation follows the Greek** — הָעַלְמָה would have given a young woman where the argument needs a virgin. **At Matthew 2:15 the Greek of Hosea 11:1 would have blocked the application, so the citation follows the Hebrew.** Hosea's Hebrew reads מִמִּצְרַיִם קָרָאתִי לִבְנִי, *out of Egypt I called my son*, and the son is unambiguously the nation, as Exodus 4:22 states outright; it is a statement about the past, not a prediction. The Septuagint pluralised it to τὰ τέκνα αὐτοῦ, *his children*, which removes the singular the argument needs. Matthew bypasses the Greek and renders the Hebrew to recover τὸν υἱόν μου. The same hand, twenty verses apart, going to whichever text supplies a singular individual.

**John does the same at 19:37.** The Hebrew of Zechariah 12:10 is secure and reads דָּקָרוּ, *they pierced*. The Septuagint has ἀνθ' ὧν κατωρχήσαντο — *because they danced mockingly* — a rendering produced by reading the consonants as רקדו, a straightforward confusion of ד and ר, which in most Hebrew scripts differ by the length of one stroke. The Greek is unusable for a crucifixion citation. John renders the Hebrew, ἐξεκέντησαν, and makes one further change: the Hebrew has אֵלַי, *they shall look upon **me***, and John writes εἰς ὅν, *upon **him** whom they pierced*.

Beyond the choice of source form, the same operation appears as choice *within* a text, and four instances are worth setting out because they are the cases where a citation most visibly shapes the narrative it supports.

**Micah 5:1–2 at Matthew 2:6, reversed.** The Hebrew calls Bethlehem צָעִיר, *little*, among the clans of Judah; the Septuagint intensifies to ὀλιγοστός, *very small*. Matthew writes **οὐδαμῶς ἐλαχίστη — "by no means least."** The citation states the opposite of both source forms. Two further operations are stacked on it: אַלְפֵי, *clans*, is read as though pointed אַלּוּפֵי, *chieftains*, giving ἡγεμόσιν, *rulers*; and the closing clause, "who will shepherd my people Israel," is not from Micah at all but spliced in from 2 Samuel 5:2, where it is said of David.

**Jeremiah 31:15 at Matthew 2:18.** Rachel weeps in **Ramah**, a town in Benjamin, north of Jerusalem; the massacre is at Bethlehem, in Judah, to the south. The two places are not the same place and the verse is not about either event. The link runs through Genesis 35:19, which locates Rachel's tomb near Bethlehem — that is, through an association with the *figure* of Rachel, not through anything the cited verse says.

**Matthew 2:23 cites a text that does not exist.** "He shall be called a Nazarene" corresponds to no sentence in any book of the Hebrew Bible, in any known Greek form, or in the Samaritan text. Matthew's citation formula is itself unusual: **διὰ τῶν προφητῶν**, plural and unnamed, where his other formulae name a prophet. The plausible candidates are consonantal: נֵצֶר, *branch*, at Isaiah 11:1, or נְזִיר, *nazirite*, at Judges 13:5 and 13:7, which the Greek renders ναζιραῖος. Either would be a play on sound rather than a quotation.

**Zechariah 9:9 at Matthew 21:2–7.** The Hebrew is synonymous parallelism, one animal named twice: *on a donkey, and on a colt, the foal of a she-ass*. The Septuagint preserves two nouns, ὑποζύγιον καὶ πῶλον νέον, because that is what the Hebrew has on the surface. Mark, Luke and John each have one animal. Matthew sends for two, has cloaks placed on both, and has him sit **ἐπάνω αὐτῶν** — *on them*, plural. This is the clearest single instance in the New Testament of a narrative detail generated by the reading of a text rather than the other way round.

To these should be added the operation Luke performs at 4:18–19, which combines all three of the mechanisms in this thesis in three moves within two verses. He quotes Isaiah 61 in the Septuagint form, which had already converted the opening of the prison into sight for the blind (§5.1). He **splices in** a clause from a different chapter — ἀποστεῖλαι τεθραυσμένους ἐν ἀφέσει, from Isaiah 58:6. And he **stops mid-verse**, ending at "the acceptable year of the Lord" and omitting the words that follow in both Hebrew and Greek: *and the day of vengeance of our God*. The programmatic statement of the ministry is a composite, and what it leaves out is as deliberate as what it includes.

One further case belongs in this section because it is habitually mis-filed as a scribal error. **Matthew 27:9 attributes a quotation of Zechariah 11:12–13 to Jeremiah**, and ℵ, B, A, C and W all read Jeremiah. The attribution is the author's, not a copyist's; the late minuscules 33 and 1579 and some Syriac and Persian witnesses that drop the name are performing the correction. The Hebrew of the passage has הַיּוֹצֵר, *the potter*, sitting one consonant away from הָאוֹצָר, *the treasury*, and the Septuagint read a third thing again, χωνευτήριον, *the foundry*. An evangelist composing from a memory of prophetic material, not from an open scroll, is the explanation the evidence supports.

## 7. The Third Silent Mechanism: Later Reading

The third mechanism is the simplest and the easiest to overlook: **passages that carry heavy weight in the modern argument and are not used by the New Testament at all.** No corruption is involved, and no author is responsible. The distance is created entirely downstream, by readers assembling a case their sources did not assemble.

**Daniel 9:24–27** is the standard chronological proof of the modern apologetic — the seventy weeks calculated forward to a dated crucifixion. **No New Testament author cites it.** Question one is decisive against the calculation in any case. The Masoretic accentuation places an *atnach*, the major verse-dividing accent, immediately after שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעָה, dividing the period into **seven weeks and then sixty-two weeks**; the figure of sixty-nine, on which every version of the calculation depends, requires disregarding that accent. The Hebrew also has **two anointed figures**, not one: a מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד at v. 25 and a מָשִׁיחַ at v. 26. And the Greek tradition, as noted in §5.1, renders the second impersonally.

**Isaiah 9:5–6** is the standard christological proof-text of the modern argument, and it too is **cited by no New Testament author**. The Septuagint does not translate the throne-name at all. Whatever the verse is doing, the first Christian writers did not do it with this verse.

**Psalm 22:17** is a third case, of a different kind. The modern argument treats "they pierced my hands and my feet" as a prophecy of crucifixion and often alleges that the Masoretes altered the text; the Hebrew reads כָּאֲרִי, *like a lion*, with **no verb at all**, and the Septuagint (Psalm 21:17) reads ὤρυξαν, *they dug*. The discovery at Nahal Hever of a psalms scroll from the late first century CE reading כארו, with a final *waw*, establishes that a Hebrew verbal reading existed in the first century and that the Greek translator was rendering a real variant rather than inventing one. It does **not** establish that the Masoretic reading is a later alteration; it establishes that both readings are ancient. And the point that closes the case is that **the New Testament never cites this clause**, in either form, at any of the four crucifixion narratives.

A fourth case shows the mechanism operating within the transmission of a translation rather than within an argument. At **Genesis 3:15** the Latin tradition produced *ipsa conteret*, *she shall crush* — a reading with no support in Hebrew, Greek or Samaritan, and one that carried a substantial weight of later devotional and doctrinal reflection. It is a later reading in the most literal sense: not a variant in the original language, not a choice by a biblical author, but a form that arose in a daughter version and was then read as though it were the text.

The class matters out of proportion to its size, because it locates a large part of the modern dispute outside the sources entirely. When the argument from prophecy is conducted over Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9, it is not being conducted over material the New Testament used. It is being conducted over material assembled later, from a translation, by readers whose case required texts their predecessors had not needed.

## 8. The Samaritan Pentateuch as Control Specimen

Everything in §§4–7 depends on inference. The compositional seams, the direction of translation, the authors' movement between source forms — none of it can be watched happening. This is the standing weakness of the whole enterprise, and it is the reason the Samaritan Pentateuch belongs in this thesis even though it says nothing about Jesus. **It is a control specimen: a complete, physically surviving, independently transmitted second edition of the Torah, whose editing can be observed rather than reconstructed.**

The scale of divergence is often overstated. Emanuel Tov's count of roughly 7,000 variants against the Masoretic Text is the figure usually quoted, but the great majority are orthographic. Abraham Tal and Moshe Florentin's analysis puts roughly **4,200 as substantial** and about 1,300 as matters of spelling and grammar. Agreements with the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text number fewer than a thousand, of which perhaps five hundred look like a genuinely shared Hebrew *Vorlage* rather than independent convergence — which is itself a significant datum, since it means a Hebrew textual tradition standing behind the Greek existed alongside the one the Masoretes transmitted.

Sorted by type, the substantial variants fall into four classes, and it is the classes rather than the count that make the specimen valuable.

**Ideological.** These are few and they are exactly where one would predict. At **Deuteronomy 27:4** the Masoretic Text has the altar built on Mount Ebal and the Samaritan has Gerizim. Throughout the centralisation formula the Masoretic imperfect יִבְחַר, *the place the Lord **will** choose*, appears in the Samaritan as the perfect בָּחַר, *has chosen* — converting an open future into an accomplished past that can only refer to Gerizim. And the Samaritan Decalogue carries a tenth commandment naming Gerizim, compiled out of Deuteronomy 11:29–30 and 27:2–7. *The direction of the Ebal/Gerizim variant is disputed and is recorded here as disputed: the Samaritan reading has ancient support, and the claim that either side altered the other requires evidence this audit did not gather.*

**Modernisation.** The Samaritan is a *Vulgärtext*: archaic and difficult forms are replaced with contemporary ones, unusual grammar is regularised, and awkward expressions are smoothed. Genesis 13:6 and Genesis 24:55 are standard examples. Nothing doctrinal is at stake; a scribal tradition is simply making its text readable.

**Small harmonisations.** At **Genesis 4:8** the Masoretic Text has Cain say something to Abel and then breaks off without reporting it; the Samaritan supplies the words, נלכה השדה, *let us go to the field*. At **Genesis 2:2** the Masoretic Text has God finish his work on the seventh day, which sits awkwardly with resting on it; the Samaritan moves the completion to the sixth.

**Large block expansions.** There are **exactly thirty-five** of these, and they are the most instructive class of all. A passage from Deuteronomy is imported bodily into Exodus so that the narrative reports the command being given as well as recalled — Deuteronomy 18:18–22, the promise of a prophet like Moses, is inserted into Exodus 20:21; Deuteronomy 9:20 is inserted into Exodus 32.

The specimen changes the relevant prior. Gap-filling, contradiction-resolution, ambiguity-closing, and relocation of textual blocks demonstrably occurred within a community that regarded its text as sacred. The Samaritan evidence does not prove that any particular New Testament seam arose by the same process, but it shows that such processes require no theory of conspiracy or bad faith. A text with two endings or an editorial voice is behaving in a way independently documented in an active scriptural tradition. [*No Original Exists*](No%20Original%20Exists_%20The%20Universal%20Reconstruction%20of%20Biblical%20Texts%20and%20the%20Forensic%20Recovery%20of%20Proto-Luke.md) reaches the related methodological conclusion that reconstruction from later witnesses is the normal condition of biblical scholarship rather than a special embarrassment of one corpus.

## 9. Patterns of Drift: From Relation Toward Identity

The preceding sections have reported four bodies of evidence — manuscript variants, compositional seams, translation divergences, and citation practice. They do not all move in one direction, and no defensible account should make them do so. The more precise question is whether the mixed transmission history surrounds a stable semantic architecture, and whether later changes and readings show a recurrent difficulty preserving that architecture.

The direction is not the one the popular argument predicts. On the sceptical account the movement should be from low christology to high christology, with scribes progressively elevating a human teacher. The audited variants do not show that. At 1 Timothy 3:16 a relative clause of manifestation becomes the flat subject “God”; at John 1:18 the earliest witnesses carry the more elevated *monogenēs theos* while much of the later tradition substitutes the more familiar “Son.” The second case is not evidence of elevation and should not be forced into the same local history as the first. Together they show why “higher” and “lower” are poor directional categories. One text is made more explicit; another is made more familiar. The recurrent movement appears at a different level: difficult relations are stabilised into categories on which interpretation can land. **The aggregate drift is from relation toward identity, not from a merely human Jesus toward a divine one.**

### 9.1 The Titles Are Shared

The premise underneath the identity-statement reading is that the christological titles are proper to one figure. In the biblical text they are not. Every one of them is held by someone else, in the same books, often in the same idiom.

| Title | Others who hold it, in the biblical text |
|---|---|
| Son of God | **Adam** (Luke 3:38); Israel corporately (Romans 9:4, υἱοθεσία); peacemakers (Matthew 5:9); all led by the Spirit (Romans 8:14, Galatians 3:26); the heavenly host in the Septuagint; the emperor, as *divi filius*, on coinage in circulation while the Gospels were written |
| Son of Man | Daniel 7:13 has "one **like** a son of man" — a comparison, not a designation — and Daniel 7:27 glosses the figure corporately as "the people of the holy ones of the Most High" |
| κύριος | "Sir" from the Samaritan woman (John 4:11); Philip, addressed by Greeks (John 12:21); a father, by his son (Matthew 21:30); any estate owner (Matthew 20:8, Colossians 4:1) |
| χριστός | Anointed priests (Leviticus 4:5); Saul, David and Solomon; and **Cyrus the Persian**, at Isaiah 45:1, τῷ χριστῷ μου Κύρῳ — a Gentile king, called the Lord's anointed |
| παῖς | David (Acts 4:25) and Israel (Luke 1:54) — the same word, in the same two books, as Acts 3:13 and 3:26 |
| μονογενής | **Isaac** (Hebrews 11:17) |
| The prophet like Moses | The prophetic succession generally; John the Baptist is asked whether he is the figure and says no (John 1:21) |
| θεός | **Satan** (2 Corinthians 4:4); **the belly** (Philippians 3:19); human judges (John 10:34, citing Psalm 82:6); pagan gods (Acts 14:11, 19:26); **Paul, by the islanders at Malta** (Acts 28:6); Herod Agrippa (Acts 12:22); and "many gods and many lords" acknowledged as a fact of the world (1 Corinthians 8:5) |

Two of these deserve to be drawn out, because they are cases where an English rendering has closed a question the Greek leaves open.

**μονογενής does not by itself mean "only-begotten."** Its New Testament usage supports *only*, *unique*, or *one of a kind*. Hebrews 11:17 is the internal control: Isaac is called Abraham's μονογενής although Abraham had other sons, because Isaac occupies the unique relation of promised heir. The traditional rendering *only-begotten* makes generation explicit where the Greek adjective need not. This bears directly on John 1:18: μονογενὴς θεός marks uniqueness of relation, and the remainder of the verse defines that relation — ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρὸς ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο, *the one who is in the bosom of the Father, that one has made him known*.

**παῖς is the Servant word.** Where Acts uses it of Jesus — 3:13, 3:26, 4:27, 4:30 — it is the Septuagint's word for the servant of Isaiah 42, 52 and 53, and the same book applies it to David and Luke applies it to Israel. It designates a **commissioned function**, someone appointed to a task by another, and the English "servant" or "child" both lose the point.

The correct conclusion from this table is narrow, and it is worth stating the boundary explicitly because it is easily overrun. Establishing that a title *can* be held by others tells us what the word permits. **It does not tell us what any given author meant by it.** A word with a wide range can still be used at full strength. What the table forecloses is only the argument that the title by itself settles the question — which is the argument most often made.

### 9.2 John 10:34: The Defence Is About Reception

One passage does what the table cannot: it records the argument being had, and it records the answer.

At John 10:33 the opponents state the charge in reported speech — ὅτι σὺ ἄνθρωπος ὢν ποιεῖς σεαυτὸν θεόν, *because you, being a man, make yourself God*. The identical construction appears at 5:18, likewise in the narrator's report of what opponents concluded. Both verses are textually secure, and both are frequently cited as evidence for what Jesus claimed. What they are evidence for is what his opponents took him to have claimed.

The reply is the datum. He quotes Psalm 82:6 — "I said, you are gods" — and gives the ground on which the word was applicable there: **πρὸς οὓς ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ἐγένετο**, *those to whom the word of God came*. Not those who were divine. Not those who claimed anything. **Those who received.** The defence offered against a charge of self-deification is an argument about reception, and it is a defence that would be unavailable to anyone whose position was that he was making an identity claim about himself.

This is where the two readings of the material separate most sharply, and where the identity frame runs out of moves. Read as an identity statement, the reply is a piece of rabbinic evasion — a lesser sense of θεός produced to deflect a charge the speaker means in a greater sense. Read as it stands, it is a straightforward statement of the actual position: the word applies to those through whom the word of God comes, and the objection is a category error made by hearers who have converted "the Father in me" into "making himself equal to God." **The text dramatises the literalising conversion**, twice, and gives the answer to it once. Whether one accepts that answer is a separate matter; that the text contains it is not in dispute, and no manuscript varies at either point.

### 9.3 John 1:34: Relation and Identity Without a Settled Direction

John 1:34 places a relational designation and a fixed title in direct textual competition, but the witnesses do not establish which came first.

At John 1:34 the Baptist's testimony reads either οὗτός ἐστιν **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ**, *this is the Chosen One of God*, or οὗτός ἐστιν **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ**, *this is the Son of God*. The first is carried by the original hand of Sinaiticus, Old Latin witnesses, the Curetonian Syriac and the Sahidic Coptic. The second is carried by **P66 and P75** and is printed in NA28. Both forms are therefore early and geographically distributed. P5 is not listed as support for ἐκλεκτός in the NA28 apparatus and cannot bear the claim previously made for it.

The semantic contrast remains real. **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ is not a low designation.** It is the Servant language of Isaiah 42:1 and states a relation — chosen, by someone, for something. ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ is a title attached directly to the figure. But semantic contrast is not chronology. If ἐκλεκτός were prior, the change would fit a movement from relation toward title; if υἱός were prior, it would not. External attestation alone leaves that direction unresolved.

Four other passages show clearer movements by different mechanisms. They should not be made to supply the direction that John 1:34 itself cannot establish:

| Passage | Form at issue | Alternative form | Mechanism or evidential status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 1:41 | **ὀργισθείς**, *moved with anger* (D, Old Latin a d ff2 r1) | σπλαγχνισθείς, *moved with compassion* | Anger-to-compassion is the more plausible scribal direction; the external evidence does not make it certain |
| John 1:34 | **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ** (ℵ*, Old Latin, Curetonian, Sahidic) | ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ (P66, P75, NA28) | Direction unresolved; early evidence stands on both sides |
| Mark 13:32 → Matthew 24:36 | **οὐδὲ ὁ υἱός**, *nor the Son* (retained in ℵ, B, D, Θ, f13, 33) | deleted in much of the Matthean tradition | Evangelist redaction, then further harmonisation |
| Mark 6:5 | **οὐκ ἐδύνατο**, *he could not* (all Greek) | *noluit*, *he would not* | Versional softening in the Old Latin only |
| Mark 10:18 → Matthew 19:17 | "Why do you call me good?" | "Why do you ask me about the good?" | Evangelist redaction — an author handling a difficult source |

The distinction in the right-hand column matters. Matthew rewriting Mark is **not corruption**: it is an author working with a source he finds theologically awkward, which is an ordinary compositional act and one his readers would have expected. What happens afterwards is a third thing again — Byzantine scribes pulling Matthew's wording back toward Mark's, undoing the evangelist's own change. Different processes can produce comparable local effects, but they do not license assigning the same direction to an unresolved variant.

### 9.4 The Secure Source–Expression Grammar

The controlling evidence for the framework reading is not a sequence of disputed variants. It is the wording that the manuscript tradition transmits securely. Variants show where copyists or communities encountered difficulty; stable passages show what they inherited. When the principal secure christological passages are mapped by grammatical relation rather than sorted into “high” and “low,” they form a coherent architecture.

| Passage | Secure textual datum | Relation encoded | Correspondential reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| John 1:1 | θεὸς is predicated of the Word while ὁ λόγος remains grammatically distinguished from ὁ θεός | Divine quality without personal collapse | The source is expressed fully in the Word without the expression becoming a second source |
| John 1:18 | No one has seen God; the unique one in the Father's bosom ἐξηγήσατο, makes or expounds him | Invisible source made known through one in interior relation to it | Divine love becomes perceptible through divine wisdom |
| John 10:30; 17:21–22 | ἕν is neuter, and the same unity-language is extended to the disciples | Unity that does not erase distinct receivers | Conjunction by shared influx rather than numerical identity of persons |
| John 10:34–38 | “Gods” are those to whom the word came; the works disclose that the Father is in Jesus and Jesus in the Father | Reception, works, and mutual indwelling answer the charge of self-deification | What is received from the source becomes present through the transparent receiver |
| Philippians 2:6–11 | Form of God, non-seizure or non-exploitation, emptying, servant-form, obedience, and exaltation by God remain in one sequence | Descent and exaltation structured by non-claiming | The receiver does not appropriate equality as possession; transparency is completed through self-emptying |
| Colossians 1:15–20 | The Son is the image of the invisible God; creation is described through him and toward him | Image and mediation, not replacement of the invisible source | The unseen source reaches expression through its visible form |
| Acts 3:13, 3:26; 4:27, 4:30 | Jesus is repeatedly called παῖς, the commissioned servant or child of God | Authority received for a task | Divine operation appears as commissioned function through a human receiver |
| 1 Corinthians 10:4 | “The rock was Christ” uses the copula rather than the language of resemblance | One reality present under another natural form | A direct instance of correspondential identification across degrees |
| Revelation 1:8; 1:17–18; 22:12–16 | Titles first spoken by the Lord God are also spoken by the dead-and-living Jesus, without a narrated contest between claimants | Shared predicates across source and expression | What belongs to the source is truthfully borne by the expression through which the source is present |

This table does not reduce the texts to a “low” christology. The predicates are too strong, the descent-language too explicit, and the scope of mediation too extensive. Nor does it support numerical identity. The texts repeatedly preserve the relation that identity metaphysics removes: the unseen and the one who makes seen; the sender and the commissioned one; the one who exalts and the one exalted; the Father within and the receiver through whom the works occur. Their claim is higher than promotion and more relational than identity. The source is present in the expression without ceasing to be source, and the expression bears the source without claiming independent possession.

**Correspondential key:**

| Textual form | Correspondential function |
|---|---|
| The Father / the invisible God | Divine love as source, not directly visible as an object |
| The Word, image, or unique expositor | Divine wisdom as the form in which love becomes knowable |
| The Father's bosom | Interior conjunction of expression with source |
| Sending, commissioning, and servant-language | Reception of function from the source rather than self-originating authority |
| Emptying and non-seizure | Refusal to convert what is received into possession |
| Mutual indwelling | Source present in receiver and receiver oriented wholly within source |
| Neuter unity | Conjunction without erasure of distinct selfhood |
| Shared divine predicates | The source truthfully manifest in and through its transparent expression |

The framework fit is exact at the level tested here. Universal influx predicts that divine operation will appear through receivers rather than as their independent property. The distinction between receiving and claiming predicts that the decisive figure will refuse self-originating goodness and power while expressing the source without remainder. Correspondence predicts that invisible cause will become visible as form without becoming a second cause. These are not repairs made after textual difficulty. They are the relations the secure text already encodes.

This result is local but positive. It does not depend on proving that every Johannine sentence is correspondential or on obtaining a whole-corpus frequency. It establishes that the principal secure passages examined here form a mutually coherent source–expression architecture and that the framework explains that architecture without discarding either the divine predicates or the preserved distinction between Father and Son. The competing low/high binary explains each half only by treating the other as a problem.

### 9.5 The Corpus Distribution

The final observation is distributional. It supports the secure mapping above but is methodologically narrower because the sample was selected from contested passages.

Sort the christological passages by *how the weight is carried* rather than by *what is concluded*, and the corpus separates into three groups:

| Corpus | How christological weight is carried | Where the disputes land |
|---|---|---|
| Johannine | Image, radiance, bosom, "has made him known," "the Father in me," neuter unity, anarthrous predicate before the verb | Grammar and lexical range; almost no textual variants |
| Undisputed Pauline | Function and office — ὤφθη, ὁρισθέντος, appointed, raised, sent, image of the invisible God | Lexical range; text secure |
| Pastorals, 2 Peter, Jude | **Stacked titles** — "our great God and Savior," "the only Master and Lord," "the God and Savior Jesus Christ" | Granville Sharp, punctuation, article placement, and the textual variants |

**Every christological Granville Sharp or punctuation dispute examined in this audit occurs in the epistolary corpus; none occurs in the Johannine set.** Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, 2 Thessalonians 1:12, Jude 4, Ephesians 5:5, and Romans 9:5 turn on whether one article governs two nouns or where punctuation is supplied to manuscripts that largely lack it. Several relevant textual alterations also occur in epistolary material: 1 Timothy 3:16 is a Pastoral; Jude 4 gains θεόν in P72, Ψ and the Byzantine tradition where ℵ, A, B, C and 33 omit it; Philippians 2:6 gains the article τῷ before θεῷ in F and G against P46, ℵ, A, B and the Byzantine text. This is a distribution within the audited sample, not a whole-corpus frequency measurement.

This is a real pattern in the audit's data, and it is consistent with the prediction stated in §2.1: through-language where the composition is correspondential, title-predication where it is not, and the disputes concentrated in the title-stacking layer. But **it is not yet a test**, and it must not be presented as one. The sample is defined by the argument — these are the passages people fight about — and passages people fight about are, by construction, passages where the wording is decidable in more than one way. A genuine test requires the fourth step, which this audit did not perform: define the corpora in advance, define the measure (through-language against title-predication), state the prediction, and then apply it **to the passages nobody argues about**. Until that is done, what is reported here is a distribution, not a result.

The relation of the Johannine material to the Jamesian tradition, and the compositional stratum from which its through-language may derive, are treated in [*The Enochic Thread in the Johannine Corpus*](The%20Enochic%20Thread%20in%20the%20Johannine%20Corpus_%20Testing%20the%20Jamesian%20Origin%20Hypothesis.md) and [*The Correspondential Architecture of the Four Gospels*](The%20Correspondential%20Architecture%20of%20the%20Four%20Gospels_%20A%20Compositional%20Analysis%20Through%20the%20Lens%20of%20Ontological%20Correspondence.md); the linguistic distinctness of the Pastorals is documented independently in [*The Pastoral Epistles in Modern Scholarship*](The%20Pastoral%20Epistles%20in%20Modern%20Scholarship_%20A%20Critical%20Validation%20of%20Authorship%20and%20Dating%20Consensus.md).

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What the Manuscript Tradition Contributes

The manuscript tradition contributes less than either side of the public argument requires, and what it does contribute is more interesting than what is claimed for it.

It contributes **one demonstrable late intrusion** in the audited set. The Comma Johanneum has no ancient Greek attestation, entered the Greek manuscript tradition under Latin influence, and made its printed Greek debut in the sixteenth century. It contributes **one directly observable christological alteration**, at 1 Timothy 3:16, where a relative pronoun became the word *God* by the addition of a crossbar and an overline, and where the direction of change is visible in corrected witnesses rather than inferred only from internal probability. It contributes a handful of well-attested expansions — the Longer Ending of Mark, the woman taken in adultery, and the fifteen examined verses of the Textus Receptus — whose contents are predominantly harmonisation, duplication, gloss, and liturgical completion rather than a coordinated christological programme. It also contributes abundant ordinary variation.

What it does not contribute is a programme. The evidence against a programme is not mere silence: the earliest witnesses at John 1:18 carry the reading a progressive-elevation account would tend to place late; the Jude 5 evidence can support the more loaded reading at an early stage; adjacent Lukan sayings divide differently across early witnesses; and sayings that create obvious christological difficulty remain secure. Mark 9:1 and 13:30 and Matthew 10:23 place fulfilment within a living generation. Mark 13:32 has the Son not knowing the day. Mark 10:18 preserves the question “Why do you call me good?” Mark 6:5 says he could not perform a mighty work there. **A tradition systematically improving christology had repeated opportunities to regularise these sayings and did not do so.** That observation constrains the progressive-elevation reconstruction more tightly than an isolated variant can.

The corollary for the apologetic side is equally firm. A tradition this stable did not need improving, and it did not deliver what is claimed from it. The high christological passages are secure — and secure in wording that, read as it stands rather than through the categories of the fourth century, says something other than what is read off it.

### 10.2 What the Translation Layer Contributes

The translation and citation layers contribute a substantial part of the distance, under conditions that make them difficult to see from the New Testament apparatus.

The source-level divergences are **invisible to the New Testament instrument**. They appear only when the Hebrew and Greek source editions are compared. Psalm 40 is different: comparison with Rahlfs–Hanhart reveals an inner-Greek difference between the Psalm and Hebrews, whose history requires a critical Septuagint apparatus not supplied by that edition.

The source-level translation differences are **innocent of Christian motive**. Jewish translators before Christianity were not preparing evidence for a later dispute. That claim does not extend automatically to the σῶμα reading in Hebrews, whose relation to the Greek Psalm tradition remains unresolved.

The layer is **load-bearing out of proportion to its visibility**. The body prepared in Hebrews 10 depends on σῶμα, which is absent from both the Hebrew and the Rahlfs–Hanhart Psalm. The specific incorruption argument of Acts 2 and 13 depends on διαφθορά, where the Hebrew speaks of the pit or destruction. The Jerusalem council's scriptural warrant for the Gentile mission uses a Greek form of Amos that differs from the Hebrew promise concerning Edom. Matthew's lexical presentation of the conception uses παρθένος where Isaiah has עַלְמָה. These observations do not refute the claims; they identify the textual form in which the scriptural warrants operate.

And beneath all of it lies the chain of §5.3: an older Jewish avoidance convention is reflected by κύριος in the Greek Bible and later recorded by Masoretic pointing. At Psalm 110:1 two lexically distinct Hebrew expressions consequently appear as one Greek word, and the New Testament then addresses Jesus with that word.

### 10.3 Implications for the Framework Hypothesis

The framework hypothesis under test in this library holds that correspondential compositions, received by communities that do not hold the key, undergo literalization: dynamic states become static entities, interior qualities become exterior persons, relations become substances. [*Reversing the Arrow*](../08_Correspondential_Texts/Reversing%20the%20Arrow_%20'Gnosticism'%20as%20the%20Downstream%20Literalization%20of%20Correspondential%20Composition.md) argues that this operation is universal rather than the property of any one movement, and [*The Literalized Fall*](../08_Correspondential_Texts/The%20Literalized%20Fall_%20The%20Sethian%20Corpus%20as%20Correspondential%20Architecture%20Without%20the%20Key.md) traces it through a corpus where the correspondential original and the literalized reception can both be examined.

Source gating determines the order of the inquiry; it does not require interpretive agnosticism after the source facts are fixed. The primary editions establish the wording, security, and direction of variants. Translation history and scribal practice identify proximate mechanisms. Only then does the framework enter as a model whose predictions can be compared with the recovered textual pattern.

At the first level, the audit establishes two results. **The progressive-elevation model fails as a description of the manuscript evidence:** the interventions are sparse, mixed, and bidirectional, while the strongest christological passages and several sayings most awkward for later identity claims are textually secure. **The secure positive grammar is source–expression grammar:** invisible source and visible image, sender and commissioned receiver, the Father within and the works performed through the Son, divine form joined to non-seizure and obedience, shared predicates joined to preserved relation.

At the second level, ordinary textual processes explain how particular surface forms arose. A copyist can complete an ambiguous relative clause; a translator can choose a more concrete Greek noun; a citer can work from a different Greek form; a later reader can treat a relation as a title. These mechanisms are not alternatives to the framework because they answer a different question. They identify **how** textual and semantic change occurred through human transmission. They do not explain why the stable source material is organized by source, reception, indwelling, image, descent, and non-claiming, or why concretisation repeatedly reduces precisely those relations.

At the third level, the framework makes the recovered pattern intelligible as one architecture. Correspondence predicts an invisible cause expressed in visible form without becoming a second cause. Influx predicts operation through a receiver whose power is received rather than self-originating. The distinction between receiving and claiming predicts a figure who bears divine predicates while refusing to possess goodness, authority, or equality as his own. Literalization predicts that downstream readers will tend to collapse these dynamic relations into fixed identities. The secure texts mapped in §9.4 exhibit the first three features directly; the recurrent subset of translation, citation, and scribal changes supplies bounded evidence for the fourth.

This is genuine corroboration, not proof generated by this audit alone. The framework entered the inquiry with independent evidential standing from tests elsewhere in the library; it was not invented to rescue these passages. The present evidence adds support because the observed combination — secure divine language, equally secure receiver-language, no progressive scribal ascent, and recurrent concretisation of relations — is expected under the framework and is poorly represented by the low/high binary. An identity model can retain every verse only by redescribing their relations as paradoxes inside an identity already assumed. The framework derives the same data from one source–expression structure.

The selected translation cases do not establish a corpus-wide law, and the distribution in §9.5 remains exploratory until measured over predefined corpora. Those limits constrain the range of the literalization claim. They do not erase the positive result already present in the secure passages or restore a tie between models that make different predictions about them.

### 10.4 The Marcionite Witness as Bounded Corroboration

The Marcionite *Evangelion* is not a surviving manuscript and cannot be given the evidential control of P75 or Codex Vaticanus. Its wording is reconstructed from hostile citations and reports, and Roth's graded method appropriately distinguishes secure attestation from recoverable wording and conjecture. The secure broad datum is a gospel with a shorter opening than canonical Luke: Tertullian's account in *Adversus Marcionem* IV moves from the fifteenth year of Tiberius to Capernaum, while Epiphanius in *Panarion* 42 reports the absence of the preface, infancy material, baptism, and genealogy. The exact wording of the transition is less secure.

The *Apostolikon* supplies one relevant convergence. Tertullian and Epiphanius attest the title **πρὸς Λαοδικέας** for the letter known canonically as Ephesians, while ἐν Ἐφέσῳ is absent from P46 and the original hands of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. These are independent forms of evidence that the destination attached to this letter was not stable. The absence of the Pastorals and Hebrews from Marcion's collection is also a fact about that collection, but by itself establishes neither the direction of textual development nor the reasons for exclusion.

Whether canonical Luke expands a shorter predecessor or the Marcionite gospel abbreviates canonical Luke requires a separate reconstruction from patristic testimony and composition-internal arguments. [*No Original Exists*](No%20Original%20Exists_%20The%20Universal%20Reconstruction%20of%20Biblical%20Texts%20and%20the%20Forensic%20Recovery%20of%20Proto-Luke.md) argues for expansion within the New Marcionite Paradigm, but this audit has not independently reproduced that case and does not use it to establish any primary text-critical conclusion. The reconstructed witness corroborates the existence of materially different early textual states; the direction between them remains outside the evidential burden of this thesis.

### 10.5 Limitations and Lacunae

The limits below constrain claims actually made by this thesis.

**The 126-item sample is selected rather than exhaustive.** It is designed around passages carrying christological or textual-critical weight, not as a random sample of the New Testament. The secure source–expression mapping in §9.4 is a direct analysis of those passages, but the corpus distribution in §9.5 may partly reflect where disputes arise. It cannot establish whole-corpus frequencies.

**The translation set is directionally mixed and selected from load-bearing passages.** Four source-level divergences plausibly concretise, others move differently, and Psalm 40 belongs to citation history rather than Hebrew–Greek translation. The thesis therefore claims recurrence in identified cases, not a measured Septuagint-wide tendency.

**The through-language prediction has not yet been tested over predefined corpora.** Section 9.5 describes the audited distribution. A full distributional test requires corpora and measures fixed in advance, application to undisputed as well as disputed passages, and a control corpus. This limitation applies to the frequency claim, not to the grammatical relations directly present in the secure texts.

**Composition-internal arguments are less determinate than direct variants.** Narrative seams, duplicated endings, and temporal discontinuities can raise a compositional question without proving the history that produced them. The thesis treats them as a separate evidential class and does not assign them the certainty of a visible correction or an attested omission.

**Reconstructed witnesses require graded use.** The Marcionite gospel is recoverable through hostile testimony rather than an extant codex. Securely attested absence, recoverable wording, and proposed direction of dependence are not equivalent. No conclusion in §9.4 depends on resolving the direction between the Marcionite gospel and canonical Luke.

**Shared titles establish lexical range, not authorial intention.** Section 9.1 defeats arguments that move directly from a title to numerical identity. The positive source–expression reading depends instead on syntax, discourse, and the convergence of multiple secure passages.

## 11. Conclusion: The Text the Apparatus Preserves

The decisive result of this audit is not that the New Testament manuscripts expose a hidden manufacture of divinity. They do not. The audited tradition contains a demonstrable late intrusion at the Comma Johanneum and a directly observable christological alteration at 1 Timothy 3:16, but it does not contain a progressive programme by which copyists turned a human teacher into God. Its interventions are sparse and mixed. Its earliest witnesses can preserve the more elevated reading, as at John 1:18. Its copyists leave intact sayings about received authority, limited knowledge, inability, obedience, prayer, and fulfilment within a living generation.

The apparatus is therefore most important for what it secures. John 1 distinguishes the Word from *ho theos* while predicating *theos* of the Word. John 1:18 places the unique expositor in the Father's bosom and makes him the one through whom the invisible God becomes known. John 10 answers a charge of self-deification with reception — “those to whom the word of God came” — and then interprets the works as the Father in Jesus. Philippians 2 joins divine form to non-seizure, emptying, obedience, and exaltation by God. Colossians 1 calls the Son the image of the invisible God. Acts repeatedly calls Jesus God's *pais*, the commissioned servant or child. Revelation gives divine predicates to the one who died and lives while retaining the source from whom the revelation comes. These are not late corrections. They are the secure textual architecture.

Translation, citation, selection, composition, and later reading act around that architecture. The Hebrew and Greek source forms do not always say the same thing; New Testament authors select between them; citation can produce a form absent from both extant source editions; early authors reshape inherited material; copyists occasionally clarify or harmonise; later readers supply punctuation and metaphysical categories. These mechanisms explain how particular expressions became more concrete or identificatory. They do not produce the central source–expression grammar, because that grammar is already present in the passages least dependent on textual reconstruction.

Read through the framework, the preserved relations resolve into one model. **The Father names divine love as source. The Word or Son names divine wisdom as that source expressed and made knowable. Jesus is the celestial human receiver in whom that expression becomes present in human life.** He is not a second source and not a merely human teacher subsequently promoted by tradition. His humanity remains real: it receives, prays, obeys, is tested, does not know all things, and does not claim goodness or power as its own. Precisely through that non-claiming, the divine source can be present in him without obstruction. The divine predicates are therefore neither honorific exaggerations nor declarations that erase the receiver. They name what is truly expressed through him.

On this reading, passages conventionally divided into “high” and “low” christology are not contrary trajectories. The image of the invisible God and the servant of God describe the same structure from opposite sides. “The Father and I are one” names conjunction; “the Father is greater than I” preserves direction of influx. Divine form and obedience belong in one hymn because reception is not possession. Shared titles in Revelation do not create rival deities because the expression bears what belongs to its source. Glorification is the completion of this conjunction, not the promotion of an ordinary human into a new divine identity.

The text-critical and framework results therefore reinforce one another. Text criticism removes the proposed scribal ascent and recovers a stable relational grammar. The framework predicts that grammar and explains why divine presence and human reception remain simultaneously true. Ordinary transmission processes remain the proximate means by which some relations were clarified, concretised, or collapsed; they are the mechanisms of the history, not a competing account of its meaning. Because the framework had independent predictive standing before this audit, the fit constitutes additional evidence rather than circular restatement.

The selected translation cases do not all move in one direction, John 1:34 does not yield a recoverable direction, and the corpus distribution awaits a predefined full-corpus test. None of those unresolved measurements is load-bearing for the central result. The principal passages are secure, and their convergence does not depend on converting a mixed transmission record into a uniform law.

The controlling verse is therefore John 10:34. When the charge is that a man makes himself God, the answer identifies those **πρὸς οὓς ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ἐγένετο** — *to whom the word of God came*. The defence turns on reception, and the following appeal turns on works performed by the Father within. The text records the conversion of received divine presence into a claim of self-deification, then supplies its own correction. The manuscripts preserve both sides without a christologically significant variant.

*The apparatus could not expose this as a variant because the source–expression structure was already in the text.*
