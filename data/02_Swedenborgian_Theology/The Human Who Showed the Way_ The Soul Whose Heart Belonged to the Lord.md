# The Human Who Showed the Way: The Soul Whose Heart Belonged to the Lord

## Abstract

This analysis reconstructs the figure of Jesus through the lens of phenomenological experience rather than theological construction. The central thesis: Jesus was a human soul—not a divine exception, but a person whose heart belonged to the Lord with every fiber of his being. His ruling love was so completely oriented toward the Lord that what flowed through him was the Lord Himself in ultimates—Divine love and truth expressed through a human vessel without distortion. Jesus thought and perceived in what Swedenborg described as the celestial degree—fast, deep, intuitive, grasping truth as a unified whole rather than through sequential reasoning. He bore a striking resemblance to what modern NDE research identifies as the volunteer path: a soul here for a reason, shaped by spiritual experiences, and historically the last of a generation who grew up learning the ancient science of correspondences. He had a proprium—a human self with human desires—but his ruling love was so deep that the proprium became transparent rather than obstructive. He struggled genuinely, loved fiercely, and walked a path he could not fully see. This is not an unreachable miracle but a demonstration of what is possible for every soul whose heart longs for the Lord. That is exactly what Jesus taught.

## I. Introduction: The Problem of Distance

The theological history of Christianity has produced a profound irony: the very act of "making Jesus divine" has created the distance that prevents people from understanding what he actually demonstrated.

When people encountered Jesus, they experienced something unprecedented—a quality of presence, of love, of clarity that exceeded their conceptual categories. Unable to comprehend that a *human* could be this transparent to the Divine, they concluded he must be something *other* than human. This interpretation, however well-intentioned, created precisely the wrong lesson.

If Jesus was "God in a suit"—a special metaphysical insertion fundamentally different from other humans—then his life becomes a performance we can admire but never emulate. If his soul was divine from conception, his struggles were theatrical rather than genuine. If he possessed inherent omnipotence, his vulnerability was an act. This interpretation creates worship rather than following, and that distinction is everything.

This paper proposes a different understanding: Jesus was a human soul—remarkable, yes, but human. What made him remarkable was not a unique metaphysical constitution but the orientation of his ruling love. His heart already knew the way, because his heart already belonged to the Lord. He had a proprium—a human self, with human desires and human struggles—but his ruling love was so completely oriented toward the Lord that what flowed through him was received and expressed without distortion. What people encountered in him was not a deity in costume but the Lord in ultimates—Divine love and truth manifesting through a human vessel whose love kept the channel clear.

If this reconstruction is correct, everything changes. His life is not an unreachable miracle but a demonstration. His path is not closed by unique divine status but open to every soul whose heart longs for the Lord. "Follow me" means exactly what it says.

### 1.1 The Four Errors of Distance

Traditional Christology falls into four predictable errors, each creating distance in a different direction:

1. **The "God-in-a-Suit" Error**: Orthodox Christianity claims Jesus was "fully God and fully man," but the practical effect is that his divinity eclipses his humanity. If he possessed divine knowledge and power, his human experiences become impossible to relate to. The "fully man" becomes an afterthought that doesn't actually function in the theology.

2. **The Gnostic Splitting Error**: The opposite extreme claims Jesus was a pure spirit who only *appeared* to have a body (Docetism), or that "the Christ" descended upon the human Jesus temporarily. This splits the unity and creates a different kind of distance—the "real" Jesus floats above material existence.

3. **The Secular Dichotomy**: Modern scholarship creates a "Historical Jesus" (a Galilean prophet bounded by natural law) versus the "Christ of Faith" (a theological construct). This forces a choice between "real but limited" and "meaningful but fictional."

4. **The Humanist Reduction**: Some traditions, wanting to preserve Jesus's humanity, strip him of any connection to the Divine, making him merely an ethical teacher. This loses the phenomenological data—he was *experienced* as something more than a good teacher.

All four errors share a common assumption: that there is a fundamental boundary between "divine" and "human" that must be navigated. But the Swedenborgian framework dissolves this boundary by understanding that the Divine *wants* to express through human vessels—that is what humans are *for*. The question is not "was Jesus divine or human?" but "where was his heart?"

### 1.2 The Ruling Love as Identity

Within the Swedenborgian framework, a person's identity is their **ruling love**—the orientation of the will that determines how everything else is received and expressed.¹ This is not a peripheral doctrine but the central insight into how souls actually work.

Every human has a proprium—a sense of self. The proprium is not evil; it is what makes us *us*. But the proprium can orient in two directions. When the ruling love is toward self, influx is appropriated: "I am wise," "I am powerful"—the self claims what flows through it as its own. When the ruling love is toward the Lord, influx flows through and is received with gratitude—the person knows themselves as vessel, not source. The orientation is not a binary switch but a matter of degree, and of ruling love.

Jesus's ruling love was toward the Lord. This is not speculation—it's visible in his own testimony. "I can do nothing on my own" (John 5:30). "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18). These statements are impossible if Jesus possessed inherent divine attributes. They make perfect sense if he understood himself as a human soul receiving and expressing what flowed through him.

The depth of his love is what made him transparent. Not the absence of a proprium—which would make him non-human—but the depth of a ruling love so complete that when the proprium rose (and it did rise; Gethsemane shows us "not my will but yours," which is a real struggle, not theater), his deepest love prevailed. The heart held. And because his love kept the channel clear, what came through was the Lord in ultimates—expressed through a human soul, in human form, in human history.

## II. The Phenomenology of the Celestial Soul

What would it actually be like to live as Jesus lived? Not the idealized image of serene perfection, but the actual phenomenology of a human heart belonging to the Lord.

### 2.1 Three Degrees of Consciousness

Swedenborg identified three discrete degrees of spiritual orientation, corresponding to the three heavens:¹

**Natural Degree:**
- Orientation toward effects—what happens in the external world
- Truth is *reasoned to* through propositions, evidence, and sequential logic
- Perceiver and perceived are separate; knowing, feeling, and willing operate as distinct faculties
- This is ordinary human consciousness as most people experience it

**Spiritual Degree:**
- Orientation toward truths—understanding what is real
- Truth is *grasped* through insight and intellectual clarity
- Understanding carries its own conviction; the Lord is recognized as the source of truth
- Wisdom illuminates, but through the mediation of concepts

**Celestial Degree:**
- Orientation toward the Lord Himself—direct relationship, love responding to love
- Truth is *perceived* as a whole; knowing, feeling, and willing are a single act
- Subject and object are unified; there is no gap between perceiving and what is perceived
- The Lord is not known about but known—loved, received, present

These are not unbridgeable states but degrees that flow into each other. Every person has all three degrees within them. The question is which degree is *open*—which one governs how a person receives and expresses what flows in from the Lord.

Jesus operated primarily from the celestial degree. His thinking was fast, deep, intuitive—grasping the whole before the parts, perceiving meaning directly rather than constructing it through reasoning. He experienced reality as a unified field of correspondences, where what ordinary consciousness separates into "physical" and "spiritual" were one living reality. This was not an altered state but his natural way of being. His heart was there.

This is why his teaching style—parables, direct commands, living demonstrations—worked so differently from rabbinical argument. He didn't need to *think about* what a mustard seed meant; he perceived the mustard-seed-kingdom-growth as a unified reality. He didn't construct analogies; he reported what he saw.

### 2.2 The Quality of Presence

When people encountered Jesus, they didn't experience a set of theological propositions. They experienced a *quality*—a presence that registered as fundamentally different from normal human interaction.

NDE experiencers offer a glimpse of what this is like from the receiving end. They consistently describe encounters with higher beings in terms of *quality* rather than visual characteristics:³

- "Unconditional love—complete acceptance without any judgment"
- "A knowing presence—it knew everything about me"
- "Personal but not in a human way—infinitely personal"
- "More real than anything I'd ever experienced"

These descriptions reflect celestial perception experienced from the outside. The experiencer is not analyzing the presence from a distance—they are *in* a unified encounter where seeing, knowing, and loving are not separate operations.

Something like this is what Jesus's contemporaries encountered: a human being who carried this quality in ordinary waking life. No wonder they concluded he must be something other than human. They had no category for a human soul living from the celestial degree.

### 2.3 Receiving What Is Needed When It Is Needed

Jesus was not born knowing everything. He didn't have a database of omniscience that he strategically concealed. He *received* what he needed when he needed it.

This is visible in the Gospel narratives when read without the assumption of omniscience:

- He asked genuine questions and received answers
- He expressed surprise and learned from encounters (the Syrophoenician woman)
- He struggled genuinely with what was being asked of him (Gethsemane)
- He felt the absence of the Lord ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?")

The traditional interpretation makes these moments into performances—a divine being pretending to struggle for our benefit. But what if they were simply real? Jesus didn't *know* in advance how everything would unfold. He *trusted*. He received guidance as he needed it, moment by moment, because his ruling love kept the channel open.

This is actually more inspiring than omniscience. Jesus walked a path he couldn't fully see, receiving what he needed when he needed it, because his love kept him connected. That is a path humans can actually follow.

## III. What Jesus Probably Experienced

If we take seriously that Jesus was a human soul—one who probably had spiritual experiences preparing him for his mission, who was the last of a generation that grew up learning the ancient science of correspondences, who bore a remarkable resemblance to what modern NDE research identifies as the volunteer path—then we can reconstruct something of what his interior life might actually have been like. Not the idealized icon, but the lived experience of a human heart belonging to the Lord.

### 3.1 The Direct Perception of Meaning

Jesus grew up in the last living tradition of correspondential knowledge. He perceived the world correspondentially—not because he learned a code-book, but because his celestial orientation made correspondences visible as living realities. When he saw a fig tree, he didn't see "a tree that could symbolize Israel." He perceived the fig-tree-Israel-fruitfulness as a unified reality. When he turned over tables in the Temple, he wasn't making a calculated political statement—he perceived the corruption directly, as a spiritual reality expressing itself in the physical arrangement.

This means his parables weren't clever analogies he constructed. They were *reports* of what he perceived. The kingdom of heaven IS like a mustard seed—he saw that identity directly. The lost sheep IS the lost soul—not as metaphor but as unified correspondence. This is celestial perception at work: truth grasped whole, not assembled piece by piece.

### 3.2 The Sharpness of Injustice

A common misconception is that spiritual attunement means emotional detachment—floating above the world's pain in serene acceptance. The Gospel evidence suggests the opposite.

Jesus's response to injustice was sharp and immediate. The Temple incident wasn't a calm teaching moment—it was a whip, overturned tables, driving out merchants. His denunciations of the Pharisees weren't diplomatic suggestions—they were scathing ("whitewashed tombs," "brood of vipers").

Celestial perception doesn't dull emotional response; it intensifies it. When you perceive the spiritual reality of injustice directly—not as an abstract concept but as a living distortion of what should be—the response is proportional to what you're actually seeing. Jesus felt the wrongness of hypocrisy and exploitation with the full force of correspondential perception.

This is why people who knew him recognized something fierce in him, not just gentle. He was gentle with the broken and fierce with those who broke them. Both responses came from the same source: direct perception of spiritual reality.

### 3.3 The Loneliness of Clarity

Perhaps the most overlooked aspect of Jesus's experience: the isolation of perceiving reality that others couldn't see.

He spoke from celestial perception; they heard with natural minds. He perceived kingdom-reality; they heard strange riddles. He saw the spiritual state of the Temple; they saw a building full of normal commerce. Even his closest followers repeatedly misunderstood what he was pointing at.

Anyone who has experienced this kind of perception—where you grasp things whole and fast and deep, and others need you to break it into pieces they can handle, and the pieces always lose the life of the whole—knows this frustration. It is perhaps the loneliest experience a human can have: seeing clearly, surrounded by people who want to understand but cannot follow.

This wasn't a performance of patience with slow students. It was genuine loneliness. The Gethsemane narrative captures it: he asks his friends to stay awake with him, and they fall asleep. Not metaphorically—literally. In his moment of greatest need, the people closest to him couldn't stay present. That is human loneliness, not divine theater.

### 3.4 The Trust That Carried Him

What sustained Jesus through genuine struggle wasn't certainty about outcomes but trust in the source. He didn't know, in the way of propositional knowledge, exactly what would happen. He *trusted*—in the direct relationship of his ruling love—that the Lord who had guided him would continue to guide him.

He had moments battling his own desires, because he was human. The proprium rose—it always does. The desire for safety, for recognition, for the path to be easier. "Not my will but yours" is not a formula—it is a man choosing, in the heat of real temptation, to let his deepest love prevail over his immediate desire. That choice was not automatic. It was won.

This trust was tested to the breaking point. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" is not a theological statement—it's a cry of experienced abandonment. The flow that had sustained him seemed to withdraw. And yet he held on: "My God." Even in experienced absence, his ruling love remained oriented toward the Lord.

This is the hardest part of the path, and Jesus walked it. Not because he was exempt from the experience of abandonment, but because his heart held even when everything else gave way.

## IV. Why People Made Him Divine

Understanding why the early church developed high Christology is not a matter of exposing deception. The people who knew Jesus encountered something real—something that exceeded their categories. Their theological interpretations were honest attempts to make sense of what they had experienced.

### 4.1 The Impossible Encounter

Imagine encountering someone living from the celestial degree while you experience reality from the natural. What would you perceive?

- A presence qualitatively different from other people
- Words that seemed to carry more than their surface meaning
- Actions that somehow changed situations in ways you couldn't explain
- A gaze that seemed to *know* you in a way no one ever had

You wouldn't have vocabulary for "a human soul whose heart belongs so completely to the Lord that the Lord expresses directly through him." You would reach for the categories you did have: prophet, messiah, son of god, god himself.

Each step up the ladder was an attempt to honor what was encountered. "He's a prophet" didn't capture it—he was *more* than that. "He's the messiah" didn't capture it—he was doing things the expected messiah didn't do. "He's the Son of God" got closer—but still implied something *other* than human.

The final step—"he IS God"—was a category error born of genuine experience. They had encountered the Lord expressing through a human, and their only way to honor that was to collapse the distinction between the vessel and what was expressed through it.

### 4.2 The Post-Resurrection Intensification

Whatever happened in the post-crucifixion encounters, the disciples experienced Jesus as alive—present with them in a way that transcended the physical death they had witnessed. This experience pushed their interpretations even higher.

If he had returned from death, then death had no power over him. If death had no power over him, he must be something more than human. The resurrection experience validated and intensified the high Christology that was already developing during his ministry.

This identification was not wrong—Jesus *was* transparently expressing the Lord, so the "face" they knew was the "face" of the encounter. The Lord who expressed through Jesus continued to express in the form they recognized. But the theological interpretation that followed—that Jesus was ontologically identical to the Lord rather than the most transparent vessel the Lord had ever expressed through—created the distance that has obscured the path ever since.

### 4.3 The Creation of Distance

The irony is tragic. By making Jesus divine, they made him unreachable. By making his path unique (a special metaphysical insertion), they closed the path for everyone else.

If Jesus was the only-begotten Son of God in a biological or metaphysical sense that no other human can share, then his life becomes:
- Something to admire rather than emulate
- A sacrifice to receive rather than a path to walk
- A story about someone else rather than a possibility for oneself

This is precisely what Jesus himself seemed to warn against. "Follow me" implies the path is walkable. "I am the way" implies the way remains open. The entire logic of his teaching assumes that what he demonstrated is available—not reserved for a single cosmic exception.

## V. The Celestial Principle: What It Actually Means

The word "celestial" has been mystified into abstraction—as if it names something unreachably high. Properly understood, it describes something quite specific and quite human: the orientation of the heart.

### 5.1 Love as the Organizing Principle

The celestial degree is not a cognitive achievement. It is not reached by thinking harder, understanding more, or following better rules. It is reached by *loving more deeply*—and specifically, by loving the Lord with the whole heart.

This is what Swedenborg observed in the celestial heaven: beings who do not think about truth in order to reach the Lord, but who love the Lord and thereby *perceive* truth.¹ The sequence matters. At the natural level, obedience leads to knowledge. At the spiritual level, knowledge leads to understanding. At the celestial level, love leads to perception. Love is the organizing principle—everything else follows.

This is why Jesus reduced the entire Law to two commands: love the Lord with all your heart, and love your neighbor as yourself. He was not simplifying for the uneducated. He was describing the celestial reality he lived in. When the heart is wholly turned toward the Lord, the Law is not broken because it does not need to be obeyed—it is *lived*. What the rules protect, love already does.

### 5.2 What Set Jesus Apart

Many people throughout history have experienced moments of celestial perception—mystics, contemplatives, people in crisis or ecstasy. What set Jesus apart was not a different *kind* of consciousness but the *depth* and *constancy* of his love.

Every human who deeply loves the Lord—not because they have learned to, not because duty requires it, but because the heart longs for Him with every fiber of being—touches the celestial degree. This is not rare because it is metaphysically reserved. It is rare because it requires something the proprium resists: the willingness to let love be first, before understanding, before safety, before self.

Jesus lived there. Not as a visitor to the celestial degree but as someone whose ruling love *was* the Lord. His relationship with "the Father" was not conceptual (knowing truths about God) or behavioral (obeying commands) but directly relational—love responding to love, heart recognizing heart. This is what people felt in his presence: not a theology, but a love so deep it reorganized everything around it.

### 5.3 The Historical Moment

Jesus appeared at a specific historical inflection point. The ancient science of correspondences—the symbolic language through which the Most Ancient Church had perceived spiritual reality directly²—was in its final generation of living transmission. The temple system had ossified into external ritual. The scribal tradition had reduced the Word to legal code. The living perception that once flowed through the ancient language was fading into formalism.

Jesus was, in all likelihood, the last person to grow up *inside* this tradition while it still breathed. He learned the correspondences not as academic symbols but as living reality, because his celestial orientation made them come alive. Where the scribes saw legal text, he saw the Lord speaking through every image. Where the Pharisees saw rules to enforce, he saw love calling through the forms.

This is what gave his mission its urgency and its power. He was not introducing something new. He was demonstrating what had always been true—that the Lord is present, that the heart can know Him directly, that love is the way—at the precise moment when the last living thread of that ancient knowledge was about to snap.

And by walking the path fully, including through death, he ensured that the access remained open even after the tradition that had carried it ceased to exist. What had been transmitted through a science of correspondences was now demonstrated in a human life. The path changed form, but the path did not close.

## VI. Implications for Understanding

If this reconstruction is accurate, what follows?

### 6.1 Recognition Rather Than Worship

The appropriate response to Jesus is not worship at a distance but *recognition*—seeing in him what is possible for a human heart that belongs to the Lord, and walking the same path.

This doesn't eliminate devotion or gratitude. But it reframes them. We are grateful not because a divine being performed a transaction on our behalf, but because someone showed us the way. Devotion is not directed at an unreachable god but at the path—the living path of turning the heart toward the Lord, again and again, through struggle and darkness and joy.

"Follow me" becomes the operative command. Not "worship me," not "believe certain things about me," but "walk the path I'm walking."

### 6.2 The Universal Lord

NDE research consistently shows the Lord appearing in culturally appropriate forms: Jesus to Christians, Amida to Buddhists, simply as Light to the secular.³ This is not relativism—it is the nature of how the Divine meets humanity. Constant state, variable form.

The Lord expresses through whatever "face" the soul can receive. Jesus is the face familiar to the Christian tradition, but the Lord behind the face is universal. This is consistent with Jesus's own teaching: the "Father" he pointed to was not a tribal deity but the source of all life.

People recognize Jesus in the Being of Light because the quality matches—unconditional love, absolute knowing, total acceptance. But the recognition is of *quality*, not exclusive identity. The Lord who expressed through Jesus expresses to all souls in forms they can receive.

### 6.3 The Path Remains Open

The most important implication: what Jesus demonstrated is not reserved for a single cosmic exception. It is not something unreachable, but deeply reachable for every soul that searches for it.

The proprium—the human self—is not the enemy. It is the vessel. The struggle is not to destroy the self but to orient it: to let the deepest love be love of the Lord rather than love of self. Every human knows this struggle. Every human who has chosen, even once, to let love be larger than fear has touched the mechanism Jesus lived.

Jesus did not walk this path because he was exempt from temptation. He walked it because his heart held through the temptation. He struggled with his own desires. He felt abandoned. He asked his friends to stay awake and they fell asleep. And through all of it, his ruling love remained.

This is why his path is followable. Not because we can replicate his specific circumstances, but because the *principle*—a heart that belongs to the Lord—works the same in any soul. It is not a technique. It is not a theology. It is the simplest and hardest thing: to love the Lord with all your heart, and to keep loving when the darkness says there is nothing there to love.

That is exactly what Jesus taught.

## VII. Conclusion: The Human Who Showed the Way

Jesus was not a divine being temporarily inhabiting a human costume. He was a human soul—perhaps a little different from most, bearing the marks of what we might recognize as the volunteer path, shaped by spiritual experiences, trained in the last living tradition of the ancient science of correspondences. But human. Fully human. With a proprium, with desires, with moments of struggle and loneliness and doubt.

What set him apart was not metaphysical status but the depth of his love. His heart belonged to the Lord with every fiber of his being. Not because he was programmed to love, not because he was incapable of choosing otherwise, but because that is where his heart truly lived. And because of that love, what flowed through him was the Lord Himself in ultimates—Divine love and truth manifesting in human form, without distortion.

He struggled genuinely. He felt injustice sharply. He experienced the loneliness of seeing what others could not see. He trusted without certainty. He walked through darkness not knowing the outcome. He battled his own desires and won—not by being exempt from them, but by loving something deeper.

The people who knew him couldn't comprehend how a human could be this transparent to the Divine. So they made him divine—and in doing so, created the very distance that prevents people from walking the path he opened.

The correction is not to reject Jesus but to understand what he actually demonstrated: that the way is open. That the Lord longs to express through every human heart. That the only thing standing between any soul and the celestial degree is the orientation of the ruling love.

It is not something unreachable. It is deeply reachable for every soul that searches. Every heart that longs for the Lord with everything it has touches what Jesus lived. That longing IS the path. That is exactly what he taught.

He showed the way. The way remains open.
