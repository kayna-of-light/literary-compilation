# From Planets to Constellations: The Outward Migration of Otherworldly Spirits and the Epistemology of Variable Form

## Summary

Between 1758 and the present, the reported home of otherworldly beings moved outward: from the planets of the solar system, in Swedenborg, the Spiritualist mediums, the Theosophists and the contactees of the 1950s, to the Pleiades, Sirius, Arcturus and Lyra in the contact and channelled literature after 1975. The move followed the space probes of 1962–1976, which closed the solar system to inhabited worlds.

This study argues that the move has to be read on two layers that must not be confused.

The first is **the literature**: a chain of books, séances, contact notes and channelled texts that inherit from one another, answer one another, respond to scientific disconfirmation, and carry a large share of construction, borrowing and inflation. The outward migration is first of all a fact about this literature.

The second is **experience**: what people undergo, often unbidden, often never explained to them, and then report with a margin of error. Experience comes in two broad kinds. Some is **forced** — sought, induced, produced on demand in séance, trance or channelling sessions. Some is **unforced** — it simply happens, as in a near-death experience. The two kinds are not equally weighted, and neither is a verdict on its own.

Read this way, three things follow. The stellar addresses of the later literature belong to the literature, not to the experience: in the project's archive of spontaneous near-death narratives they are almost absent, and where a being's star of origin does appear, the experiencer usually says the label came later, from books, quizzes or psychics. What does recur in unforced experience is the **quality and function** of the beings met, not their address — the framework's "constant state, variable form". And Swedenborg's own rules for speaking with spirits, which he set down in the same weeks as his planetary reports, are the right instrument for sorting both layers: applied to him, they keep his observations of spiritual quality and discount the planetary addresses, which are exactly the kind of statement he warned against trusting.

## 1. Two Layers, and Why They Must Be Kept Apart

### 1.1 Experience and literature

An experience is not the same thing as its report, and a report is not the same thing as the literature built from it. Between what someone undergoes and the page lie the receiver's memory, vocabulary and expectations; between the page and the next book lie editors, publishers, readers who want more, and authors who have read the earlier books. The outward migration of otherworldly homes can be a fact about any of these stages. Most of the evidence for it is evidence about the last one.

### 1.2 Forced and unforced experience

The distinction that matters most within experience is between what is sought and what simply comes.

**Forced experience** is induced or solicited: the séance, the trance session, automatic writing, channelling on schedule, the deliberate opening of the mind to whatever speaks. Its products depend heavily on the medium's own stock of ideas and on the wishes of those present. **Unforced experience** arrives without being sought: the near-death experience, the spontaneous out-of-body experience, the vision that interrupts an ordinary day. The experiencer often never learns why or how it happened.

The distinction sorts weight, not verdicts. A forced setting does not prove that nothing real occurs in it, and an unforced experience is not thereby free of later accretion. Both points are documented below.

### 1.3 The framework's own criteria

The sharpest statement of the distinction comes from inside the framework. On 20 March 1748 — in the same weeks in which he recorded the spirits he associated with Mars and Jupiter — Swedenborg wrote:

*"When spirits begin to speak with man, he must beware lest he believe them in anything; for they say almost anything; things are fabricated by them, and they lie … wherefore, when spirits were speaking, I was not permitted to have faith in the things which they related"* (*Spiritual Diary* §1622).

He explained why such speech is unreliable on material matters: spirits "judged and affirmed from my persuasion. Consequently if I demonstrated anything falsely, they would be persuaded concerning that also, for in respect to material things they cannot judge from themselves" (*Spiritual Diary* §1776).

Ten years later he distinguished, in effect, forced from unforced contact. Speaking with spirits "is rarely granted because it is dangerous." Those who "meditate much on religious subjects, and are so intent upon them as to see them as it were inwardly within themselves, begin to hear spirits speaking with them"; such persons "are visionaries and enthusiasts; and whatever spirit they hear they believe to be the Holy Spirit," while the spirits themselves "see falsities as truths." By contrast, "some also who lead a solitary life sometimes hear spirits talking with them, and without danger" (*Heaven and Hell* §249). Speech with the angels of heaven, he adds, is granted only to those "who are in truths from good" (*Heaven and Hell* §250).

These are the framework's own discrimination rules, not an outside skeptic's. They say three things that this study relies on: what spirits say about material facts follows the receiver's persuasion; solicited contact draws in spirits that take falsities for truths; and unsought contact is the safer kind.

## 2. What Happened in the Literature

### 2.1 The phases of the outward move

**The solar system (1748–1930s).** The plurality-of-worlds debate, charted by Steven J. Dick (1982), Michael J. Crowe (1986) and David Dunér (2016), made the planets into Earth-like globes that teleology and analogy required to be inhabited (Dunér 2016; Crowe 1986). Swedenborg's statement of the principle is that "where a terrestrial body exists, so does the human being" (*Arcana Coelestia* §9237, quoted in Bedford 2006). His *Spiritual Diary* (1748), *Arcana Coelestia* (1749–1756) and *Earths in the Universe* (1758) placed spirits on Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the Moon, and also on earths "in the starry heaven." In the 1890s the Geneva medium Hélène Smith produced a "Martian cycle," inaugurated at a séance of 25 November 1894 (Flournoy 1900). Theosophy placed the "Lords of the Flame" and Sanat Kumara on Venus (Greer 2003).

**The contactees (1950–1965).** Desmond Leslie's half of *Flying Saucers Have Landed* opens with "ancient legends" set "about eighteen million years ago" and tells how "the Lord of the Flame, or Sanat Kumara" came to Earth from Venus; George Adamski's half reports a telepathic visitor from Venus met near Desert Center, California, on 20 November 1952 (Leslie and Adamski 1953). The book physically binds the Theosophical legend to the new saucer reports. The dominant contactee literature of the 1950s named the planets, above all Venus (Melton and Eberhart 1995; Rothstein 2013); George Van Tassel's "Ashtar" (Helland 2003) and George King's Aetherius Society, founded in 1955 after his contact with the Venusian Master Aetherius (Lewis 2003), belong to the same wave.

**The stars (from 1975).** Between 1962 and 1976 the *Mariner*, *Venera* and *Viking* missions showed Venus to have a crushing carbon-dioxide atmosphere and a surface far too hot for life, Mercury to be airless, and Mars to be a cold desert in which the *Viking* landers of 1976 detected no life (Dunne 1974; NASA, "60 Years and Counting"; PDS Geosciences Node, "Mariner Missions"). The contactee literature changed its cosmography. Gregory L. Reece builds the turn into the structure of his history: chapter 5, "Scout Ships from Venus," and chapter 6, "Beam Ships from the Pleiades" (Reece 2007). On 28 January 1975 the Swiss contactee Eduard "Billy" Meier recorded his first meeting with "Semjase," who said she originated "in the Pleiades" (Stevens 1988, 33). Brad Steiger popularised the "starseed" idea (Steiger 1976; Steiger and Steiger 1981), and the channelled literature of the 1980s and 1990s built an interstellar genealogy of humankind — Lyra, Sirius, Orion, the Pleiades, Arcturus (Royal and Priest 1989, 19–20; Marciniak 1992). Starseeds today describe themselves as "aliens or alien consciousnesses in human form" (Crockford 2021).

### 2.2 The literature names its own reason for moving

The study of new religious movements names the disconfirmation as the turning point. The channellers of the 1970s and after "channel beings who originate on planets far beyond the reach of contemporary science," and "most importantly, their accounts of life on their home planet is not susceptible to possible falsification, a major flaw of the early contactees whose descriptions of Venus, Mars, and the Moon were disconfirmed even in their lifetime" (Encyclopedia.com, "Contactees").

The contact literature says the same in its own voice. In Meier's notes of 7 July 1975, Semjase dismisses "the deceptive pretentions of certain ones who spoke worldwide of presumed contacts with human beings of your neighboring planet Venus" and predicts that "after not too many years it will become evident to you by your sciences, that on that planet exist no human creatures of the form described by certain deceivers, neither in spiritual nor material form"; she cites the planet's heat and pressure and the Soviet probes "completely squashed" before reaching its surface (Stevens 1988, 269–274). The newer layer of the literature disowns the older one by appeal to the probes, and moves its own beings to a place the probes cannot reach. A second route out was dematerialisation: beings once described as pilots of metal craft became non-physical or "ultraterrestrial" (Helland 2003). Royal and Priest's Arcturus is "more of a realm than an actual place" (Royal and Priest 1989, 52).

### 2.3 How the literature was made

Read closely, the sources show how much of the literature is construction, borrowing and inflation.

**The séance produces what the sitters want.** Flournoy followed Hélène Smith's mediumship for five years. Her "Martian" language, he showed, "is only a puerile counterfeit of French," created "in order to respond to the manifest desire of the sitters" to know what the words meant; the Martian romance itself grew from an atmosphere of popular astronomy and spiritist expectation that "every good medium has the right to ask herself whether she is not the being predestined to accomplish this unrivalled mission" (Flournoy 1900, ch. 5). His verdict is not a blanket dismissal: "As to the supernormal, I believe I have actually found a little telekinesis and telepathy. As to lucidity and spiritistic messages, I have only encountered some brilliant reconstructions, which the hypnoid imagination, aided by latent memory, excels in fabricating in the case of mediums" (Flournoy 1900, ch. 11). Something real, in other words, and a great deal of construction on top of it.

**The contact notes carry the science and the pseudo-science of their day.** Semjase's Venus passage of 1975 reproduces the probe results — a surface temperature of 457 °C, a day of 117 Earth days, a carbon-dioxide atmosphere — but gives two different surface pressures within a few sentences, "334 times higher" and "107 times greater" than Earth's (Stevens 1988, 271–272). It explains Venus as a body captured "3,453 years ago" whose passage close to Earth plunged "the whole solar system … into disorder and chaos" and set the planet "rotating in the opposite direction" (Stevens 1988, 271). That is the shape of Immanuel Velikovsky's *Worlds in Collision* (1950), in which Venus is a newcomer, a comet whose near-collisions with Earth in the age of the Exodus brought the plagues of Egypt before it settled into its present orbit (Sladek 1974, 23–27). The same passage claims that Semjase's people are "the highest developed creatures who from outside your world travel to this Earth" (Stevens 1988, 268). Sympathetic later authors record that when Meier's models of Pleiadian craft were found, "the whole case was labeled a hoax" (Royal and Priest 1989, 47). By Swedenborg's rule of *Spiritual Diary* §1776, a communication on material things that mirrors the receiver's library is what should be expected; by any rule, it cannot be taken as information from the Pleiades.

**The channelled books are compiled from other books.** Royal and Priest describe *The Prism of Lyra* as "a compilation of years of insights, deductive reasoning, and channeling," with "meticulous cross referencing of various channels" and "research into a number of respected anthropological and metaphysical works," adding that "the reader may consider these ideas to be literal or symbolic, for the story is the same" (Royal and Priest 1989, 7). Their bibliography lists Robert Temple's *The Sirius Mystery* (1976), three books by Zecharia Sitchin, Raymond Moody's *Life After Life*, Gary Kinder's *Light Years* (on Meier), John Fuller's *The Interrupted Journey*, Whitley Strieber's *Communion* and the channel Darryl Anka (Royal and Priest 1989, 84). Their trinary Sirius comes with a footnote to the astronomers who speculated about it and to the Dogon (Royal and Priest 1989, 34–35); their teaching that the light at the end of the tunnel is "a representation of the Arcturus vibration" comes with a quotation from Moody (Royal and Priest 1989, 52–53).

**Channelling is a forced mode by its own description.** Norma Milanovich received *We, the Arcturians* in a "semi-trance state, allowing the source to communicate through her via a microcomputer," typing "as fast as 60 to 80 words a minute, non-stop, for as long as an hour or more"; the transcripts were run through a spell checker, and "whenever this software package determined that a word was misspelled, and suggested a correction, the correction was given priority" (Milanovich 1990, vii–viii). The beings "had never appeared to her" (Milanovich 1990, 37), yet the book describes their bodies — "about one meter high," with skins "of a greenish color" and three fingers (Milanovich 1990, 39–40). Marciniak's Pleiadians say of themselves: "We like to pride ourselves on being storytellers," and "no matter what story we tell you today, we guarantee you that a year from now we will tell you a different story" (Marciniak 1992, ch. 1).

### 2.4 What the literature's continuities can and cannot show

Set beside Swedenborg's planetary spirits, parts of the later literature echo him. His spirits of Mars "live in societies, but not under governments" and are "for the most part celestial men" (*Spiritual Diary* §1540; *Earths in the Universe* §85); the Pleiadians of the post-1975 books are teachers of love who set out "to create a culture based on harmony, truth, and unconditional love" in which "the Whole was as important as the Self" (Royal and Priest 1989, 45; see also Stevens 1988, 141–142; Marciniak 1992, ch. 1). His spirits of Jupiter make the education of their children their greatest concern and "know nothing whatever" of the sciences (*Earths in the Universe* §§48, 62); Milanovich's Arcturians are ruled by "those who govern our units and take care of our young," measure learning by "Light frequency" rather than "the tallying of facts and data," and are "not able to destroy" (Milanovich 1990, 55, 115–116, 119). His Mercurian and Saturnian profiles have no clear counterpart in these books.

Such echoes are weak evidence. The literature is a chain of inheritance: the Theosophical Venus passes through Leslie to Adamski, Meier's Pleiades pass through Kinder to Royal and Priest, and every channelled book stands on earlier ones. A quality that recurs in the literature may recur because experiences recur, or because authors inherit motifs from their predecessors, and the literature alone cannot say which. It also mixes the qualities with their opposites — Royal and Priest's Pleiadians, once worshipped as gods by early peoples, "began to relish the power they were given" (Royal and Priest 1989, 46). The literature is a record of what was written, not a clean record of what was met.

## 3. What Happens in Experience

### 3.1 Do unforced experiences carry stellar addresses?

If the stellar addresses belong to the literature, they should be rare in spontaneous experience, and where they appear they should tend to arrive afterward. The project's NDE archive allows a direct check. A full-text search of all 6,768 raw files (6,751 unique narratives) from the NDERF and IANDS archives for the star names of the contact literature — the Pleiades, Sirius, Arcturus, Lyra, Vega, Andromeda, Orion, Zeta Reticuli and "starseed" — finds fourteen files that use one of the terms; five use it of something else (a son named Orion, a Chevrolet Vega, NASA's Orion programme, an e-mail address), leaving nine accounts (*star_name_search.py*). Read one by one, they fall into three groups.

- **Five describe travel among named stars**: "We went to the Pléiades and Orion's Belt" (NDERF 8836, Bonnie J); a passage through the Orion Nebula where Jesus "opened a portal" (NDERF 9508, Sarah M); the constellation Orion on the way home (NDERF 2422, Lou F); finding oneself "in the middle of the Pleiades" (NDERF, Brenda M, 2025); and a spiral of stars identified only afterwards: "Years later while surfing the web I came across exactly what I saw and it said that it was the Andromeda galaxy" (NDERF 9687, Leonard M). None of these gives a being a star of origin.
- **One** affirms that "Starseeds exist" without naming a star (NDERF 9996, MB).
- **Three** tie a being or the experiencer to a star. In two, the experiencer says where the label came from. A French Canadian experiencer writes that he knows he is "a Starseed. All Starseed quizzes give me 95% … I am connected with the Pleiadians. I discovered all this at the end of 2016 at the same time that I discovered the books" (IANDS, "French Canadian man has lifetime of NDEs and after-effects"). Another writes, "I later met with many psychics and others who have helped me understand … that I came from the star Sirius" (IANDS, "Hitting head at age 8 leads to lifetime of visits to heaven and spirit work"). The third answers a questionnaire item on named religious figures with "PLEIADIANS, Beings of light," without saying where the name came from (NDERF 8295, Cortney P).

Unnamed "other planets" appear more often, usually as places visited or glimpsed rather than as the homes of the beings met. Several experiencers put their own error bars on such scenes. One thought she had been taken aboard a spacecraft — "by Aliens, perhaps? But I realized this was only wishful on my part" (NDERF 971, Maggie S). Another writes of "meeting an ancestor from another planet": "I do have a belief in life outside of Earth. I'm curious if that could have tainted the experience" (NDERF 9848, Robert RB). A third describes a tall being who "may have been my Guarding Angel, or an Extraterrestrial… Or perhaps One in the Same" (NDERF 8911, Dennis M). And one who began "asking questions like a small child. What about aliens? And parallel universes and life on other planets and UFOs" received "a great yet quiet chuckle of amusement," and adds that the being "was more than he appeared to be to me in that moment, this was just what I could comprehend at this time" (NDERF 3648, Bridget F).

The result is what the two-layer reading predicts. In roughly 6,750 spontaneous accounts, the named stars of the contact literature are nearly absent, and where a being's star of origin does appear, the experiencer usually reports acquiring it later, from books, quizzes, psychics or the web. Unforced experience does not supply the astronomy; the literature supplies it afterwards.

### 3.2 What recurs in unforced experience

What the same archive does show, in audited analyses, is constancy of function under variation of name and form. The religious background of the experiencer predicts the name given to the presence met barely above chance (cross-validated AUC ≈ 0.53; *NDE notebook 01*). The function of the beings tracks who they are: divine or religious figures teach in 23.0% of encounters, deceased relatives in 4.5% (length-adjusted odds ratio 6.27, 95% CI 3.94–9.98; *NDE notebook 07*). And experience frequently contradicts expectation: 57.2% (95% CI 52.9–61.3) of experiencers of a Being of Light who comment on the point report that what they met contradicted or surprised their expectations (*NDE notebook 01*). These are the marks of an encounter that is not simply produced by the receiver's prior beliefs, though shaped by them in form.

Two limits apply. All these variables are coded from one retrospective narrative, so the associations show that the reports are coherent, not that the reported events occurred. And the perceptual mode does vary with the receiver: among Christian experiencers, encounters named "Jesus" are coded as a visual being of light 45 percentage points more often than those described as an unknown presence, which in turn report more experience of unity (*NDE notebook 04*). The constancy is of state and function; the form varies — which is the framework's principle stated at the level of data, without any astronomy at all.

### 3.3 Unforced is not unmediated

An unforced experience still passes through a person. It is remembered, told, retold, read about, and fitted to a vocabulary. The archive shows the process in motion: experiences described afterwards in the vocabulary of the starseed books, an Andromeda found on the web years later. The margin of error is real, and so is inflation — the move from an experience of being loved and of not belonging to an identity as a "Light warrior" with a Pleiadian guide, as the apostle Thomas, or as a being from Sirius. What the experience itself leaves is usually simpler, and often unexplained even to the one who had it.

## 4. Swedenborg Between the Layers

### 4.1 The kind of experience he had

Swedenborg's state fits neither category cleanly. He describes it as given: his respiration "was so formed by the Lord, that I could respire inwardly for a considerable time," and "is so directed, whilst I am ignorant (thereof), in order that I may be with spirits and speak with them" (*Spiritual Diary* §3317). He also records that in childhood he "wished to hold my breath purposely" at morning and evening prayers, and later noticed that he held his breath "as if it were tacit" when concentrating (*Spiritual Diary* §3320). His was not a near-death experience, and not a séance; it was a sustained state, lasting decades, which he attributed to the Lord and to which he applied his own warnings.

### 4.2 Observation and explanation

His notes distinguish what he perceived from what he inferred. He defined the "planetary" spirits by their function in the Grand Man — those of Jupiter "represent rational ideas," of Saturn "interior sense, or reason," of Mercury "knowledges," of Mars "thought" (*Spiritual Diary* §1558, appended note of 19 March 1748). He recorded that in the spiritual world "there is no notion of time or space," so that those "in other earths" were "made to be present in a moment of time" (*Spiritual Diary* §3476). And of one class of spirits he wrote that he "was hence persuaded" they came from Jupiter, then qualified it: they might be from one of its satellites, "I am not sure … for as I could not have an idea of any sort of men except such as live on earths surrounded by atmospheres" (*Spiritual Diary* §§1669–1670). The address, here, is openly his own inference, limited by what his natural idea could hold.

His own rules therefore cut through his own reports. The planetary addresses are statements about material things — the class of statement in which spirits "judge from man's persuasion" (*Spiritual Diary* §1776), and in which he himself "was not permitted to have faith in the things which they related" (*Spiritual Diary* §1622). The persuasion was the science of his time: the premise of inhabited planets came to him from his scientific formation and the natural philosophers he read (Goerwitz 1985; Dunér 2016). What his rules leave standing is the observation — the quality and function of the spirits he met. This is the library's standing principle: Swedenborg was right in his observations, and not always in his explanations, above all where they concerned the physical world (*The Living Book* §3.4). His report of the Ancient Word in Great Tartary is a different case, in which observation and place converge (*The Living Book* §§3.2–3.3, 4.3).

### 4.3 The Swedenborgian commentators

Allen J. Bedford states the problem plainly: there is "not one whit of evidence that any life at all, much less human life, exists anywhere in this solar system … except right here on planet earth," and "many Swedenborgians have developed elaborate explanations for this dearth of evidence" (Bedford 2006, 315–316). The Swedenborg Foundation lists the readers' theories: that he spoke metaphorically; that he met real spirits but was mistaken or misled about their planets; that life once existed there or exists undetected (Swedenborg Foundation n.d.). Lee Woofenden puts the second plainly — "Swedenborg was wrong about this … It's just that they didn't come from the planets he thought they did" — and moves the spirits' homes to planets around other stars (Woofenden 2014). That relocation repeats the literature's own move of the 1970s: it keeps a physical address by placing it beyond reach. The two-layer reading needs no address at all. What Swedenborg met were spirits of a given quality; the planet was his explanation of them.

## 5. Conclusion

The outward migration from planets to constellations is real, but it is first a fact about a literature. That literature inherits from itself, borrows from the science and pseudo-science of its day, answers its own disconfirmation by moving its beings beyond the reach of probes, and in its channelled forms is produced in the forced mode that Swedenborg himself warned against. Its echoes of his planetary qualities may reflect recurring experience or inherited motif; the literature cannot tell which, and it should not be read as a record of encounters without heavy discount.

Beneath it lies experience, much of it honest and much of it unforced. In the project's archive of spontaneous near-death narratives, the stellar addresses of the contact literature are almost absent, and where they appear they were usually acquired afterwards. What recurs is the quality and function of the beings met, under names and forms that vary with the receiver — constant state, variable form, observed in the data without any astronomy.

Swedenborg stands between the layers, and his own rules sort them. Spirits judge material things from the receiver's persuasion (*Spiritual Diary* §1776); what they relate is not to be believed (*Spiritual Diary* §1622); solicited contact draws in enthusiastic spirits who see falsities as truths, while unsought contact is safer (*Heaven and Hell* §249). Applied to the later literature, these rules discount its cosmologies. Applied to Swedenborg, they discount his planetary addresses and keep his observations. The open question is whether the qualities recorded in the literature correspond to those in unforced experience; it can be tested by coding the qualities of the beings met in spontaneous accounts and comparing them with the channelled texts, with the comparison fixed in advance.
