**Citation:** Denzey Lewis, Nicola, and Justine Ariel Blount. "Rethinking the Origins of the Nag Hammadi Codices." *Journal of Biblical Literature* 133, no. 2 (2014): 399–419.
**URLs:**
- [SBL Press / Scholarly Publishing Collective](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/sblpress/jbl/article-abstract/133/2/399/179126/Rethinking-the-Origins-of-the-Nag-Hammadi-Codices)

**Date accessed:** 2026-03-23
**Source quality:** High — peer-reviewed in JBL, one of the top biblical studies journals

## Key Findings

### Challenge to the Standard Narrative
- The authors eliminate as "unsupportable": the connection to the Pachomian monastery, much of Muhammad Ali al-Samman's account, and the supposed specific location of the find
- They challenge Robinson's narrative as containing "inconsistencies, ambiguities, implicitly colonialist attitudes, and assumptions"

### The Grave Goods Theory
- Propose the codices were commissioned by "private Greco-Egyptian citizens in late antiquity" for personal use
- The texts were deposited "as grave goods following a practice well attested in Egypt"
- Some volumes may have been intentionally crafted as funerary deposits — Christian "Books of the Dead"
- This places them in a funerary tradition that began in the dynastic era, not a monastic preservation tradition

### Supporting Evidence
- Multiple codices may have come from different tombs, not one unified library
- The discovery site appears to be a cemetery area
- Physical evidence from the codices shows no signs of deliberate hiding
- The Codex Tchacos (Gospel of Judas) was found in a family tomb — precedent for codices as grave goods
- The excellent preservation suggests protected burial in containers, consistent with funerary practice

### Scholarly Reception
- Larry Hurtado found their argument provided "a strong basis" for the view
- However, Lundhaug & Jenott (2015, 2018) and Linjamaa (2024) have pushed back, further strengthening the Pachomian hypothesis
- The debate is ONGOING — not settled

## Counter-Arguments (from Lundhaug/Jenott/Linjamaa)
- Cartonnage evidence still strongly links to Pachomian monastic infrastructure (monk Sansnos's cattle receipts)
- Headquarters monastery at Pabau only 8.7 km from burial site
- Linjamaa (2024) argues codices were used by "a small intellectual monastic elite at a Pachomian monastery"
- The codices were "a smaller part of a much wider Christian library"

## Significance for the Research

**This is a genuinely open question.** Two competing theories:

1. **Preservation theory:** Monks buried the texts deliberately to protect them from Athanasius's destruction order → supports the "seed bank" reading
2. **Grave goods theory:** Private individuals commissioned the texts and were buried with them → complicates the "seed bank" reading

Either way:
- The TEXTS exist and their content is established
- They were found in 1945 after ~1,600 years
- The timing of discovery is not affected by the burial theory
- The correspondential content is present regardless of who buried them and why

But intellectual honesty requires acknowledging the debate. The thesis should present both possibilities and note that the providential pattern (timing, convergence with other discoveries, arrival of interpretive tools) holds under either scenario.

**Important note:** Pachomius himself died c. 348 CE — the same year as the latest dated cartonnage receipt. This is a striking coincidence that neither theory fully explains.
