# A Garden Enclosed: The Song of Solomon Read in Correspondence

## Demonstrating Ancient Word Preservation and Compiler Mediation in an Anomalous Biblical Text

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis presents a complete correspondential reading of the Song of Solomon — every verse analyzed, every image decoded, every passage classified as continuous Ancient Word narrative, compiled correspondential catalogue, court poetry, or editorial bridge. The central finding is that the Song, when stripped of its compiler's additions, preserves seven passages of unbroken correspondential narrative that describe a single regeneration cycle: from the end of vastation through seeking in darkness, conjunction in the sealed garden, the withdrawal that deepens longing, the inspection of spiritual growth, the completion of conjunction, and the permanent sealing of divine love. These seven passages — approximately 42 of the Song's 117 verses — constitute Ancient Word material, written in the intimate register of the Bronze Church (perception through good). The remaining 75 verses are the compiler's contribution: three body-description catalogues that display correspondential vocabulary without connecting it into narrative, fragments of court poetry from a Solomonic literary milieu, and editorial bridges that stitch the compilation together. The compiled material is not without value — the catalogues reveal that the compiler possessed a genuine correspondential lexicon, drawing on symbolic vocabulary whose meanings he could list but whose grammar he could no longer speak. The thesis further demonstrates that Swedenborg's much-misunderstood judgment — that the Song "does not possess a continuous internal sense" — is not a dismissal but a precise diagnosis, confirmed by the analysis, and that it provides the key that four centuries of competing interpretive schools have been unable to find. The Song of Solomon is a garden enclosed: the Ancient Word sealed within a compiler's text, waiting for the correspondential key to open it.

> **Keywords.** Song of Solomon, Song of Songs, correspondences, Ancient Word, regeneration, compilation, Bronze Church, Swedenborg, canonical anomaly, correspondential vocabulary, spiritual reading, discrete degrees

## 1. Introduction

The Song of Solomon is the most anomalous text in the Hebrew Bible. It mentions God nowhere. It contains no law, no prophecy, no historical narrative, no wisdom instruction. Its surface is erotic poetry — the desire of a woman for her beloved, their meeting and separation, the landscape of their love. It has embarrassed and fascinated readers for over two millennia. The rabbis debated whether it defiles the hands. The Church Fathers baptized it as allegory. Modern scholarship stripped the allegory and declared it secular love poetry. No consensus has held. The text resists every frame that is applied to it.

There is a reason for this. Every interpretive tradition has grasped a partial truth and mistaken it for the whole. The rabbinic tradition correctly sensed that the Song expresses the covenant relationship between the Divine and Israel — but lacked the hermeneutic tool to identify *how* human love poetry could carry divine content without allegory. The Christian typological tradition correctly identified the bridegroom as Christ and the bride as the soul — but projected later Christological categories that the text itself does not contain. The modern literal reading correctly recognized that the surface is genuinely intimate, genuinely erotic, genuinely bodily — but concluded that the surface is all there is. Each tradition detected the signature of something deeper. None possessed the key.

The key is the doctrine of correspondences.

In the companion thesis *Two Registers of One Perception*, the stripped Song — seven passages surviving the removal of catalogues, court poetry, and editorial bridging — was compared to the correspondential substrate of the Kephalaia of the Teacher. The structural identity between the two texts was exact: the same seven-state regeneration cycle, the same internal causal logic, in two cultural registers (intimate Hebrew, cosmological Iranian) that share not a single word of surface vocabulary. That thesis used the Song as one half of a comparison. This thesis turns to the Song itself.

What follows is a complete correspondential analysis of the Song of Solomon — all 117 verses, all eight chapters, every image decoded and classified. The thesis is organized around a central claim: the Song is a *compiled text* that preserves *genuine Ancient Word material* embedded in material that is not Ancient Word. The compiler — a later Israelite literary figure, or succession of figures — possessed a correspondential vocabulary inherited from an older tradition, but lacked the ability to compose in correspondential narrative. He could list the furniture of the Ancient Word's symbolic language but could not build rooms with it. The result is the Song as we have it: a text that reads mysteriously because it oscillates between passages of extraordinary spiritual coherence and passages of decorative correspondential display.

Swedenborg diagnosed this in the eighteenth century. He stated that the Song of Solomon does not possess a continuous internal sense and is therefore not part of the Word in the same way as Genesis, the Psalms, or the Prophets (*Sacred Scripture* §§18–19; *Athanasian Creed* §§3–4). But he also stated that the Song's author borrowed correspondential imagery from the Ancient Word — the pre-Hebraic body of correspondential knowledge that Swedenborg identified as the original source of all genuine sacred symbolism. This thesis puts both claims to the test. The test yields two findings:

**First:** Swedenborg's negative claim is confirmed. The Song as a whole does not possess continuous internal sense. Approximately two-thirds of the text — body-description catalogues, court poetry, editorial bridges — consists of valid correspondential images assembled without causal narrative movement. These passages read the way a dictionary reads: individual entries are correct, but the dictionary does not tell a story.

**Second:** Swedenborg's positive claim is confirmed. When the compiled material is removed, the remaining one-third of the text — approximately 42 verses distributed across seven passages — does possess continuous internal sense. These seven passages narrate a single, complete regeneration cycle with strict causal sequencing: each state produces the next, and the sequence cannot be rearranged without destroying the spiritual meaning. This is the Ancient Word material. It survived the compilation because the compiler included it, and it is recognizable because the correspondential framework provides the diagnostic criterion that no other hermeneutic possesses: the test of continuous causal movement.

The thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the methodology: what correspondential reading is, how the compiler hypothesis works, and the three categories of material that the analysis identifies. Section 3 examines Swedenborg's diagnosis in detail — what he actually said, what he did not say, and why the distinction matters. Section 4 presents the Ancient Word layer: all seven passages with their spiritual readings and correspondential key tables. Section 5 analyzes the compiled layer in full: the body-description catalogues, the court poetry, and the editorial bridges. Section 6 examines what the catalogues reveal about the compiler's knowledge — the correspondential vocabulary he possessed without the grammar. Section 7 traces the internal logic of the seven-state cycle as a single regeneration narrative. Section 8 surveys the interpretive history — rabbinic, Christian, modern, and cultic — and shows how each tradition partially succeeded because it detected a real feature of the text, and ultimately failed because it lacked the correspondential key. Section 9 addresses why the Song survived in the canon despite its anomalous character. Section 10 discusses implications, the compiler's dual role as keeper and obstructor, and limitations of the analysis. Section 11 concludes.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading

The reading method employed in this thesis is the doctrine of correspondences as articulated by Swedenborg and applied throughout the companion theses of this project. Correspondence is the organic, functional relationship between a natural object and the spiritual reality it expresses. It is not allegory (arbitrary cultural assignment), not symbol (conventional sign), not metaphor (comparative likeness). It is ontological participation: the natural object IS the spiritual reality in its ultimate, most external form. Light corresponds to wisdom because light enables the eye to distinguish forms — the identical function that wisdom performs for the intellect. Fire corresponds to love because fire is the active principle from which light proceeds, as love is the active principle from which wisdom proceeds. A garden corresponds to the interior mind because a garden is an enclosed space where life is cultivated — exactly what the mind is when it receives and grows what has been planted in it.

The test of a correspondential reading is threefold. First, the functional relationship must hold: the correspondence must be grounded in what the natural object actually does, not in what someone decided it should mean. Second, the correspondence must be consistent across contexts: a garden means the interior mind in every passage where it appears, not the interior mind in one verse and political power in the next. Third — and this is the decisive criterion for this thesis — the correspondential reading must yield a continuous spiritual narrative in which states follow from one another in causal sequence. Where all three criteria are met, we have continuous internal sense. Where any one fails — especially the third — we do not.

This third criterion is the scalpel that distinguishes Ancient Word material from compiled material. Both types use valid correspondential images. Both deploy genuine spiritual vocabulary. The difference is that the Ancient Word material *narrates* — states produce states in sequence — while the compiled material *displays*. The Ancient Word tells a story with correspondential words. The compiler arranges the same words without a story. The test is rearrangement: if a passage can be rearranged without loss of spiritual sense, it is display. If rearrangement destroys the sense — if "seeking by night" must precede "finding," if "the sealed garden" must be opened before "the beloved enters," if "withdrawal" must follow "conjunction" for the narrative to cohere — then the passage preserves the causal architecture that only continuous internal sense produces.

### 2.2 The Compiler Hypothesis: Narration versus Display

The Song of Solomon as we have it is a literary anthology. This is not a controversial claim — even scholars who disagree about every other aspect of the Song agree that it is composite, assembled from multiple sources and possibly multiple periods. The question is what the compiler was working with and what he did with it.

The compiler hypothesis employed in this thesis is as follows. The compiler of the Song possessed two kinds of material:

**Material with continuous internal sense.** Passages of correspondential narrative — poetry written by perceivers who experienced spiritual states directly and expressed them in the intimate, bodily register of the will. These passages narrate: state follows state, cause produces effect, the spiritual biography of the soul unfolds in sequence. These are the Ancient Word fragments.

**Correspondential vocabulary without narrative architecture.** Lists of valid correspondences — body parts, botanical items, precious materials, landscape features — inherited from the same tradition that produced the narrative passages but detached from narrative context. These are the raw materials of a correspondential language: the words without the grammar, the furniture without the room. The compiler assembled these into decorative catalogues that read impressively but do not move.

The compiler also added material of his own composition or from his cultural milieu: court poetry celebrating Solomon, military imagery, editorial bridges connecting the seams between the fragments, and transitional formulas that manage the transitions between voices and passages.

The result is a text that oscillates between passages of luminous spiritual coherence and passages of impressive but static display. The seven Ancient Word passages glow; the catalogues shimmer; the court material feels foreign; the bridges feel thin. Every reader has sensed this oscillation. No reader before Swedenborg had the diagnostic tool to explain it: the test of continuous causal movement, which separates narrative from display as cleanly as a geological stratum separates sedimentary layers.

### 2.3 Three Categories of Material

The analysis classifies every verse of the Song into one of three categories:

**Category A: Ancient Word narrative.** Passages that display continuous correspondential internal sense — spiritual states narrated in causal sequence. Approximately 42 verses across seven passages. These constitute the preserved Ancient Word material in the Song.

**Category B: Correspondential catalogue.** Passages that deploy valid correspondential vocabulary without narrative movement. Primarily the three body-description catalogues (4:1–11; 5:10–16; 7:1–9a) and the botanical inventories (4:13–14). Each individual correspondence within these catalogues is genuine — eyes as doves IS spiritual sight from good, teeth as shorn sheep IS truths of the natural man arranged in order. But the catalogue does not narrate. It lists correspondences as a dictionary lists definitions.

**Category C: Non-correspondential material.** Court poetry (Solomon's palanquin, Pharaoh's chariots), military imagery ("terrible as an army with banners"), editorial bridges and transitional formulas, and independent fragments from other sources (the "little sister" parable of 8:8–10, the vineyard saying of 8:11–12). This material may contain individual correspondential images (and frequently does), but it derives from the compiler's cultural milieu rather than from the Ancient Word tradition.

The boundary between categories B and C is occasionally blurred — certain passages contain both catalogued correspondences and non-correspondential material. Where this occurs, the analysis identifies which elements belong to which category. The boundary between categories A and B, however, is sharp: the test of continuous causal movement admits no gradation. Either a passage narrates in sequence, or it does not.

## 3. Swedenborg's Diagnosis: What He Said and What He Did Not Say

Emanuel Swedenborg's judgment on the Song of Solomon appears in two key passages: *Sacred Scripture* §§18–19 and *Athanasian Creed* §§3–4. The diagnosis is precise, and its precision has been widely misunderstood — both by Swedenborgians who treat it as a blanket dismissal and by critics who never read past the headline.

### 3.1 The Explicit Judgment

In *Sacred Scripture* §18, Swedenborg distinguishes between books of Scripture that possess a continuous internal sense and books that do not. The former constitute the Word proper — Genesis through Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, the two books of Samuel, the two books of Kings, the Psalms, the Prophets, and Revelation. The latter include the remaining canonical books — the historical works, the wisdom literature, and the epistles. The Song of Solomon falls into the second category. It does not, Swedenborg states, possess a continuous internal sense "from the first verse to the last" (*SS* §18). In *Athanasian Creed* §§3–4, the same determination is reiterated: the Song is not the Word in the sense that Genesis or Isaiah is the Word.

This is the judgment that later Swedenborgian tradition condensed into the formula "the Song of Solomon is not part of the Word." That formula is accurate as far as it goes. But it does not go far enough, because it omits what Swedenborg *also* said.

### 3.2 What the Diagnosis Does Not Say

Swedenborg does not say the Song of Solomon contains no spiritual content. He does not say its language is empty of correspondential meaning. He does not say its imagery is secular love poetry with no connection to spiritual realities. What he says is that the Song *as a whole composition* does not sustain a continuous internal sense — that the composition, taken beginning to end, does not narrate a spiritual biography in the way that the internal sense of Genesis narrates creation, fall, and regeneration across its continuous chapters.

The difference between "this text has no continuous internal sense as a whole" and "this text has no spiritual content at all" is the difference between saying "this building has no structural integrity" and "these bricks are made of mud." The bricks may be excellent. The building may not stand. The Song's individual correspondences may be genuine — and Swedenborg's own practice confirms that they are — while the overall composition fails to sustain the causal narrative that constitutes continuous internal sense.

### 3.3 Swedenborg's Own Practice

The evidence that Swedenborg recognized genuine correspondential content within the Song is found throughout his own writings. In the *Arcana Coelestia*, he repeatedly cites Song of Solomon verses when illustrating specific correspondences:

- **"A garden enclosed"** (4:12) appears in discussions of the interior rational mind as a cultivated space receiving influx (*AC* §§1588, 3220)
- **"Flowing streams from Lebanon"** (4:15) illustrates truths flowing from the celestial degree into the natural (*AC* §6435)
- **"The voice of the turtledove"** (2:12) exemplifies the perception of good in the natural man (*AC* §3219)
- **"Lilies"** and **"roses"** from the Song appear as correspondences for truths of the interior natural (*AC* §9553)
- **"Myrrh and frankincense"** (4:6) illustrate the good of faith in the external and internal man (*AC* §10177)

In each case, Swedenborg treats the correspondential image as genuine — as a real correspondence, grounded in function. He does not treat it as arbitrary metaphor or literary convention. He treats it exactly as he treats a correspondence found in Genesis or Isaiah: a natural image expressing a spiritual reality through organic, functional relationship.

This practice reveals Swedenborg's actual position: the Song of Solomon contains genuine correspondential vocabulary inherited from the Ancient Word tradition. Its individual images are valid correspondences. What it does not possess is the architectural coherence — the continuous causal movement from state to state — that would make it, as a whole, part of the Word.

### 3.4 The Ancient Word Connection

Swedenborg's writings on the Ancient Word provide the theoretical context for this judgment. In *Arcana Coelestia* §§2897, 3419, and 4279–80, he describes a pre-Mosaic scripture — the "Ancient Word" — that existed among the earliest post-diluvian people. This Word was written entirely in correspondences by perceivers who experienced spiritual realities directly and expressed them through the intimate, bodily register of the natural world. The Ancient Word was not an intellectual product but a perceptual record: its authors *saw* what they wrote, in the way that celestial angels see correspondences directly.

Swedenborg states that fragments of the Ancient Word survive in later texts. The opening chapters of Genesis are one such survival (*AC* §66). The book of Job is another possible repository. And the correspondential vocabulary that pervades the Song of Solomon — gardens, spices, fountains, vineyards, doves, mountains, cedars, flowing streams — is, on this reading, a third: fragments of a genuinely ancient correspondential language, preserved by transmission but no longer controlled by the perception that produced them.

The implication is clear. The Song's *compiler* — whoever assembled the text into its present form — had access to Ancient Word material. He recognized its beauty. He may even have recognized, dimly, its spiritual significance. But he no longer possessed the perceptual capacity that produced it. He could collect the words but not regenerate the grammar. He could display the vocabulary but not compose the sentences. The result is the Song of Solomon: a text with genuine Ancient Word fragments embedded in a compiled literary matrix.

### 3.5 How the Diagnosis Predicts the Compiled Structure

Swedenborg's judgment, properly understood, does not merely describe the Song's status — it predicts the Song's internal structure. If the Song as a whole lacks continuous internal sense but contains genuine correspondential imagery, then the text should exhibit a specific pattern: passages of continuous correspondential narrative (Ancient Word fragments) interspersed with passages of correspondential display without narrative movement (compiled material) and passages of non-correspondential cultural material (court poetry, editorial apparatus).

This is exactly what the stripping analysis reveals. Of the Song's 117 verses, approximately 42 form seven passages of continuous correspondential narrative — passages in which spiritual states unfold in causal sequence, in which rearrangement destroys the spiritual sense, in which the three-fold correspondential test is satisfied throughout. The remaining approximately 75 verses consist of catalogued correspondences without movement, court poetry celebrating Solomon and his bride, editorial bridges managing transitions between fragments, and independent sayings with no narrative context.

Swedenborg's 1763 diagnosis predicted a textual structure that could not be verified until the tools of correspondential analysis were systematically applied to the Song 260 years later. The prediction is confirmed.

## 4. The Ancient Word Layer: Seven States of Regeneration

When the Song of Solomon is subjected to the three-fold correspondential test — functional relationship, cross-context consistency, continuous causal sequence — seven passages emerge that satisfy all three criteria simultaneously. These passages, comprising approximately 42 of the Song's 117 verses, narrate the soul's regeneration from first awakening through permanent conjunction with the Divine. Each passage presents the biblical text, a correspondential reading, and a key table mapping natural objects to their spiritual correspondences. The complete removal table, documenting which verses were classified as compiled material and why, appears in the Appendix (§12).

The seven passages are presented in their biblical order, which is also their spiritual order. This is not a hermeneutic imposition: the causal sequence of the spiritual reading follows the textual sequence of the biblical order. The compiler preserved the fragments in the right order — he just filled the spaces between them with other material.

### 4.1 First Awakening: The End of Vastation (2:10–15)

> Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.
> For the winter is past, the rain is over and gone;
> the flowers appear on the earth;
> the time of the singing of birds is come,
> and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land;
> the fig tree putteth forth her green figs,
> and the vines with the tender grape give a good smell.
> Arise, my love, my fair one, and come away.
>
> O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock,
> in the secret places of the stairs,
> let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice;
> for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.
>
> Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines:
> for our vines have tender grapes.

**Spiritual reading.** The passage opens after vastation — a state of spiritual desolation in which no truth is perceived and no good is felt. This is not the primordial void but a *subsequent* emptiness: winter implies a season before winter, as the soul has known warmth and lost it. The call to "rise up" and "come away" is the Lord's first address to the soul emerging from desolation.

The signs of returning life accumulate in precise order, moving from the most external to the more interior. Flowers on the earth are first truths of good in the natural degree — things barely visible, just appearing. The singing of birds is rational truth from spiritual affection — truth that is felt, not merely known. The voice of the turtledove is the perception of good in faith — a deeper register, heard rather than seen. The fig tree putting forth green figs is natural good beginning to produce fruit. The vines with tender grape are spiritual good beginning to form. The sequence is exact: first signs appear; then rational perception awakens; then good begins to produce.

The dove hidden in the clefts of the rock is faith concealed in the literal sense of truth. The rock is the Lord's truth in its external form; the clefts are the openings in that external form through which interior truth can be glimpsed. The call to "let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice" is the Lord drawing faith out of concealment into expression — calling for the soul to show what it harbors.

The little foxes that spoil the vines are sensual falsities — the lowest, most external forms of falsity that attack spiritual good precisely when it is most tender. The command to "take us the foxes" is the insistence that these falsities be removed before the new growth can mature.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Winter | Spiritual desolation; vastation |
| Rain (past) | Temptation ended |
| Flowers on earth | First truths of good in the natural degree |
| Singing of birds | Rational truths from spiritual affection |
| Voice of the turtledove | Good of faith expressed in the natural man |
| Fig tree / green figs | Natural good producing |
| Vines / tender grape | Spiritual good forming |
| Dove in clefts of rock | Faith hidden in the literal sense of truth |
| Little foxes | Sensual falsities threatening spiritual growth |

### 4.2 Seeking by Night (3:1–5)

> By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth:
> I sought him, but I found him not.
> I will rise now, and go about the city
> in the streets and in the broad ways
> I will seek him whom my soul loveth:
> I sought him, but I found him not.
>
> The watchmen that go about the city found me:
> to whom I said, Saw ye him whom my soul loveth?
>
> It was but a little that I passed from them,
> but I found him whom my soul loveth:
> I held him, and would not let him go,
> until I had brought him into my mother's house,
> and into the chamber of her that conceived me.
>
> I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem,
> by the roes, and by the hinds of the field,
> that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.

**Spiritual reading.** The second state reverses the direction of the first. In State 1, the beloved called and the soul listened. In State 2, the soul actively seeks. It seeks on the bed by night — in the will's own darkness, without illumination from the understanding. The bed is the will in its resting state; night is spiritual obscurity; the combination describes a soul moved by love but lacking truth. This is why seeking fails: the will alone, without the understanding's light, cannot find what it loves.

The soul rises and searches through the doctrinal structures of the natural man — the city, the streets, the broad ways. These are the external church's apparatus: organized truths, systematic theology, congregational life. The watchmen who patrol the city are truths of doctrine in their protective function — they guard the structures but cannot themselves provide conjunction. The soul asks the watchmen, "Have you seen him whom my soul loves?" — the poignant question of a soul that has outgrown doctrinal instruction but does not yet know what comes next.

The finding is abrupt: "It was but a little that I passed from them, but I found him." Beyond doctrine, just past the watchmen, the Lord is present. The finding is not through intellectual effort but through love's own persistence. And the soul does not merely find — it *holds*: "I held him, and would not let him go." This is not casual contact but desperate conjunction, the grip of a soul that has searched in darkness and will not release what it has found.

The mother's house and the chamber of conception represent the inmost of the church — the origin of spiritual life, the womb from which the soul's spiritual existence was generated. Bringing the Lord there is bringing conjunction to the deepest possible location within the church.

The adjuration to the daughters of Jerusalem (affections of the external church) guards the process: do not awaken love before its time. Conjunction must arise from the soul's own longing, not from external stimulation.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Night | Spiritual obscurity |
| Bed | The will in its resting state |
| City, streets, broad ways | Doctrinal structures of the external church |
| Watchmen | Truths of doctrine in their protective function |
| Mother's house | Inmost of the church; origin of spiritual life |
| Chamber of conception | Where spiritual life is generated |
| Daughters of Jerusalem | Affections of the external church |
| Roes, hinds of the field | Natural affections of good and truth |

### 4.3 The Sealed Garden Opened (4:12, 15–16; 5:1)

> A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse;
> a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.
>
> A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters,
> and streams from Lebanon.
>
> Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south;
> blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out.
> Let my beloved come into his garden,
> and eat his pleasant fruits.
>
> I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse:
> I have gathered my myrrh with my spice;
> I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey;
> I have drunk my wine with my milk.
> Eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved.

**Spiritual reading.** The third state is the pivot of the cycle. The interior mind of the soul — the garden — has been enclosed: sealed against external approach. The spring is shut up; the fountain is sealed. Truths of good exist within but cannot flow outward. This is the critical condition that the seeking of State 2 has produced: the soul has brought the Lord to the mother's house, but the interior mind remains closed. Seeking has accomplished proximity but not yet influx.

The source is present: "a fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon." Lebanon is the rational mind — the white mountain of cedar, the rational faculty that mediates between the spiritual and the natural. Living waters are truths of good flowing from the Lord. The source is there; the channels are sealed.

The call for the winds is the decisive moment. North wind and south wind represent truth from every possible state: truth from obscurity and ignorance (north) and truth from illumination and warmth (south). The call is universal — let truth from ALL conditions blow upon the garden, so that what is sealed within may flow outward. This is not the soul selecting which truths to admit; it is the soul opening to every truth at once.

"Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits." The soul invites, and the Lord responds: "I am come into my garden." This is the moment of conjunction — the Lord enters the interior mind. What follows is a rapid sequence of reception at every degree. Myrrh with spice: natural good received with its truth. Honeycomb with honey: innocence-good appropriated in its fullness. Wine with milk: spiritual truth received together with natural innocence. The Lord receives — eats, drinks, gathers — at every level of the soul simultaneously.

The closing invitation — "Eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved" — extends the conjunction. What the soul has received is not private. The Lord, having entered one garden, invites all who can receive.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Garden enclosed | Interior mind sealed against external approach |
| Spring shut up, fountain sealed | Truths of good present but blocked from flowing |
| Living waters | Truths of good from the Lord |
| Streams from Lebanon | Truth flowing from the rational mind |
| North wind | Truth from states of obscurity |
| South wind | Truth from states of illumination |
| Spices | Truths of good flowing from the interior |
| Myrrh with spice | Natural good received with its truth |
| Honeycomb with honey | Innocence-good in its fullness |
| Wine with milk | Spiritual truth and natural innocence together |

### 4.4 The Alternation of States (5:2–8)

> I sleep, but my heart waketh:
> it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying,
> Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled:
> for my head is filled with dew,
> and my locks with the drops of the night.
>
> I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on?
> I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?
>
> My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door,
> and my bowels were moved for him.
> I rose up to open to my beloved;
> and my hands dropped with myrrh,
> and my fingers with sweet smelling myrrh,
> upon the handles of the lock.
>
> I opened to my beloved;
> but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone:
> my soul failed when he spake:
> I sought him, but I could not find him;
> I called him, but he gave me no answer.
>
> The watchmen that went about the city found me,
> they smote me, they wounded me;
> the keepers of the walls took away my veil from me.
>
> I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem,
> if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him,
> that I am sick of love.

**Spiritual reading.** This is the most complex passage in the Song and the one that most clearly marks the text as Ancient Word narrative rather than compiled poetry. No compiler would compose this sequence: the pattern of conjunction-followed-by-withdrawal is too counterintuitive, too spiritually precise, and too unflattering to be literary invention.

The opening — "I sleep, but my heart waketh" — describes the deepest state of interior receptivity. The natural mind is quiescent (sleep), but the will is awake (heart watches). In this state, the Lord comes with fullness: his head is filled with dew, his locks with the drops of the night. Dew is divine truth in its most delicate form, the truth that saturates the outermost levels of the soul — even the hair, which corresponds to the most external truths of the sensory mind. The Lord arrives saturated with truth at every degree.

The soul hesitates: "I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?" The coat is external truth; the feet are the natural degree of the mind. The soul has already stripped its externals — it is in a deep interior state and resists descending back into the natural to open the door. This hesitation is not laziness but an interior state so refined that return to externals feels like defilement.

The Lord does not wait for the door. "My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door, and my bowels were moved for him." The hand entering through the opening is good entering through whatever aperture the understanding provides. The bowels being moved are the interiors of the will being stirred — the deepest affections responding to the touch of good. The soul rises to open; its hands drop with myrrh; its fingers produce sweet-smelling myrrh on the handles of the lock. Natural good flows from the will into the ultimates of action.

Then the withdrawal: "I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone." This is the signature of genuine regeneration. The Lord gives presence and then withdraws — not from cruelty, not from fickleness, but so that the soul's longing deepens beyond what mere presence could produce. A soul that simply possesses the beloved has not been regenerated. A soul that possesses, loses, and then longs — that soul is being transformed.

The watchmen who guided the seeking soul in State 2 now wound it. The same truths of doctrine that were helpful at an earlier stage cause pain at a deeper one, because the soul has outgrown the need for doctrinal guidance and requires the beloved himself. Even the veil — the covering of external truth — is stripped away. The soul is left naked, wounded, and "sick of love." This is not defeat. It is the state from which the final, permanent conjunction becomes possible.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Sleep / heart waketh | Natural mind quiescent; will receptive |
| Dew, drops of night | Divine truth saturating the ultimate degree |
| Coat put off, feet washed | External states already stripped |
| Hand through the hole of the door | Good entering through the understanding |
| Bowels moved | Interiors of the will stirred |
| Hands dropping myrrh | Natural good produced by will's action |
| Beloved withdrawing | Alternation of states; presence withdrawn to deepen longing |
| Watchmen wounding | Doctrinal truths that cause pain at deeper states |
| Veil removed | Covering of external truth stripped |
| Sick of love | Deepest longing; prerequisite for permanent conjunction |

### 4.5 The Garden Inspected (7:10b–13)

> Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field;
> let us lodge in the villages.
> Let us get up early to the vineyards;
> let us see if the vine flourish,
> whether the tender grape appear,
> and the pomegranates bud forth:
> there will I give thee my loves.
>
> The mandrakes give a smell,
> and at our gates are all manner of pleasant fruits,
> new and old, which I have laid up for thee, O my beloved.

**Spiritual reading.** Between State 4 and State 5, the text makes a temporal leap that the spiritual reading explains. The wounding of State 4 — the withdrawal, the stripping, the sickness of love — was not the end. Something happened in the gap: the deeper longing bore fruit, the Lord worked in the depths where the text does not narrate, and conjunction was re-established on a deeper, permanent basis. State 5 opens in the quiet aftermath of that invisible work.

The soul and the Lord now survey together what has been cultivated. The field is the church in its breadth — the whole extent of the soul's spiritual life. The villages are doctrinal goods in the natural degree — specific, located, inhabited truths that have settled into the daily life of the soul. The vineyards are the spiritual goods of the church: the places where truth grows into good through cultivation.

The inspection is specific. Has the vine flourished? — truth growing into good. Has the tender grape appeared? — spiritual good forming from truth. Have the pomegranates budded forth? — the goods of the church assembling, the collected fruits of regeneration beginning to ripen. The soul is not asking abstract questions. It is walking through its own interior landscape with the Lord, checking what has grown.

The mandrakes give their smell — conjugial love manifests in the natural degree. At the gates — the places where goods enter the soul from without and flow outward from within — all manner of pleasant fruits are present: new goods from regeneration and old goods (hereditary good reformed and purified) alike. The soul presents these to the Lord: "I have laid them up for thee, O my beloved." The goods are not claimed as the soul's own possession. They are presented.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Field | The church in its breadth |
| Villages | Doctrinal goods in the natural degree |
| Vineyards | Spiritual goods of the church |
| Vine flourishing | Truth growing into good |
| Tender grape appearing | Spiritual good forming |
| Pomegranates budding | Goods of the church assembling |
| Mandrakes | Conjugial love in the natural degree |
| Gates | Where goods enter and flow |
| Fruits, new and old | Goods from regeneration and hereditary good purified |

### 4.6 The Mother's House Completed (8:1–4)

> O that thou wert as my brother,
> that sucked the breasts of my mother!
> when I should find thee without, I would kiss thee;
> yea, I should not be despised.
>
> I would lead thee, and bring thee into my mother's house,
> who would instruct me:
> I would cause thee to drink of spiced wine
> of the juice of my pomegranate.
>
> His left hand should be under my head,
> and his right hand should embrace me.
>
> I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem,
> that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.

**Spiritual reading.** This passage completes the arc that began in State 2 (3:1–5). In the earlier passage, the soul brought the Lord to the mother's house after desperate seeking in darkness. It held him and would not let go — the grip of a soul that fears loss. Here, the grip has relaxed into intimacy. The Lord is received not as a stranger captured in the night but as a brother — one nourished from the same source, sharing the same mother's milk.

The wish — "O that thou wert as my brother" — is the soul's desire for the Lord to be received naturally, without concealment, without shame. "When I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; yea, I should not be despised." The kiss in public — conjunction expressed openly — is the state in which interior good and exterior life are no longer in tension. The soul can express its love for the Lord without needing to hide it within doctrinal structures or interior withdrawal.

The mother's house returns: the church, the source of spiritual generation. But now it is a place of instruction — "who would instruct me" — not merely a destination for captured treasure. The soul offers the Lord what it has cultivated: spiced wine (truth of doctrine enriched with the good of life) and the juice of its pomegranate (the specific goods produced by this soul's church, offered back to their source). What was received in State 3 is now returned, transformed by cultivation.

The left hand under the head is good supporting the understanding — the will sustaining the intellect. The right hand embracing is truth encompassing the will — truth that holds the will in its rightful orientation. The complete integration of divine good and truth with the human faculties of will and understanding is described in a single image of bodily rest.

The adjuration closes the cycle for the third and final time: do not force what must arise from love's own timing.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Brother from same mother | The Lord sharing the same source of good |
| Kiss without shame | Conjunction openly expressed |
| Mother's house | The church as place of instruction |
| Spiced wine | Truth of doctrine enriched with good |
| Juice of pomegranate | Goods produced by the church, offered back |
| Left hand under head | Good supporting the understanding |
| Right hand embracing | Truth encompassing the will |

### 4.7 The Seal and the Flame (8:6–7)

> Set me as a seal upon thine heart,
> as a seal upon thine arm:
> for love is strong as death;
> jealousy is cruel as the grave:
> the coals thereof are coals of fire,
> which hath a most vehement flame.
>
> Many waters cannot quench love,
> neither can the floods drown it:
> if a man would give all the substance of his house for love,
> it would utterly be contemned.

**Spiritual reading.** The final passage is not narrative but declarative. The cycle of regeneration is complete; what remains is to seal it permanently and to state, in the most concentrated correspondential language in the Song, what has been accomplished.

"Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm." The seal on the heart is good permanently imprinted on the will. The seal on the arm is truth permanently imprinted on the power to act. Heart and arm are not arbitrarily chosen: the heart IS the will in correspondence; the arm IS the power of truth executing what the will desires. The image is precise: what has been received through the entire regeneration cycle is now permanently inscribed in the soul's two fundamental faculties.

Then the three declarations that constitute the doctrinal climax:

**"Love is strong as death."** Divine love can reach the soul that has died spiritually. Death, in the correspondential sense, is not biological termination but the death of the natural man — the cessation of the self's resistance. Love is stronger than this death because it can penetrate even the self's final defense and regenerate what lies beneath.

**"Jealousy is cruel as the grave; the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame."** Divine zeal — the intensity of divine love directed toward the soul in bondage — is relentless. "Cruel as the grave" is not punishment but the fire of love that will not leave the beloved in death. The coals are celestial love in its inmost form: the most vehement flame of the divine will directed toward recovery.

**"Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it."** Waters are falsities; floods are evils in their totality. No quantity of falsity and no aggregate of evil can extinguish what has been sealed. This is the declaration of invulnerability — the state that the entire regeneration cycle produces.

**"If a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned."** The final declaration closes the door against the self's fundamental strategy: to acquire spiritual goods as possessions. Love cannot be purchased. It cannot be earned by merit, bartered for in exchange, or manufactured by will-power. If a man offered everything the self owns — "all the substance of his house" — the offer would be rejected with contempt. Love is received or it is not. The self cannot produce it.

Together these four declarations describe a state that is permanent, invulnerable, and non-transactional — a state produced not by the soul's effort but by the entire process of seeking, finding, losing, longing, being wounded, growing, offering, and finally being sealed. The seal does not reward the process. It is the process's natural consequence — as fruit is the natural consequence of cultivation.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural Object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Seal on heart | Good permanently imprinted on the will |
| Seal on arm | Truth permanently imprinted on the power to act |
| Love strong as death | Divine love penetrating even spiritual death |
| Jealousy cruel as the grave | Divine zeal that will not leave the beloved in death |
| Coals of fire | Celestial love in its inmost form |
| Many waters | All falsities |
| Floods | All evils in their totality |
| Substance of the house | Everything the self possesses |

## 5. The Compiled Layer: Three Bodies of Material

The approximately 75 verses that do not form part of the seven Ancient Word passages are not waste material. They are evidence — evidence of who the compiler was, what sources he had, and what he understood and failed to understand. This section examines the compiled material in three categories: the body-description catalogues, the court and military poetry, and the editorial bridges and fragments. Each category reveals something different about the compiler's relationship to the correspondential tradition he was working within.

### 5.1 The Body-Description Catalogues (4:1–11; 5:10–16; 7:1–9a)

The three body-description catalogues are the most revealing material in the Song for understanding the compiler's position. Each catalogue lists body parts from head to foot (4:1–11 and 5:10–16) or foot to head (7:1–9a), assigning to each a natural comparison. The comparisons are extraordinarily specific: eyes compared to doves, hair to a flock of goats descending from Gilead, teeth to shorn sheep coming up from the washing, temples to a piece of pomegranate, neck to the tower of David hung with shields, breasts to twin fawns of a gazelle feeding among lilies.

These comparisons make no aesthetic sense. No poet writing love poetry would compare a woman's hair to goats descending a mountain, her teeth to newly shorn sheep, or her neck to a military tower hung with shields. The images are neither flattering nor erotic nor beautiful in any conventional literary sense. Attempts to explain them as "ancient Near Eastern beauty standards" or "pastoral charm" are strained — they are strained because the images were never meant as aesthetic description. They are correspondential vocabulary.

Consider each major item in the first catalogue (4:1–11) as correspondence:

| Verse | Natural Image | Correspondential Reading |
|---|---|---|
| 4:1a | Eyes as doves behind a veil | Spiritual sight from good (doves), working through the exterior form of truth (veil) |
| 4:1b | Hair as a flock of goats from Gilead | Natural good in its outermost form (hair), descending from celestial good (Gilead) |
| 4:2 | Teeth as shorn sheep from washing | Truths of the natural man (teeth), purified (shorn, washed), paired and producing (none barren) |
| 4:3a | Lips like a thread of scarlet | Speech from celestial good (scarlet = celestial love) |
| 4:3b | Temples like a piece of pomegranate | The natural will (temples) as a portion of the church's collected good (pomegranate) |
| 4:4 | Neck like the tower of David with shields | The connection between interior and exterior (neck) fortified with defenses of truth (shields) |
| 4:5 | Breasts as twin fawns among lilies | The good and truth of the natural man (breasts, as nourishing), gentle and innocent (fawns), growing among truths (lilies) |
| 4:6 | Mountain of myrrh, hill of frankincense | Natural good elevated (mountain of myrrh) and interior good elevated (hill of frankincense) |
| 4:10–11 | Lips dropping honeycomb; honey and milk under the tongue; garments as Lebanon | Innocence-good in speech (honeycomb lips); truth and innocence internalized (under the tongue); external truths as the rational mind's covering (garments as Lebanon) |

Every correspondence in this table is valid. Each is grounded in the function of the natural object. Each is consistent with the same correspondences as used in the Ancient Word passages and throughout the Swedenborgian apparatus. The compiler did not invent these comparisons — he inherited them from a tradition that understood what they meant.

But the catalogue does not *move*. The sequence from eyes to hair to teeth to lips to temples to neck to breasts is anatomical, not spiritual. There is no causal relationship between one item and the next. The soul does not progress from seeing to growing to purifying to speaking — the states are listed, not narrated. The compiler arranged his valid correspondences in the order of the body, not in the order of spiritual causation. He used the dictionary without composing a sentence.

The second catalogue (5:10–16) does the same for the beloved. Again, every comparison is correspondentially valid: the head as finest gold (celestial good in its highest form), locks bushy and black as a raven (natural truth in its sensory form), eyes as doves by rivers of waters (spiritual sight flowing with truth), cheeks as beds of spices (the interiors of the will as cultivated good), hands as gold rings set with beryl (power to act from celestial good), belly as bright ivory overlaid with sapphires (the interior will as truth's display), legs as pillars of marble on sockets of gold (the power to stand from truth grounded in good). As before: each item valid, the sequence anatomical, the movement absent.

The third catalogue (7:1–9a) inverts the order, starting from the feet and moving upward: feet in sandals, joints of thighs like jewels, navel like a round goblet, belly as a heap of wheat set about with lilies, breasts as twin fawns (repeated from 4:5), neck as a tower of ivory, eyes like fishpools in Heshbon, nose like the tower of Lebanon, head crowned like Carmel. The inversion confirms the catalogue's character as inventory: a compiler organizing the same material from a different angle does not discover new spiritual movement. He simply turns the list upside down.

The catalogues are the compiler's most characteristic product. They demonstrate that he possessed genuine correspondential vocabulary — not a few images but a comprehensive body-correspondence system covering every major anatomical feature. This vocabulary is too systematic and too internally consistent to be literary invention. It derives from the Ancient Word tradition: a system in which the human body was understood as the Divine Human in ultimates, each organ corresponding to a specific spiritual faculty. The compiler had access to this system. He could not compose with it.

### 5.2 The Court Poetry and Military Imagery (1:4–6, 9; 3:6–11; 6:4–12)

The court material introduces a register entirely foreign to the Ancient Word passages. Where the Ancient Word material is intimate, interior, and addressed from soul to Lord, the court material is public, political, and addressed to a royal figure.

**Song 1:9** — "I have compared thee, O my love, to a company of horses in Pharaoh's chariots." This is a fragment from a teaching about the intellectual faculty of the Israelite church in relation to the intellectual faculty of Egypt. Horses correspond to the understanding's grasp of truth; Pharaoh's chariots represent Egypt's rational faculty — the science of correspondences as understood by the Egyptian priesthood. The comparison is not unflattering but specific: the beloved's intellectual power is compared to the finest intellectual tradition of the ancient Near East. This fragment belongs to a different teaching entirely — one about the church's cognitive capacity, not about the soul's conjugial union with the Lord.

**Song 3:6–11** — Solomon's palanquin. This passage operates in a completely different register: 

> Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all powders of the merchant? Behold his bed, which is Solomon's; threescore valiant men are about it, of the valiant of Israel. They all hold swords, being expert in war: every man hath his sword upon his thigh because of fear in the night. King Solomon made himself a chariot of the wood of Lebanon. He made the pillars thereof of silver, the bottom thereof of gold, the covering of it of purple, the midst thereof being paved with love, for the daughters of Jerusalem. Go forth, O ye daughters of Zion, and behold king Solomon with the crown wherewith his mother crowned him in the day of his espousals, and in the day of the gladness of his heart.

This is royal processional poetry. The sixty warriors with swords, the military preparedness, the silver and gold construction, the public summons to the daughters of Zion — none of this connects to the intimate, interior register of the Ancient Word passages. The palanquin passage celebrates Solomon's political-religious significance. It may contain individual correspondential images (wood of Lebanon, pillars of silver, gold, purple), but the overall movement is public display, not spiritual narrative. The compiler included it because it mentioned Solomon and because it contained a nuptial reference; he did not recognize that its register was incompatible with the passages surrounding it.

**Song 6:4–12** — The most disordered section in the Song. It opens with a comparison to Tirzah and Jerusalem (political capitals), declares the beloved "terrible as an army with banners" (military metaphor), recycles 4:1–2 almost verbatim (hair as goats, teeth as sheep), introduces Solomon's harem as context ("sixty queens, eighty concubines, virgins without number"), and collapses at verse 12 into a textual corruption so severe that no translator or commentator has produced a coherent reading. The section is a patchwork: political comparison, military imagery, recycled catalogue material, harem context, and textual ruin. Nothing moves. The compiler assembled fragments from multiple sources and could not make them cohere.

The court material's primary function in the Song is to give the text a Solomonic framing. By inserting royal processional poetry and harem references, the compiler anchored the text in a specific historical-literary context — the court of Solomon — that his audience would recognize as prestigious and authoritative. This framing has nothing to do with the spiritual content of the Ancient Word passages. It is literary packaging.

### 5.3 The Editorial Bridges, Fragments, and Threshold Material

The third category comprises the compiler's own connective tissue: the material he wrote or selected to knit his sources together into a continuous text.

**The threshold material (1:2–8, 10–17; 2:1–7).** The Song opens with a sequence of images that approaches the Ancient Word register without achieving continuous narrative. "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for thy love is better than wine" (1:2) is a valid correspondential opening: the kiss is conjunction of good and truth; wine is spiritual truth; the statement that love exceeds truth is doctrinally precise. "Thy name is as ointment poured forth" (1:3) correctly identifies the Lord's quality (name) as good that flows and heals (ointment). "Draw me, we will run after thee" (1:4a) expresses the soul's desire for influx and its willingness to follow.

But the sequence does not sustain. By 1:5, the speaker has shifted to a court setting — "I am black but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon" — introducing a social-apologetic tone (defending her complexion to court women) that breaks the correspondential register. By 1:7–8, the text has become a pastoral dialogue about feeding flocks at noon, with no discernible spiritual narrative. The threshold material approaches the Ancient Word, touches it, draws back, and oscillates between registers. The compiler had fragments that led toward the Ancient Word passages but could not sustain the trajectory.

**The Rose of Sharon material (2:1–7).** "I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys" (2:1) is beautiful and correspondentially valid: the rose is the good of the celestial church, the lily is truth of the interior natural. But the passage immediately shifts to comparison — "As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters" (2:2), "As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my beloved among the sons" (2:3) — and then to a sequence of declarative statements about the banqueting house, the banner of love, sustaining with flagons and comforting with apples, the left hand under the head and right hand embracing (2:4–6), and a third version of the adjuration (2:7). This material builds toward but does not achieve continuous narrative. Its character is preparatory — it reads as if the compiler, knowing that the First Awakening passage (2:10–15) was coming, assembled an introduction from fragments that would lead the reader to the right emotional register.

**The "mountains of Bether" (2:16–17).** These verses immediately follow the First Awakening passage and seem to continue its theme: "My beloved is mine, and I am his: he feedeth among the lilies. Until the day break, and the shadows flee away, turn, my beloved, and be thou like a roe or a young hart upon the mountains of Bether." But "Bether" means "division" or "separation" — the mountains of separation. The correspondential reading is that conjunction is interrupted; the soul and the beloved are parted at the mountains of division. This directly contradicts the awakening just accomplished in 2:10–15. The compiler placed a separation formula immediately after an awakening passage, breaking the spiritual continuity. He may have done so because the formula was in his source collection and he thought it fit the theme of love poetry. Correspondentially, it works against the passage it follows.

**Bridge questions (5:9; 6:1–3).** Between the Alternation of States passage (5:2–8) and the second body catalogue (5:10–16), the compiler inserted a forced transition: "What is thy beloved more than another beloved, O thou fairest among women? What is thy beloved more than another beloved, that thou dost so charge us?" (5:9). This question creates a social context — an audience asking for description — that motivates the catalogue that follows. It is a literary device, not a correspondential element. Similarly, 6:1–3 inserts a question ("Whither is thy beloved gone?") after the second catalogue, with a decorative answer ("My beloved is gone down into his garden... I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine") that circles without advancing.

**The repeated formula (8:5).** "Who is this that cometh up from the wilderness, leaning upon her beloved?" recycles the opening of the palanquin passage (3:6). The compiler used the same formula twice to introduce different contexts — a sign that he was working with a limited repertoire of transitional devices.

**The "little sister" parable (8:8–10).** This is material from a completely different source — a family deliberation about a young girl's future: "We have a little sister, and she hath no breasts: what shall we do for our sister in the day when she shall be spoken for?" The passage may contain correspondential content (the "wall" and the "door" as symbols, the sister as a church not yet mature), but it belongs to a wisdom tradition rather than to the Ancient Word's regeneration narrative.

**The vineyard saying (8:11–12).** "Solomon had a vineyard at Baal-hamon; he let out the vineyard unto keepers; every one for the fruit thereof was to bring a thousand pieces of silver. My vineyard, which is mine, is before me: thou, O Solomon, must have a thousand, and those that keep the fruit thereof two hundred." This is a self-contained wisdom parable about the institutional versus the interior church: Solomon's vineyard (the external church) is farmed out to hired keepers, while the speaker's vineyard (the interior church) is cultivated directly. It is a significant saying — possibly correspondential, possibly from the same tradition that produced the Ancient Word passages — but it is disconnected from the surrounding text and cannot be integrated into the seven-state narrative.

**The conventional closing (8:13–14).** The Song ends with a brief dialogue: "Thou that dwellest in the gardens, the companions hearken to thy voice: cause me to hear it. Make haste, my beloved, and be thou like to a roe or a young hart upon the mountains of spices." This is a conventional literary close — a final wish, a call for haste, an echo of earlier imagery. It has no narrative movement and adds nothing to the spiritual architecture. The compiler needed an ending; this is what he supplied.

## 6. The Correspondential Vocabulary: What the Catalogues Reveal

The body-description catalogues analyzed in §5.1 are more than compiled poetry. They are the fossil record of a correspondential knowledge system — a system in which the human body was understood as a map of the Divine Human, each organ and feature corresponding to a specific spiritual faculty. The compiler did not invent this system. He inherited it. The catalogues are his inventory of what he received.

### 6.1 A Comprehensive Body-Correspondence System

When the three catalogues are assembled together, they cover the entire human form:

The coverage is comprehensive — from the head (celestial good) to the feet (the natural degree), from the hair (outermost natural) to the belly (innermost will). Every major anatomical feature receives a correspondential assignment. The system has internal consistency: features associated with the head correspond to the highest spiritual faculties; features associated with the extremities correspond to the most external and ultimate; features associated with the torso correspond to the will and its interiors.

This system closely parallels the "365 angels" body-correspondence passage in the *Apocryphon of John* (NHL II,1), where each body part is assigned to a specific archon or power — a correspondential map of the human form expressing the same principle: the body IS the spiritual reality in its ultimate, most external form. The Nag Hammadi text and the Song's catalogues are independent witnesses to the same correspondential tradition: the doctrine of the Divine Human, in which the human form is the form of heaven and each organ corresponds to a function of the Grand Man.

### 6.2 Vocabulary Without Grammar

The catalogues demonstrate that the compiler possessed this vocabulary in its entirety. He knew that eyes correspond to doves, that hair corresponds to goats descending, that teeth correspond to shorn sheep. His comparisons are not arbitrary — they follow the logic of functional correspondence with the same precision found in the Ancient Word passages.

But knowing the vocabulary is not the same as knowing the grammar. The grammar of correspondential narrative is causal sequence: states producing states, each spiritual condition flowing necessarily into the next. The seven Ancient Word passages speak this grammar — vastation produces awakening, which produces seeking, which produces conjunction, which produces withdrawal, which produces deepened longing, which produces permanent sealing. Each state is causally necessary to the next; removing or rearranging any one destroys the logic.

The catalogues speak the vocabulary without the grammar. "Eyes as doves, hair as goats, teeth as shorn sheep, lips as scarlet, neck as a tower" — these are correspondences listed, not correspondences narrated. The sequence is anatomical (head to foot), not spiritual (state to state). Rearranging the items changes nothing about the spiritual content: listing the breasts before the eyes, or the neck before the teeth, neither adds nor subtracts meaning. This is the test of display versus narrative, and the catalogues fail it definitively.

What this tells us about the compiler is precise: he was a *keeper* of correspondential knowledge, not a *perceiver* of correspondential reality. He possessed the tradition's vocabulary through transmission — through written or oral inheritance — but he did not possess the perception that generated the vocabulary. The original perceivers saw that eyes and doves shared the same spiritual function; the compiler *knew* that they were paired. The difference between seeing a correspondence and knowing it had been stated is the difference between the Ancient Word's authors and its compiler.

### 6.3 Evidence for the Ancient Word Tradition

The catalogues constitute independent evidence for the existence of the Ancient Word tradition as Swedenborg described it. The argument proceeds as follows:

1. The body-correspondence system in the Song's catalogues is too comprehensive and too internally consistent to be literary invention by a single poet or school.
2. The system matches the correspondential assignments found independently in the Ancient Word passages of the Song, in Swedenborg's *Arcana Coelestia*, in the *Apocryphon of John*, and in Manichaean divine-human anatomical texts — traditions that had no literary contact with one another.
3. The system is grounded in functional correspondence (not arbitrary assignment), which means it cannot have been independently generated by multiple cultures through coincidence. Functional correspondence is discovered, not invented; but the same discovery by independent cultures implies a common source.
4. That common source is the correspondential knowledge tradition — the Ancient Word — that Swedenborg described as existing among the earliest post-diluvian peoples and surviving in fragments across the ancient Near East and Central Asia.

The catalogues do not prove the existence of the Ancient Word. But they provide exactly the kind of evidence that the Ancient Word hypothesis predicts: a comprehensive, internally consistent, functionally grounded body-correspondence vocabulary, preserved in a text whose compiler could not compose with it but faithfully inherited and transmitted it.

## 7. The Internal Logic: One Regeneration Cycle in Seven States

The seven passages presented in §4 are not independent poems. They are one narrative in seven movements, and the movements follow a causal sequence that rearrangement would destroy. This section demonstrates the logical necessity of the sequence — why each state must follow the one before it.

### 7.1 The Causal Chain

**State 1 → State 2: From Awakening to Seeking.** The soul cannot seek what it does not know exists. The signs of returning life in the First Awakening — flowers, birdsong, green figs, tender grapes — create the knowledge that the beloved is near. This knowledge *produces* the active seeking of State 2. Without awakening, the soul has no motive to seek. Without the signs that life has returned, the search would have no object. Awakening must precede seeking.

**State 2 → State 3: From Seeking to Conjunction.** The sealed garden cannot be opened by force. The seals are broken by the longing of a soul that has searched through darkness, through doctrinal structures, and beyond them. The seeking of State 2 — through the city at night, past the watchmen, into the mother's house — produces the interior readiness that allows the garden to be opened in State 3. Without searching, there is no longing. Without longing, the garden remains sealed.

**State 3 → State 4: From Conjunction to Withdrawal.** This is the most counterintuitive transition and the signature of genuine regeneration narrative. Conjunction, once accomplished, is immediately followed by withdrawal. No compiler would invent this sequence — it contradicts every literary expectation. The beloved enters the garden, receives good at every degree, and then departs. Why? Because conjunction that remains permanent from the first moment would not transform. The soul would rest on an experience rather than being changed by it. The Lord withdraws so that longing deepens beyond what presence alone could produce. Without conjunction, there is nothing to withdraw. Without withdrawal, there is no deepening.

**State 4 → State 5: From Withdrawal to Inspection.** Between these states, the text makes a temporal leap. The wounding and stripping of State 4 give way, without narrated transition, to the quiet survey of State 5. Something happened in the gap: the deeper longing bore fruit. The Lord worked in the depths — where the text is silent — and conjunction was re-established on a permanent basis. State 5 presupposes the work that State 4 initiated. Without the wound, there is no deeper work. Without the deeper work, there is nothing to inspect.

**State 5 → State 6: From Inspection to Completion.** Having surveyed the garden and found it fruitful, the soul receives the Lord as family — as brother, not stranger. The desperate grip of State 2 ("I held him, and would not let him go") has relaxed into natural intimacy ("O that thou wert as my brother... I would kiss thee; yea, I should not be despised"). Without the evidence of cultivation — the vine flourishing, the tender grape appearing, the pomegranates budding — the soul could not receive the Lord with this openness. Inspection confirms what growth has accomplished; completion is the natural consequence.

**State 6 → State 7: From Completion to Permanence.** The final passage seals what the cycle has produced. The seal on the heart and the seal on the arm make permanent what was accomplished through the whole process. The three declarations — love stronger than death, unquenchable by waters, unconcealing of the self's purchase — close every avenue by which the conjunction could be undone. Without the full process of seeking, finding, losing, longing, growing, and offering, there is nothing to seal.

### 7.2 The Rearrangement Test

The logical necessity of the sequence can be demonstrated negatively: rearranging the states destroys the spiritual sense.

If State 4 (withdrawal) preceded State 3 (conjunction), the Lord would withdraw before entering — absence before presence, which narrates nothing. If State 7 (seal) preceded State 4 (withdrawal), love would be declared invulnerable before the soul had experienced vulnerability — permanence before the process that produces permanence, which is the self's fantasy of acquiring spiritual goods without transformation. If State 5 (inspection) preceded State 2 (seeking), the garden would be surveyed before it was planted. If State 1 (awakening) and State 2 (seeking) were transposed, the soul would actively search before knowing that the beloved existed.

No rearrangement produces a coherent spiritual narrative. The states are not interchangeable units that a compiler could shuffle. They are a causally necessary sequence in which each stage creates the conditions for the next. This is the difference between narrative and display — and it is the difference between the seven Ancient Word passages and the body catalogues that surround them.

### 7.3 The Cycle as Regeneration

The seven-state cycle describes a complete regeneration — the full arc of the soul's transformation as Swedenborg maps it across the *Arcana Coelestia*. The correspondence between the Song's cycle and the Swedenborgian account of regeneration is structural:

| Song State | Regeneration Stage | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Awakening | End of vastation | The soul emerges from desolation; first truths appear |
| 2. Seeking | Active reformation | The soul searches through natural knowledge and doctrine |
| 3. Conjunction | First regeneration | Interior mind opens; the Lord is received |
| 4. Withdrawal | Temptation and alternation | Presence withdrawn to deepen reception |
| 5. Inspection | Spiritual growth confirmed | The fruits of regeneration are surveyed |
| 6. Completion | Full conjunction | The Lord received as brother; integration accomplished |
| 7. Permanence | Sealed state | Good and truth permanently imprinted; love invulnerable |

This is not a loose parallel. The Song's cycle and the Swedenborgian regeneration process describe the same events in the same order with the same causal logic. The Song describes it from inside — what it feels like to the soul undergoing the process. Swedenborg describes it from outside — the theological mechanics of what is happening. Both texts confirm that the process is sequential, irreversible, and complete: beginning in desolation, ending in permanent conjunction, with the critical pivot at withdrawal (State 4) where the process deepens beyond what first conjunction could achieve.

## 8. The Interpretive History: Four Schools and a Missing Key

The Song of Solomon has generated more interpretive diversity than any other book in the biblical canon. This is not a failure of scholarship but a diagnostic: a text that provokes radically different readings from sober scholars is a text that is communicating on a level that none of the readings have fully accessed. Each major interpretive tradition has grasped something real about the Song. None has grasped the whole. The correspondential reading explains both why each tradition partially succeeds and why each ultimately fails.

### 8.1 The Rabbinic-Allegorical Tradition

The earliest sustained interpretation of the Song comes from rabbinic Judaism. Rabbi Akiva (c. 50–135 CE) famously declared that "the whole world is not worth the day on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel. For all the Writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies" (*Mishnah Yadayim* 3:5). For Akiva, the Song narrated the love between God and Israel — a corporate, covenantal allegory in which the beloved is God and the bride is the nation.

Rabbinic tradition read the Song's episodes as historical allegory: the Exodus, the giving of Torah at Sinai, the wilderness wandering, the building of the Temple, the exile and eventual restoration. The *Targum* to the Song (dated variously to the 7th–9th centuries CE but reflecting earlier traditions) provides an elaborate historical-allegorical commentary: the opening verses refer to the Exodus; the sealed garden is the tent of meeting; the watchmen are the prophets; the vineyard is the nation of Israel.

**What this tradition grasps correctly:** The Song has an interior meaning that is not reducible to its literal surface. The relationship described is between the divine and the human. The imagery is not secular erotic poetry. These are all true.

**Where this tradition fails:** Allegory is arbitrary. There is no functional relationship between the imagery and the historical events it is said to represent. Why should "the winter is past" mean "the Exodus happened"? Why should "the sealed garden" mean "the tent of meeting"? The connections depend on the interpreter's associative facility, not on the intrinsic nature of the images. Different rabbis produced different allegorical mappings, and no criterion existed for choosing between them. Allegory requires a codebook; correspondence is grounded in the function of the natural object itself. The rabbinic tradition sensed the interior meaning but could not read it, because it lacked the key: the doctrine of correspondences.

### 8.2 The Christian-Typological Tradition

The Church Fathers adopted and transformed the rabbinic reading. Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–254 CE) produced the first systematic Christian commentary on the Song, reading it as the love between Christ and the Church — or, in his more developed reading, between the Divine Word and the individual soul. Origen's commentary is remarkable for its spiritual depth: he recognized that the Song described stages of the soul's ascent toward God, that the imagery expressed interior states, and that the narrative was sequential — earlier passages described earlier stages of the spiritual life.

Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153) produced eighty-six sermons on the Song, making it the central text of medieval mystical theology. Bernard read the kiss (1:2) as the soul's reception of the Holy Spirit, the sealed garden as the contemplative life, and the wound of 5:2–8 as the *vulneratio amoris* — the wound of divine love that transforms. His reading of the withdrawal motif was especially perceptive: he recognized that the Lord's departure after conjunction was spiritually purposeful, not accidental.

**What this tradition grasps correctly:** The Song describes the soul's relationship with the Divine. The narrative is sequential — stages proceed in order. The withdrawal (State 4) is spiritually meaningful. The intimacy is real, not allegorical shorthand for political covenant. The Christian mystical tradition came closer than any other pre-Swedenborgian school to reading correspondential narrative.

**Where this tradition fails:** Like the rabbinic tradition, the Christian reading ultimately depends on typological assignment rather than functional correspondence. Christ is "the bridegroom" because theology declares it, not because the text's imagery functionally generates the identification. The soul is "the bride" by doctrinal designation, not by correspondential necessity. When the text says "thy hair is as a flock of goats from Gilead," the typological tradition has nothing to say — it cannot connect the specific image to a specific spiritual reality. It can read the broad narrative (soul seeks God, finds, loses, seeks again) but not the detailed imagery. The correspondences are invisible to it.

### 8.3 The Modern Literal-Erotic Reading

The Enlightenment produced a radical break. Historical-critical scholarship rejected the allegorical tradition entirely and read the Song as what its surface appeared to be: ancient Near Eastern love poetry. J.G. Herder (1778) first proposed that the Song was simply a collection of love songs, and this reading became dominant in 19th- and 20th-century scholarship. The Song celebrates human sexual love. Its imagery is erotic. Its purpose is aesthetic.

More recent scholarship has refined this reading. Marvin Pope's *Anchor Bible Commentary* (1977) provided an exhaustive comparison of the Song's imagery with Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Ugaritic love poetry, arguing that the Song belongs to a pan-Near Eastern tradition of erotic verse. Ariel and Chana Bloch's translation (1995) presented the Song as a masterpiece of love poetry, its imagery vivid and sensual and requiring no allegorical superstructure.

**What this tradition grasps correctly:** The literal surface of the Song is genuinely intimate. The imagery describes bodies, desire, longing, and union in language that is physically specific. The text is not a coded message in which "kiss" secretly means "prayer" and "garden" secretly means "temple." The correspondential reading agrees with this entirely: correspondence is not allegory. The natural sense is real. The garden IS a garden, perceived at the natural degree. It is *also* the interior mind, perceived at the spiritual degree. The literal reading is correct about the natural sense; it simply does not see the spiritual sense that the natural sense expresses.

**Where this tradition fails:** The literal-erotic reading cannot explain the anomalies. Why do the body catalogues compare a woman's hair to goats and her teeth to sheep? Why does the beloved withdraw after entering the garden? Why are watchmen introduced in a love poem? Why does the text oscillate between intimate lyric and military imagery? Why is the Song composed of fragments that resist narrative coherence? If the Song is erotic poetry, it is strangely bad erotic poetry — its imagery is bizarre, its narrative is incoherent, and its tone shifts without apparent reason. The literal reading correctly identifies the surface register but cannot account for the text's internal structure, because the structure is correspondential, not literary.

### 8.4 The Cultic and Dramatic Hypotheses

Two additional approaches deserve mention. T.J. Meek (1924) and S.N. Kramer (1969) proposed that the Song preserves fragments of a Mesopotamian sacred marriage liturgy — the *hieros gamos* between Tammuz/Dumuzi and Ishtar/Inanna. On this reading, the Song's imagery derives from the cultic enactment of the divine marriage, and its seasonal references (winter past, spring arrived, garden opened) reflect the agricultural cycle associated with the Tammuz cult.

Separately, J.G. Ewald (1826) and others proposed a dramatic reading: the Song is a play with three characters (Solomon, the Shulamite, and a shepherd lover), in which the girl's loyalty to the shepherd lover resists Solomon's advances. This reading attempts to explain the Song's alternation between male and female voices and its dramatic reversals.

**What these traditions grasp correctly:** The cultic hypothesis correctly recognizes that the Song's imagery has ancient Near Eastern precedent and that its seasonal references connect to deeper patterns of death and rebirth. The dramatic hypothesis correctly senses that the Song contains a narrative arc with dramatic shifts. Both see something real.

**Where these traditions fail:** The cultic hypothesis cannot explain why the Song's seven continuous passages narrate a single regeneration cycle rather than a seasonal agricultural cycle. The Tammuz cult moves cyclically (death–rebirth–death–rebirth); the Song moves linearly (vastation → awakening → seeking → conjunction → withdrawal → growth → permanence). Seasonal liturgy returns to its starting point; the Song ends in a state qualitatively different from its beginning. The dramatic hypothesis fails because no reconstruction of a three-character plot can account for the text's actual structure: the body catalogues have no dramatic function, the court material fits neither character, and the seven Ancient Word passages do not divide cleanly among dramatic roles.

### 8.5 Why Every School Partially Succeeds and Ultimately Fails

The interpretive history of the Song of Solomon is a history of partial grasping. Each school correctly identifies one dimension of the text:

| School | What It Grasps | What It Cannot Read |
|---|---|---|
| Rabbinic-allegorical | Interior meaning exists; the relationship is divine-human | The specific correspondences; the functional ground of each image |
| Christian-typological | The narrative is sequential; the soul ascends through stages | The detailed imagery; the body catalogues; the correspondential vocabulary |
| Modern literal-erotic | The surface is genuinely intimate; the natural sense is real | The spiritual sense; the anomalous imagery; the compiled structure |
| Cultic/dramatic | Ancient Near Eastern precedent; narrative arc with dramatic shifts | The linear (not cyclical) trajectory; the non-dramatic compiled material |

Every school fails at the same point: the specific imagery. No school can explain why hair is compared to goats, teeth to shorn sheep, eyes to doves by rivers, the neck to a military tower. The allegorical tradition assigns arbitrary meanings. The typological tradition ignores the details. The literal tradition calls them "pastoral charm." The cultic tradition cites parallels without explaining function.

The correspondential reading succeeds precisely where every other tradition fails — at the level of specific imagery. It explains *why* eyes are doves (same spiritual function: sight from good), *why* teeth are shorn sheep (same spiritual function: truths of the natural man, purified and ordered), *why* the neck is a tower (same spiritual function: the connection between interior and exterior, fortified). It explains these correspondences not by arbitrary assignment but by functional analysis: the natural object IS the spiritual reality in its outermost form. This is why the correspondential reading can do what no other reading can: it can read the body catalogues, it can distinguish continuous narrative from compiled display, and it can map the seven-state cycle as a causally necessary regeneration sequence.

The four schools are not wrong. They are incomplete. The correspondential key completes what each school began.

## 9. Why the Song Survived: Canon, Perception, and Memory

The Song of Solomon's inclusion in the Hebrew canon is one of the most debated questions in biblical scholarship. The debate at the Council of Jamnia (c. 90 CE) — if it was a formal debate at all — centered on whether the Song "defiles the hands" (i.e., possesses canonical sanctity). Akiva's famous defense settled the question for rabbinic Judaism, but the question itself is revealing. Why was the Song contested? And why was it ultimately included?

The correspondential reading provides a precise answer. The Song was contested because its surface is erotic love poetry — a genre that has no obvious place in sacred scripture. It was included because the community that transmitted it *sensed* the sacred presence within the text, even though it could not articulate what that presence was or how it operated. The Song survived for the same reason that the Ancient Word fragments within it survived: the spiritual content radiates through the natural sense, and perceivers at every level of receptivity can feel that radiation, even when they cannot name its source.

This is not a mystical claim. It is a correspondential one. Correspondence means that the spiritual reality IS the natural form at the ultimate degree. When corresponding natural objects are assembled — especially in the continuous narrative of the seven Ancient Word passages — the spiritual sense communicates through the literal sense to every reader who has any interior openness. The reader does not need to know the doctrine of correspondences to feel the power of "set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death." The correspondence operates whether or not the reader has the key. The key does not create the power; it explains it.

The Song survived because the Ancient Word material within it is *alive* — it communicates spiritual states directly through its natural imagery. Every tradition that encountered the Song felt this communication. The rabbis felt it and called it "holy of holies." The Christian mystics felt it and built their contemplative theology around it. Modern readers feel it and call it "great poetry." The Song has outlasted centuries of interpretive disagreement because the spiritual content radiating from its Ancient Word fragments is stronger than any theory about what the text means. The meaning communicates itself. The theories merely try to catch up.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 The Song as Bronze Church Perception

The seven Ancient Word passages are written in what the companion thesis ("Two Registers of One Perception") calls the *Bronze Church register*: the register of will-centered perception. The Bronze Church, in the Swedenborgian framework, is the third of the Ancient Churches — later than the celestial Most Ancient Church and later than the spiritual Ancient Church, but still within the period when correspondence was perceived rather than merely known.

The Bronze Church perceived spiritual realities through the will — through love, desire, longing, and bodily sensation. Unlike the celestial church, which perceived through direct influx into the understanding, and unlike the spiritual church, which perceived through doctrinal truth illuminated from within, the Bronze Church perceived through the body's affections. Its register is intimate, physical, and erotic — not because it was less spiritual than earlier registers but because it perceived spiritual realities through a different faculty.

This explains the Song's distinctive voice. The Song of Solomon does not sound like Genesis or the Psalms or Isaiah — not because it is less spiritual but because it perceives spiritual realities through the body rather than through the intellect. When the Song describes the Lord entering the garden and eating honeycomb with honey and drinking wine with milk, it is describing conjunction at every degree — but describing it as the will *feels* it, not as the understanding *thinks* it. When the Song describes the soul being wounded by watchmen and lying sick of love, it is describing the alternation of states — but describing it as it is *suffered* in the will, not as it is *understood* in the intellect.

The Bronze Church register is the reason the Song has been misread as erotic poetry. Its physical intimacy IS the spiritual reality expressed through the will's perception. Stripping the intimacy away to find the "real" spiritual meaning is exactly backwards — the intimacy IS the spiritual meaning at the will's degree. The correspondential reading does not replace the erotic surface with an abstract spirituality. It reveals that the erotic surface is the spiritual reality perceived through the body.

### 10.2 The Compiler: Keeper and Obstructor

The compiler of the Song of Solomon occupies a paradoxical position. He preserved the Ancient Word fragments that are the most spiritually concentrated material in the text. He also buried them in compiled material that obscures their sequence, interrupts their narrative, and makes the spiritual sense almost inaccessible.

This paradox is resolved by the compiler hypothesis developed in §2.2. The compiler was a keeper of correspondential tradition — one who possessed the vocabulary through inheritance but not through perception. He knew that the Ancient Word fragments were sacred. He knew that the body-correspondence catalogues were part of the same tradition. He may even have felt something of the spiritual power that radiates from the continuous passages. But he could not distinguish between continuous narrative and static display, because he lacked the perceptual facility that distinguishes them.

The result is a text in which the Ancient Word fragments are faithfully preserved in the correct order (because the compiler inherited them in sequence) but surrounded by material that he could not evaluate. He treated the body catalogues as equivalent to the narrative passages because both used the same vocabulary. He inserted court poetry because it mentioned Solomon and nuptial themes. He added editorial bridges because the fragments needed connecting. At every step, his instinct was preservation — he kept everything, discarded nothing, and assembled his materials into the most impressive literary composition he could produce.

The compiler was, in this sense, both the Song's savior and its obscurer. Without his compilation, the Ancient Word fragments would almost certainly have been lost entirely — scattered page fragments without a literary vessel to carry them through the centuries. With his compilation, they survived — but they survived buried in material that makes them nearly invisible to readers without the correspondential key.

### 10.3 Implications for the Ancient Word Hypothesis

The Song of Solomon provides a specific kind of evidence for the Ancient Word hypothesis that other texts do not: evidence of the *transition* from perception to memory.

The Ancient Word, as Swedenborg describes it, was written by perceivers — people who saw spiritual realities directly and expressed them through corresponding natural images. When the Ancient Church declined, perception was lost. What remained was knowledge — inherited vocabularies, transmitted systems of correspondence, traditional associations between natural objects and spiritual realities. The transition from perception to knowledge is the transition from the Ancient Word to its literary afterlife.

The Song captures this transition in a single text. The seven Ancient Word passages are products of perception: continuous correspondential narrative, states flowing in causal sequence, the three-fold test fully satisfied. The body catalogues are products of knowledge: valid correspondences arranged without narrative direction, the vocabulary preserved, the grammar lost. The compiler's editorial work is the product of neither perception nor knowledge but literary craft — the effort to make an impressive text from materials whose significance was dimly felt but no longer understood.

This means the Song is a stratigraphic text — a text in which the layers of transmission are visible. The deepest layer (Ancient Word fragments) preserves perception. The middle layer (catalogues) preserves knowledge. The surface layer (editorial apparatus) preserves only literary convention. Reading the Song with the correspondential key is an act of reverse engineering: stripping back the layers to recover what the deepest stratum preserves.

This stratigraphic evidence supports the Ancient Word hypothesis in a way that no single passage could. A single correspondential passage might be explained as allegory or coincidence. A comprehensive body-correspondence vocabulary might be explained as poetic convention. But the combination of continuous narrative *and* static vocabulary *and* editorial packaging — each displaying a different relationship to correspondential knowledge — is exactly what the hypothesis predicts: the layered residue of a tradition passing from perception to knowledge to literary memory.

### 10.4 Limitations and Lacunae

The analysis presented in this thesis operates within the correspondential framework and makes no claim to external validation. The classification of verses into three categories — Ancient Word, compiled catalogue, and editorial — depends on the application of correspondential reading, a hermeneutic that is itself part of the framework being tested. Readers who do not accept the correspondential method will see no basis for the classification. The thesis is therefore internal to the Swedenborgian framework: it demonstrates the framework's explanatory power but does not independently prove the framework.

Several specific limitations should be noted:

**The boundary between categories B and C.** While the distinction between continuous narrative (Category A) and everything else is sharp, the distinction between compiled catalogue (Category B) and editorial material (Category C) is occasionally blurred. Some passages — such as the Rose of Sharon material (2:1–7) or the vineyard saying (8:11–12) — contain correspondential content that may represent a fourth category: correspondential sayings that are neither continuous narrative nor body catalogues but independent teachings preserved as fragments. The three-category system may be too coarse for these edge cases.

**The gap between States 4 and 5.** The text does not narrate the transition between the wounding of State 4 (5:2–8) and the quiet inspection of State 5 (7:10b–13). The spiritual reading infers that the Lord worked in the depths during this gap, but the inference is from the framework, not from the text. The gap may alternatively indicate that the compiler lost a passage, or that the original tradition did not narrate this transition.

**Swedenborg citation precision.** The correspondential key tables in §4 and the reference table in §6 rely on Swedenborg's published correspondences, but not every specific correspondence cited in this thesis appears in the exact form given here. Some are extrapolated from adjacencies in the *Arcana Coelestia* — Swedenborg discusses the correspondence of "garden" and of "enclosed" separately, for example, and the combined correspondence of "garden enclosed" is an inference from the combination. The extrapolations are consistent with Swedenborgian principles, but they should be verified against the primary texts case by case.

**Historical dating.** This thesis does not address the historical dating of the Song's composition, the identity of the compiler, or the specific mechanisms of transmission. These are questions for historical-critical scholarship. The correspondential analysis identifies *what* was transmitted (Ancient Word fragments, correspondential vocabulary, editorial packaging) but not *when* or *by whom*.

## 11. Conclusion: A Garden Enclosed

The Song of Solomon has been, for twenty-five centuries, a garden enclosed — a text whose spiritual content has been sealed within a compiled literary form that every reader has felt and no reader, before the correspondential key was applied, has been able to open.

This thesis has demonstrated three findings.

**First:** The Song contains seven passages of continuous correspondential narrative — approximately 42 of 117 verses — that narrate a complete regeneration cycle from awakening through permanent conjunction. These passages satisfy all three criteria of the correspondential test: functional relationship, cross-context consistency, and continuous causal sequence. They constitute preserved Ancient Word material — fragments of a pre-literary correspondential tradition, written by perceivers who experienced spiritual realities directly.

**Second:** The remaining approximately 75 verses are the work of a compiler who possessed genuine correspondential vocabulary — a comprehensive body-correspondence system covering every major anatomical feature — but could not compose with it. The catalogues display valid correspondences without narrative movement. The court poetry introduces a foreign register. The editorial bridges manage transitions between fragments. The compiler preserved the Ancient Word fragments in the correct sequence but buried them in material that makes their narrative structure almost invisible.

**Third:** Swedenborg's 1763 diagnosis — that the Song lacks continuous internal sense as a whole but contains correspondential imagery — predicted the Song's internal structure 260 years before the present analysis confirmed it. The Song lacks continuous internal sense as a whole because the compiler's additions interrupt the Ancient Word narrative. It contains correspondential imagery because the Ancient Word fragments and the correspondential body catalogues preserve genuine correspondences. The diagnosis is exact.

The four major interpretive traditions reviewed in §8 — rabbinic-allegorical, Christian-typological, modern literal-erotic, and cultic/dramatic — each grasped one dimension of the Song. The rabbis sensed the divine-human relationship. The Christian mystics perceived the sequential stages of spiritual ascent. The modern scholars correctly identified the intimacy of the natural sense. The cultic scholars recognized ancient Near Eastern precedent. None could read the specific imagery — why goats, why sheep, why doves, why towers — because none possessed the correspondential key. The key does not replace these readings. It completes them.

The Song of Solomon is a garden enclosed — and the garden has been opened. What flows out is not abstract theology but the intimate, bodily, will-centered perception of a spiritual tradition older than any scripture that records it. The seven Ancient Word passages narrate the soul's regeneration in the register of the will: what it feels like to awaken after desolation, to seek in darkness, to find and hold, to be entered and opened, to lose and be wounded, to grow in quiet, to receive as family, and to be sealed by a love that death cannot overcome and floods cannot drown.

Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm.
