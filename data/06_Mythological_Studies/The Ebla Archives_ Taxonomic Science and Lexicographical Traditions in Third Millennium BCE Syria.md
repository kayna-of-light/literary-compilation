# **The Ebla Archives: Taxonomic Science and Lexicographical Traditions in Third Millennium BCE Syria**

## **I. Introduction: The Epistemological Revolution of the Northern Levant**

The recovery of the Royal Archives of Ebla (modern Tell Mardikh) in 1974–1975 fundamentally reordered the historiography of the ancient Near East. Prior to this watershed moment, the third millennium BCE was viewed primarily through a bipolar lens, dominated by the great riverine civilizations of Sumer and Akkad in Mesopotamia and the Old Kingdom in Egypt.1 The Levant, particularly inland Syria, was relegated to the periphery—a region assumed to be a cultural backwater, sparsely populated by nomadic Amorite tribes and lacking the urban complexity or literacy required for high civilization.2 The excavation of Palace G and its repository of nearly 20,000 cuneiform tablets shattered this paradigm, revealing Ebla not merely as a commercial outpost, but as a sovereign center of intellectual production, possessed of a scribal tradition that rivaled the great academies of Fara and Abu Salabikh.1

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of the intellectual life of Ebla, with a specific focus on its achievements in taxonomic science and lexicography. The Eblaite scribes did not simply borrow Mesopotamian writing; they adapted it to classify and codify their own reality. Through the mechanism of *Listenwissenschaft* (list-science), they subjected the natural world—birds, fish, minerals, and plants—to rigorous categorization, creating the world’s first bilingual dictionaries in the process.4 Furthermore, the recent and startling discovery of alphabetic inscriptions on clay cylinders at the nearby site of Tell Umm el-Marra (ancient Tuba), dated to circa 2400 BCE, suggests that this region was also the crucible for the most enduring technology of knowledge transmission: the alphabet.7

The integration of these findings—the Eblaite encyclopedias and the Umm el-Marra alphabet—points to a distinct "Syrian" school of thought. This tradition, characterized by a pragmatic yet deeply theological drive to name and order the cosmos, provides the historical bedrock for the "wisdom of the Bene Qedem" (Children of the East) later celebrated in the biblical corpus.7 This report will trace the contours of this tradition from the clay shelves of Palace G to the trade routes of the Jabbul Plain, demonstrating how the scholars of the Early Bronze Age Levant laid the foundations for the taxonomic and lexicographical systems that would define the intellectual history of the ancient world.

## **II. The Archaeological Context: Urbanism and Archival Practice in Palace G**

### **A. The Discovery and Identification of Ebla**

The Italian Archaeological Expedition to Syria (MAIS), under the direction of Paolo Matthiae, commenced operations at Tell Mardikh in 1964\.5 Located approximately 55 kilometers southwest of Aleppo, the site comprises a massive 60-hectare mound, yet its ancient identity remained a mystery for the first four years of excavation.1 It was only in 1968, with the recovery of a votive statue dedicated by King Ibbit-Lim to the goddess Ishtar, that the site was definitively identified as Ebla, a city previously known only as a name in Mesopotamian inscriptions.5

The decisive breakthrough occurred in 1974 and 1975 with the discovery of the Royal Palace G, a monumental administrative complex dating to the Early Bronze Age IVA (c. 2400–2300 BCE).5 Within this palace, archaeologists uncovered the state archives in situ—a rarity in Near Eastern archaeology, where tablets are often found in secondary rubbish dumps.5

### **B. The Architecture of Knowledge: The Archive Rooms**

The physical disposition of the archives reveals a sophisticated system of information management. The tablets were concentrated in two primary loci:

1. **The Great Archive (L.2769):** This room, located off the Court of Audience, contained the bulk of the administrative and economic texts, as well as the major lexicographical lists.5 The tablets were found arranged on wooden shelves (which had burned and collapsed), stored upright with their rectos facing outward and tilted backward to allow the *incipit* (the first line functioning as a title) to be read without moving the object.11  
2. **The Small Archive (L.2712):** A smaller repository that held a specialized collection of texts, possibly related to specific bureaucratic functions or active processing.5  
3. **The Trapezoidal Archive (L.2764):** Another storage area contributing to the total count of approximately 17,000 inventory numbers, representing roughly 1,800 complete tablets and thousands of fragments.11

The fire that destroyed Palace G—likely set by the conquering armies of the Akkadian Empire (under Sargon or Naram-Sin) or perhaps Mari—baked the clay tablets, preserving them for four millennia.5

### **C. The Ebla Empire and the Regional Network**

The archives portray Ebla as the hegemon of a vast commercial and political network. The city controlled a territory stretching from the Euphrates in the east to the Orontes in the west, with vassals and trading partners including Emar, Nagar, Kish, and the enigmatic city of Tuba (Umm el-Marra).1 The population of the city itself is estimated at 40,000, with over 250,000 inhabiting its hinterland and dependency network.2

The texts reveal a "commercial empire" rather than a purely territorial one. Ebla’s power was exerted through trade routes, diplomatic marriages, and the management of strategic resources like textiles, metals, and timber.2 This cosmopolitan environment, where Sumerian teachers from Kish mingled with Syrian scribes, created the conditions for the intense inter-linguistic and taxonomic activity preserved in the lexical lists.14

## **III. The Eblaite Language: A Philological Bridge**

### **A. Classification and the "Graphical Barrier"**

The discovery of the Ebla tablets unveiled a new language, Eblaite, which constituted the earliest substantial corpus of Semitic texts known to history.15 The classification of Eblaite has generated one of the most vigorous debates in Semitic philology.

* **The East Semitic Hypothesis:** Scholars such as Gelb, Huehnergard, and Caplice argue that Eblaite shares fundamental morphological features with Old Akkadian, classifying it as an East Semitic language.16 Key isoglosses include the use of the ventive suffix and specific verbal conjugations.18  
* **The Northwest Semitic Hypothesis:** Conversely, scholars noting the strong lexical correspondences with later Canaanite languages (Ugaritic, Hebrew) and the distinct structure of personal names have argued for a classification as "Early Northwest Semitic" or "Paleo-Syrian".16  
* **The Consensus View:** Most modern assessments view Eblaite as an East Semitic language that retains many archaic features and shares a geographic *sprachbund* with the Northwest Semitic languages, effectively functioning as a bridge between the two branches.16

The "graphical barrier" refers to the difficulty of expressing this Semitic language using the Sumerian cuneiform script.16 Eblaite scribes employed a mixed system:

* **Sumerograms:** Signs representing whole words (e.g., LUGAL for "king"), which were likely read aloud in Eblaite (*malkum*).20  
* **Syllabograms:** Signs used for their phonetic values to spell out Eblaite words, particularly proper names and prepositions.17  
* **Determinatives:** Unpronounced signs used to classify nouns (e.g., GIŠ for wood, MUŠEN for birds).17

### **B. Unique Linguistic Features**

Eblaite exhibits unique features that distinguish it from the contemporary Akkadian of Mesopotamia. A prime example is the first-person singular pronoun. While Akkadian uses *anāku*, Eblaite texts attest to the form */ˀanā/* (written *a-na*), aligning it with the West Semitic *'ani* found in Hebrew and Ugaritic.22 This specific isogloss suggests that while the scribal tradition was Mesopotamian in origin, the underlying vernacular had deep roots in the Syrian milieu.

## **IV. Listenwissenschaft: The Epistemology of the List**

### **A. The Theoretical Framework**

The production of lists, or *Listenwissenschaft* (science of lists), was the dominant intellectual activity of the literate elite in the third millennium BCE.6 Originally dismissed by Wolfram von Soden as a static and unimaginative rote exercise, modern scholarship—led by Niek Veldhuis, Miguel Civil, and others—has re-evaluated these lists as a complex epistemological project.23

In the Mesopotamian and Syrian worldview, writing was not merely a tool for communication but a means of organizing reality. To list the names of things was to define their essence and fix their place in the cosmic hierarchy.6 The list was the "privileged format for recording knowledge," covering everything from the names of gods to the parts of a plow.6 At Ebla, this tradition was not only adopted but significantly innovated upon.

### **B. The Typology of Eblaite Lists**

The Ebla corpus contains a wide array of lexical texts, many of which have direct duplicates in the libraries of Fara (Shuruppak) and Abu Salabikh in Mesopotamia, demonstrating Ebla's integration into a transnational "republic of letters".25

1. **Sign Lists:** Pedagogical texts designed to teach the cuneiform inventory. The "Ebla Sign List" (ESL) organizes signs by shape and provides Semitic readings, representing a local curriculum distinct from the standard Mesopotamian lists.27  
2. **Thematic Lists (Encyclopedias):** These lists group words by semantic category. Major examples found at Ebla include lists of animals, birds, fish, plants, metals, textiles, and professions (LU A).28  
3. **The Vocabulary of Ebla (VE):** The most significant lexicographical achievement of the period. This text exists in multiple recensions (A, B, C, D) and lists approximately 1,450 Sumerian logograms.17

### **C. The Bilingual Innovation**

The singular contribution of the Eblaite scribes to the history of science was the creation of the bilingual dictionary.15 Unlike the monolingual Sumerian lists of Mesopotamia, the Eblaite texts frequently add a column or a gloss providing the Semitic translation of the Sumerian term.4

This innovation was born of necessity: Sumerian was a foreign language in Syria, and Eblaite students needed a key to unlock the prestigious Mesopotamian curriculum.25 In the "Vocabulary of Ebla," the Sumerian lemma is often followed immediately by its Eblaite equivalent, creating a direct bridge between the two linguistic worlds.4 This practice represents the earliest known systematic attempt at comparative linguistics and translation theory.4

## **V. Taxonomic Science at Ebla: Inventorying the Natural World**

The "nature lists" of Ebla—covering animals, birds, fish, plants, and stones—reveal a deep interest in biological classification. These texts were not merely economic inventories but scientific attempts to categorize the diversity of life using the tools of the cuneiform script.2

### **A. Ornithology: The Bird Lists**

The Eblaite bird lists, identified by the determinative MUŠEN, are extensive and detailed.2 Scribes faced the challenge of mapping Sumerian bird names—often based on the marshland ecology of southern Iraq—onto the distinct avian fauna of the Syrian steppe.31

* **The "Colored Bird" (*sa-su-ga-lum*):** One notable entry equates the Sumerian *nam-dar-mušen* (literally "bird of many colors" or "partridge") with the Eblaite *sa-su-ga-lum*.28 This term is a cognate of the Northwest Semitic *ssgr*, found in the Deir 'Alla inscription and Hebrew (Jeremiah 8:7 *sus* or *sis*), usually identified as a crane, swift, or swallow.28 The Deir 'Alla context describes the *ssgr* "reproaching the eagle," implying a small but vocal bird.28  
* **Birds of Prey:** The term *a-a-tum* (normalized *ayyatum*) appears as a translation for a bird of prey, directly cognate with the Hebrew *'ayyah* (falcon/kite).21 Similarly, *bar-su-um* (*parsum*) appears as another raptor, linked to the Hebrew *peres* (ossifrage/vulture).21

### **B. Ichthyology: The Fish Lists**

Despite Ebla's inland location, the archives contain lists of fish (determinative KU6), derived from the Mesopotamian "Early Dynastic Fish List".32 These lists include descriptions that seem to mix morphological observation with culinary utility.

* **Sandal Fish:** Likely a flatfish or flounder, described by its shape.33  
* **Snake Fish:** An eel or lamprey, categorized by its serpentine form.33  
* **Local Adaptation:** The Eblaite recensions of these lists show variations from the Mesopotamian canonical versions, suggesting that scribes were adapting the lists to reflect the riverine fish of the Euphrates and Orontes rather than just blindly copying Sumerian texts.4

### **C. Botany: The Plant and Tree Lists**

The botanical lists (determinatives GIŠ for trees, U2 for plants) provide crucial data on the flora of the third millennium Levant.35

* **The Cedar (*arzatum*):** The Sumerian *giš-nun-sal* (Noble/Princely Tree) is equated in the Vocabulary of Ebla (VE 471\) with *ar-za-tum*.21 This is the Eblaite feminine form of the word for "cedar," cognate with the masculine Hebrew *'erez* and the Ugaritic *'rz*.38 The feminine form *'arzah* appears uniquely in Zephaniah 2:14, providing a stunning philological link across two millennia.38  
* **Hyssop (*zūpu*):** Administrative texts record the use of aromatic plants. The term *zūpu* is linked to the Hebrew *'ezob* (hyssop), a plant central to biblical purification rituals.39  
* **Tamarisk (*bīnu*):** Equated with the Akkadian *bīnu*, this tree was a staple of the semi-arid landscape.41

### **D. Invertebrates and the "Creeping Things"**

Eblaite taxonomy included a category for small creatures that move close to the ground, anticipating the biblical category of *remes* ("creeping things").

* **The "Lion of the Ground":** The Eblaite list MEE 4 116r V 4 contains the entry *na-iš ḳàr-ḳá-rí-im*, literally "lion of the ground/earth".42 This poetic kenning is used to designate a snake or serpent.42 Remarkably, the exact same phrase (*nēšu ša ḳaḳḳari*) appears over a thousand years later in the Gilgamesh Epic (Tablet XI), identifying the snake that steals the plant of immortality.42 This demonstrates a profound continuity in the lexicographical imagination of the Near East.  
* **Scorpions and Locusts:** Words for scorpion (*aq-ra-bu*) and locust (*ir-bi-num*) are well-attested, often in contexts related to apotropaic magic or agricultural threats.43

## **VI. The Umm el-Marra Discovery: Rewriting the History of Literacy**

### **A. Ancient Tuba and the Jabbul Plain**

Approximately 50 kilometers east of Aleppo lies the site of Tell Umm el-Marra, situated in the Jabbul Plain.12 This region served as a critical interface between the rain-fed agricultural zones of western Syria and the arid steppe inhabited by pastoral nomads.12 Philological and archaeological evidence strongly supports the identification of Umm el-Marra with the ancient city of **Tuba**, a vassal state and trading partner frequently mentioned in the Ebla archives.3 Tuba was a hub on the east-west trade route moving goods like lapis lazuli and metals between Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean.3

### **B. The Clay Cylinders of Tomb 4**

In excavations conducted between 1994 and 2010, and analyzed in subsequent years, a Johns Hopkins team led by Glenn Schwartz made a discovery in Tomb 4 at Umm el-Marra that challenged the timeline of the alphabet.7 Tomb 4 was an unlooted, high-elite burial dating to the Early Bronze Age IV (c. 2300 BCE), roughly contemporary with the fall of Ebla.9 Inside, alongside gold and silver jewelry and the skeletons of *kunga* equids (prestigious donkey-onager hybrids), were found four lightly baked clay cylinders, each about the size of a finger.7

### **C. The "Silanu" Inscription and Alphabetic Origins**

These cylinders bear inscriptions that do not conform to cuneiform or hieroglyphic systems. Instead, they feature linear characters that closely resemble the letters of the later Semitic alphabets.7

* **The "Silanu" Reading:** Semitist Ted Lewis has tentatively deciphered one of the sequences as the name **"Silanu"**.7 The etymology may be linked to the Semitic root *ṣlm* (image) or *ṣl* (shadow), suggesting a personal name.7  
* **Dating Implications:** Radiocarbon dating of the tomb context places these artifacts at approximately **2400–2300 BCE**.7 This is approximately 500 to 600 years older than the Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions from Serabit el-Khadim and Wadi el-Hol, which were previously considered the oldest alphabetic writing (c. 1800 BCE).7

### **D. The "Tagging" Hypothesis**

Unlike the monumental or bureaucratic texts of Ebla, these cylinders were likely used as tags. They are perforated, suggesting they were tied to vessels or goods to identify ownership or content—perhaps "Property of Silanu" or "Contents: \[Commodity\]".7 This points to a "utilitarian" origin for the alphabet: a simplified script developed not by scribal elites for state administration, but perhaps by merchants or "nomadic carriers" who needed a portable, easy-to-learn system for trade along the Jabbul Plain corridor.7

Scholars like Christopher Rollston have hailed this as a "game changer," noting that the morphology of the signs fits the trajectory of early alphabetic writing, but pushes its origin back into the heart of the Syrian "Urban Revolution".51

## **VII. The Bene Qedem and the Wisdom of the East**

### **A. The "Children of the East" in Historical Context**

The biblical texts frequently refer to the **Bene Qedem** ("Children of the East") as a people of the Syro-Arabian desert renowned for their wisdom.7 1 Kings 4:30 states that "Solomon’s wisdom surpassed the wisdom of all the people of the East (Bene Qedem) and all the wisdom of Egypt."

The Ebla archives and the findings at Umm el-Marra provide the first concrete historical *sitz im leben* (setting in life) for this tradition.7 The Bene Qedem were likely the pastoralist populations inhabiting the steppe lands east of the urban centers like Ebla and Tuba.7 These groups were not isolated; they were the "carriers" of the caravan trade that moved goods and ideas between the Euphrates and the Levant.7

### **B. Taxonomic Wisdom as a Shared Tradition**

The specific nature of the wisdom attributed to Solomon and the Bene Qedem is strictly taxonomic. Solomon is described not as a philosopher in the Greek sense, but as a cataloger of nature: "He spoke of trees, from the cedar that is in Lebanon to the hyssop that grows out of the wall. He spoke also of beasts, and of birds, and of creeping things, and of fish" (1 Kings 4:33).7

This fourfold categorization—Trees/Plants, Beasts, Birds, Fish/Creeping Things—mirrors precisely the structure of the lexical lists found at Ebla.2

* **The Cedar and the Hyssop:** Solomon’s merism (from the greatest tree to the humblest herb) uses the same botanical extremes found in the Ebla lists (*arzatum* to *zūpu*).21  
* **Animal Taxonomy:** The biblical category of *remes* ("creeping things") finds its conceptual ancestor in the Eblaite grouping of small terrestrial creatures, insects, and reptiles.59

The discovery of the "Silanu" alphabet tags in a context of trade and elite burial at Umm el-Marra suggests that this "wisdom"—the ability to name, list, and categorize—was shared between the urban scribes of Ebla and the mobile, mercantile elites of the "East" (Bene Qedem).7 The alphabet, a tool of mobile commerce, may have been the vehicle by which this taxonomic worldview was preserved and transmitted by nomadic groups after the collapse of the Eblaite state.

## **VIII. The Collapse: Climate Change and the End of the Eblaite World**

### **A. The 4.2kya Event**

The vibrant urban civilization of Ebla and Tuba came to a catastrophic end around 2300–2200 BCE.46 While military conquest by the Akkadians (Sargon or Naram-Sin) is the proximate cause often cited in texts ("I destroyed Ebla and Amanus"), archaeological and paleoclimatological evidence points to a massive environmental driver: the 4.2 kiloyear aridification event.46

This event was a mega-drought that reduced precipitation across the Mediterranean and West Asia by up to 30-50% for several centuries.61 In the Jabbul Plain, where agriculture was rain-fed and pushed to its limits to support high population densities, the drop in rainfall was devastating.3

### **B. Systemic Fragility and De-Urbanization**

Dr. Glenn Schwartz argues that these early Syrian cities were "brittle" systems. They had intensified agriculture to the breaking point; the 4.2kya drought was the "straw that broke the camel's back," leading to a systemic collapse.3

* **Abandonment:** Ebla was sacked and burned. Umm el-Marra (Tuba) saw a dramatic reduction in settlement size.62  
* **Pastoralist Shift:** The population did not vanish but shifted strategies, moving toward the pastoral-nomadic lifestyle of the Amorites and the Bene Qedem.61 This period (EB IV / Intermediate Bronze Age) was formerly seen as a "Dark Age," but is now understood as a time of restructuring where the locus of culture moved from the palace to the tent and the caravan.

## **IX. Conclusion: The Legacy of Syrian Science**

The Ebla archives and the discoveries at Umm el-Marra demonstrate that third-millennium Syria was a center of scientific innovation that profoundly influenced the intellectual history of the Near East. The Eblaite scribes, through their *Listenwissenschaft*, developed a rigorous method for ordering the natural world, creating a taxonomic legacy that persisted for millennia.

This tradition of listing and classifying—from the "cedar to the hyssop"—was not lost in the fires that consumed Palace G. Instead, it appears to have been carried into the Iron Age by the "Children of the East," encoded in the wisdom traditions of the Bible and, perhaps most significantly, in the very letters of the alphabet that were first scratched onto clay cylinders in the Jabbul Plain. The "Silanu" inscription serves as a silent witness to a moment when the administrative needs of Syrian commerce sparked a revolution in literacy, democratizing the power to name and creating a system of writing that would eventually conquer the world.
