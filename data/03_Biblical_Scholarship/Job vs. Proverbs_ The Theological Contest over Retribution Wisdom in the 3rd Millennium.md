# Job vs. Proverbs: The Theological Contest over Retribution Wisdom in the 3rd Millennium

## Abstract

This study investigates the hypothesis that the biblical books of Job and Proverbs represent two distinct, competing wisdom traditions—the Correspondential and the Mashal—whose origins anchor deeply in the intellectual horizon of the 3rd millennium BCE, specifically the Uruk-Ebla axis. Contrary to the prevailing historical-critical consensus that situates these texts in the post-exilic Persian period, philological and literary evidence suggests they preserve a primordial theological contest over the nature of divine justice and the validity of retribution theology. This report analyzes the linguistic fossils linking Job to Eblaite, the relationship between Proverbs and the Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak*, and the radical divergence of the "Suffering Righteous" motif in Job from its Mesopotamian antecedents. Furthermore, it integrates the theological hermeneutics of Emanuel Swedenborg, who distinguished Job as a book of the "Ancient Word" written in pure correspondences, contrasting it with the didactic, non-serial nature of Proverbs. Ultimately, this report argues that Job functions as a systematic "anti-Proverbs" polemic, utilizing the ancient genre of *Listenwissenschaft* (science of lists) to deconstruct the scribal attempt to domesticate the Divine through retributive logic.

## 1. The Uruk-Ebla Horizon: The Primordial Genesis of Wisdom

The intellectual history of the Ancient Near East is characterized by a tension between two fundamental modes of engaging reality: the urge to classify and control the world through order (Logos/Mashal), and the confrontation with the ineffable, chaotic, and correspondential nature of the divine (Mythos/Joban). To understand the biblical expression of this tension, one must transcend the boundaries of the Iron Age monarchy of Israel and descend into the "Deep Time" of the 3rd millennium BCE, specifically the scribal culture that flourished between the urbanization of Uruk (c. 4000–3100 BCE) and the archives of Ebla (c. 2500–2250 BCE).

### 1.1 The Scribal Mind and *Listenwissenschaft*

The genesis of wisdom literature is inextricably linked to the invention of writing itself. In the Uruk IV period (c. 3350–3200 BCE), the first proto-cuneiform texts appeared. While the majority were administrative receipts, a significant minority (approximately 15%) were lexical lists. These lists were not mere dictionaries; they were the manifestation of a "listing science" or *Listenwissenschaft*.

The *Listenwissenschaft* represented the first intellectual attempt to impose order on chaos by naming and categorizing every element of the cosmos—from professions and officials (as in the *Lu A* list) to animals, plants, and stars. Ideally, this listing activity was believed to reflect the *me*—the divine ordinances or immutable truths that held the universe together. To know the list was to know the essence of the thing and, by extension, to possess wisdom.

This "encyclopedic" drive provides the conceptual DNA for the Book of Proverbs, which presents itself as a collection of collections, organizing moral reality into binary categories (wise/foolish, righteous/wicked). However, it also sets the stage for the Book of Job, which utilizes the *form* of the list (particularly in the Divine Speeches) to subvert the *function* of control, demonstrating that the cosmos is wild, untamable, and beyond the "listing" capacity of human retribution theology.

### 1.2 The Coexistence of Genres in the 3rd Millennium

A critical corrective to the evolutionary model of biblical criticism—which posits a linear development from simple folk sayings to complex theological dramas—is the evidence from the 3rd millennium that these genres coexisted. The archives of Fara (ancient Shuruppak), Abu Salabikh, and Ebla reveal that didactic instructions (the *Mashal* tradition) and speculative, lament-based wisdom (the precursor to the Joban tradition) flourished side-by-side as early as 2600 BCE.

This coexistence suggests that the tension between "conventional wisdom" (retribution) and "skeptical wisdom" (suffering) is not a chronological development of the late Israelite period but a foundational dialectic of Semitic thought. The "contest" between Job and Proverbs is thus a late canonical expression of a debate that had been raging in the scribal schools of Syria-Mesopotamia for two millennia prior to the composition of the Hebrew Bible.

## 2. The Mashal Tradition: The Instructions of Shuruppak and the Lineage of Proverbs

The Book of Proverbs stands as the *par excellence* representative of the "Mashal" tradition—a form of wisdom characterized by didactic instructions, often framed as a father's advice to his son, which presupposes a rigid cause-and-effect moral universe. The direct literary ancestor of this tradition is the Sumerian text, *The Instructions of Shuruppak*.

### 2.1 The Instructions of Shuruppak: The Prototype

Dated to approximately 2600 BCE (Early Dynastic IIIa period), *The Instructions of Shuruppak* is among the oldest known pieces of literature. The text is framed as the advice of Shuruppak, a pre-flood ruler, to his son Ziusudra (the Sumerian counterpart to Noah). This framing device is identical to the "My son, hear the instruction of thy father" formula found in Proverbs 1–9.

The *Instructions* operate on a theology of retribution and social utility. They offer pragmatic advice on navigating the social hierarchy, avoiding conflict, and managing a household. The "good life" is attainable through adherence to community norms and the avoidance of disruptive behavior.

**Table 1: Comparative Analysis of Shuruppak and Proverbs**

| Thematic Element | Instructions of Shuruppak (c. 2600 BCE) | Book of Proverbs |
|------------------|----------------------------------------|------------------|
| **Framing** | Father (Shuruppak) to Son (Ziusudra/Noah figure) | Father to "My Son" (Chapters 1–9) |
| **Suretyship** | "You should not vouch for someone: that man will have a hold on you." | "He who is surety for a stranger will suffer for it." (Prov. 11:15) |
| **Social Friction** | "Do not pick a quarrel; do not humiliate yourself." | "Do not contend with a man for no reason." (Prov. 3:30) |
| **Sexual Ethics** | "Do not laugh with a girl who is married; the slander is strong." | Warnings against the "Strange Woman" / Adulteress (Prov. 5-7) |
| **Theology** | Secular/Pragmatic; Retribution is social and immediate | Yahwistic; Retribution is divinely guaranteed ("Fear of the Lord") |

### 2.2 The Retribution Nexus: "Act-Consequence"

The core theological postulate shared by Shuruppak and Proverbs is the "Act-Consequence Nexus." This worldview asserts that the universe is morally coherent: righteous actions inherently produce positive outcomes (wealth, long life, honor), while wicked actions produce negative outcomes (poverty, premature death, shame).

In the *Instructions of Shuruppak*, this is presented as a matter of social physics. If you steal, you will be caught; if you insult your mother, you will be hated. In Proverbs, this nexus is elevated to a cosmological principle upheld by Yahweh: "The curse of the Lord is on the house of the wicked, but He blesses the home of the just" (Prov. 3:33).

This tradition assumes that wisdom is the skill of discerning this hidden order. It is an optimistic epistemology that believes the world is readable and that human suffering is a solvable puzzle—if one is suffering, one must have violated the order. It is precisely this rigid, mechanistic application of the Mashal tradition that the Book of Job targets for deconstruction.

### 2.3 The Amenemope Connection

The connection between Proverbs and the ANE wisdom corpus extends beyond Shuruppak. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (c. 1200 BCE) shows such striking verbal parallels to Proverbs 22:17–24:22 that direct literary dependence is widely accepted by scholars. For example, Amenemope's admonition, "Guard yourself from robbing the poor, from being violent to the weak," is mirrored in Proverbs 22:22: "Do not rob the poor because they are poor, or crush the afflicted at the gate."

However, the Shuruppak connection is more fundamental because it establishes the *genre* of the father-to-son instruction in the Semitic sphere a millennium before Amenemope. The shared vocabulary of "abomination" (*to'evah* in Hebrew, *nì-gig* in Sumerian/Eblaite) further cements this link, suggesting a continuous stream of tradition regarding "taboo" behaviors that offend the divine order.

## 3. The Correspondential Tradition: Job and the "Ancient Word"

While Proverbs represents the crystallization of the didactic *Mashal* tradition, the Book of Job appears to emerge from a different, perhaps older, stratum of wisdom—one that Emanuel Swedenborg identified as the "Ancient Word." This tradition is characterized not by linear, didactic instruction, but by "Correspondences"—a mode of perception where natural phenomena are viewed not as objects of scientific classification (as in *Listenwissenschaft*) but as direct symbols of spiritual realities.

### 3.1 Swedenborg's Distinction: "The Word" vs. "The Writings"

Swedenborg's theological restructuring of the canon provides a unique heuristic for understanding the Job-Proverbs dichotomy. He distinguishes between books that constitute "The Word" (*Verbum*) and those that are merely "Writings" (*Hagiographa* or *Ketuvim*).

- **The Word:** Books dictated by "Immediate Inspiration" that possess a continuous, internal spiritual sense in every word and syllable. This internal sense describes the regeneration of man and the glorification of the Lord. The Pentateuch, the Prophets (Joshua–Kings, Isaiah–Malachi), the Psalms, the Gospels, and Revelation fall into this category.

- **The Writings:** Books written by "Mediate Inspiration" (via the author's intellect) that contain dogmatic truth and are useful for the church but lack the continuous internal series. **Proverbs** is explicitly placed in this category.

- **The Book of Job:** Job occupies a unique, liminal space in Swedenborg's system. It is **not** part of the Israelite "Word" proper, but it is a book of the **"Ancient Church"** (the spiritual epoch preceding the Israelite/Jewish Church). It is written entirely in **correspondences** ("pure significatives"), but unlike the "Word," it does not treat solely of the Lord and His kingdom in a continuous series.

### 3.2 The "Ancient Word" and Prophetic Enunciations

Swedenborg asserts that an "Ancient Word" existed in the 3rd millennium (and earlier) in Great Tartary (Central Asia/Mesopotamia) prior to the Israelite Word. He specifically links the "Enunciators" (Hebrew *Moshalim*) mentioned in Numbers 21:27 not to "proverb speakers" but to "Prophetic Enunciators" who possessed this Ancient Word.

The term *Mashal* in this archaic context does not mean a "maxim" or "aphorism" (as in Proverbs) but a "prophetic enunciation"—a spiritually potent, correspondential utterance. Swedenborg argues that the Book of Job is a survival of this mode of writing.

This distinction is profound. If Proverbs operates in the mode of the *Mashal* as "didactic comparison" (Logos), Job operates in the mode of the *Mashal* as "correspondential revelation" (Mythos). Job uses the "objects of the world" (whirlwinds, behemoths, sores) not as metaphors for moral instruction but as *correspondences* for spiritual states of temptation and vastation.

### 3.3 Job as a Book of the "Ancient Church"

Swedenborg's classification of Job as a book of the "Ancient Church" aligns remarkably well with modern philological evidence anchoring Job in the 3rd millennium (or at least preserving that horizon). The book's setting in the patriarchal age, its lack of Mosaic legal references, and its use of archaic divine names (Shaddai, Eloah) all support the idea that it represents a pre-Mosaic wisdom tradition.

In this framework, Job is not merely "wisdom literature" alongside Proverbs; it is a survivor of a lost theological epoch—the "Correspondential" tradition—which viewed the relationship between the natural and spiritual worlds as transparent and immediate, rather than mediated through the "laws" of retribution.

## 4. The Suffering Righteous: A Divergence of Traditions

The contest between Job and Proverbs is most visible in their handling of the "Suffering Righteous" motif. While Proverbs (and Shuruppak) upholds the view that suffering is the result of folly or sin, the Mesopotamian literary tradition produced texts that questioned this logic. However, the Book of Job represents a radical break even from its Mesopotamian cognates.

### 4.1 "Man and His God": The Sumerian Job

The text *Man and His God* (c. 2000 BCE) is often cited as the "Sumerian Job." In this poem, a nobleman suffers illness and social isolation and laments to his personal god. However, the theological resolution is fundamentally different from the biblical Job.

In *Man and His God*, the sufferer admits: "Never has a sinless child been born to its mother... a sinless workman has not existed from of old." The protagonist accepts the premise of the *Mashal* tradition: if he is suffering, he *must* be guilty, even if the sin is unknown or unintentional. His plea is for mercy, not justice. He does not claim innocence; he claims ignorance of his specific offense.

### 4.2 *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi*: The Babylonian Job

Similarly, the Akkadian poem *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom") features a sufferer, Shubshi-meshre-Shakkan, who is baffled by his misfortune despite his apparent piety. Yet, like the Sumerian protagonist, he ultimately acquiesces to the inscrutability of the gods and assumes that he must have inadvertently offended Marduk. The "solution" is the restoration of cultic favor through magic and prayer.

### 4.3 The Biblical Job: The Radical Claim of Innocence

The biblical Job breaks decisively with this tradition. Job does *not* admit to secret sin. He does *not* accept the premise that "never has a sinless man existed." On the contrary, the text explicitly states in the prologue that Job is "blameless and upright" (Job 1:1), a verdict endorsed by God Himself.

Throughout the dialogues, Job maintains his innocence against the friends' accusations. He invites God to a legal trial (*rib*), confident that he will be vindicated. This is a theological revolution. Job asserts that a human being can be in right standing with God *and still suffer*. By holding to his integrity ("I will not deny my integrity," Job 27:5), Job falsifies the retribution theology of Shuruppak and Proverbs.

If Proverbs represents the "conventional wisdom" of the ANE (Act-Consequence), Job represents a unique "counter-tradition" that refuses to resolve the tension by blaming the victim.

## 5. Linguistic Archaeology: Eblaite Fossils and the Dating Debate

The dating of Job and Proverbs is a battlefield of biblical scholarship. Conventional criticism often dates the final form of these books to the post-exilic period (5th–4th c. BCE), citing "late" linguistic features (Aramaisms). However, the discovery of Eblaite has upended this consensus, suggesting that many of these "late" features are actually "early" archaisms preserved from the 3rd millennium.

### 5.1 Eblaite Cognates in Job

The Book of Job contains numerous *hapax legomena* (words occurring only once) and peculiar forms that baffle translators. Eblaite offers a key to unlocking these "fossils."

- **Water (*mw*):** In Job 9:30, the text reads *bemo-sheleg* ("with snow water?"). The word *mo* (*mw*) is strange; Hebrew for water is *mayim*. Masoretic scribes attempted to correct this to *mey*. However, Eblaite texts reveal that the archaic word for water was indeed *mw*. Job preserved a 3rd-millennium spelling that had been obsolete for centuries by the time of the exile.

- **Possessions (*min'lam*):** In Job 15:29, the word *min'lam* appears. It is a hapax. Context suggests "wealth" or "possessions." Eblaite vocabularies list *ma-ni-lum* as a word for "property" (originally "cow" or "livestock"), confirming the Joban usage and pointing to a pastoral, pre-monarchic economic context.

- **Enclitic *mem* (*wm-*):** Both Job and archaic Hebrew poetry use the enclitic *mem* for emphasis, a syntactic feature attested in Eblaite (*u-ma*, "and/indeed") but rare in standard Biblical Hebrew.

These fossils suggest that the author of Job was not an Aramaizing latecomer but a custodian of a deep Semitic linguistic heritage—possibly the "Ancient Word" tradition Swedenborg described.

### 5.2 Archaic Features in Proverbs

While Proverbs is often dated to the monarchy (Solomon/Hezekiah), it too contains linguistic strata that reach back to the 3rd and 2nd millennia.

- **The Root *mšl*:** The very word for "proverb," *mashal*, has an Eblaite/Akkadian cognate *mšl* ("to equal," "to compare," "to rule").

- **Linguistic Stability:** The "Instruction" genre (*Shuruppak*, *Ptahhotep*) shows remarkable stability over millennia. The "Type B" structure of Proverbs (Title, Prologue, Main Text) is found in 3rd-millennium texts, debunking the idea that "complex" forms must be late.

- **Early Hebrew Poetry:** Studies by Young, Rezetko, and Ehrensvärd challenge the "Late Biblical Hebrew" dating method, arguing that many "late" forms appear in early inscriptions and that style, not just chronology, dictates linguistic choice.

**Table 2: Linguistic Fossils in Job and Proverbs**

| Hebrew Term | Biblical Citation | Eblaite/Archaic Cognate | Significance |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------|
| **מו (*mw*)** | Job 9:30 | *mw* (water) | Archaic spelling preserved; predates standard *mayim* |
| **מנלם (*min'lam*)** | Job 15:29 | *ma-ni-lum* (property/cow) | Eblaite economic term found only in Job |
| **וּמ (*wm-*)** | Job/Proverbs (various) | *u-ma* (and/indeed) | Enclitic *mem* used for emphasis; archaic syntactic feature |
| **משל (*mšl*)** | Proverbs 1:1 | *mšl* (to compare/rule) | Root attested in Eblaite administrative texts |

The presence of these forms supports the hypothesis that Job and Proverbs are not merely post-exilic theological reflections but literary artifacts that carry the genetic markers of the Uruk-Ebla horizon.

## 6. Job as an Anti-Proverbs Polemic

If Proverbs represents the codification of the *Mashal* tradition (retribution, order, success), then Job functions as a systematic "anti-Proverbs" polemic. This is not merely a disagreement over doctrine but a collision of genres.

### 6.1 The Friends as Conventional Wisdom

Job's three friends—Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar—are not straw men. They are the authorized representatives of the *Instructions of Shuruppak* and the Solomonic Proverbs. They speak in *meshalim*.

- **Eliphaz:** Appeals to observation and experience ("I have seen..."). He quotes the retribution principle: "Who that was innocent ever perished?" (Job 4:7). This echoes Proverbs 10:25: "When the tempest passes, the wicked is no more, but the righteous is established forever."

- **Bildad:** Appeals to tradition and the "former generations" (Job 8:8). He asserts the mechanical reliability of God's justice: "God will not reject a blameless person" (Job 8:20).

- **Zophar:** Appeals to dogmatic theology. He assumes Job's guilt is a priori necessary to save the character of God.

Their speeches are "conventional wisdom" in the strictest sense. They are applying the *Instructions* correctly: "Action A (Sin) leads to Consequence B (Suffering)." Therefore, since Consequence B is present, Action A must have occurred.

### 6.2 "Proverbs of Ashes": The Paremiological Critique

Job's response is to attack the genre itself. In Job 13:12, he calls their maxims "proverbs of ashes" (*mishlei efer*). This is a devastating critique. He is saying that the *Mashal*—the very vehicle of wisdom in the Shuruppak/Solomon tradition—has become "ash": dead, burnt-out, and fragile.

Scholars of paremiology (the study of proverbs) identify "anti-proverbs" as sayings that twist traditional wisdom to reveal its limitations. Job engages in a sustained "anti-proverb" performance.

- **Proverb:** "The light of the righteous rejoices, but the lamp of the wicked will be put out." (Prov. 13:9)
- **Job's Anti-Proverb:** "How often is it that the lamp of the wicked is put out? That their calamity comes upon them?" (Job 21:17)

By turning the proverb into a rhetorical question, Job forces the friends (and the reader) to confront the empirical reality that contradicts the dogma.

## 7. The Divine Response: *Listenwissenschaft* and the Subversion of Order

The culmination of the contest is the Divine Speeches (Job 38–41). God appears out of the whirlwind—a symbol of chaos—and speaks. But He does not offer a legal defense (as Job requested) nor a confirmation of retribution (as the friends expected).

### 7.1 God as Scribal Master: The Cosmic List

God's speech takes the form of a list. "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" (Job 38:4). He enumerates the sea, the dawn, the snow, the rain, the lion, the raven, the wild goat, the wild donkey, the ostrich, the horse, the hawk, and the eagle.

This is *Listenwissenschaft*. God is reciting an *Onomasticon*, a lexical list of the cosmos, similar to the Egyptian *Onomasticon of Amenemope* or the Sumerian lexical lists found at Ebla. However, there is a subversive twist.

- **Scribal Lists:** Designed to categorize, tame, and control reality. Naming implies mastery.
- **God's List:** Designed to demonstrate the *impossibility* of human mastery. The animals listed are wild, useless to humans, and untamable (the wild donkey, the ostrich).

God uses the genre of "Order" (the list) to reveal the reality of "Wildness." He shows that wisdom is not about domesticating the world through the *me* (ordinances) but about participating in a cosmos that includes the chaotic and the incomprehensible.

### 7.2 Behemoth and Leviathan: The Limits of Retribution

The final movement focuses on Behemoth and Leviathan (Job 40–41). These are not merely large animals; they are symbols of primordial chaos (associates of Tiamat/Yam/Lotan from ANE myth). By celebrating these monsters, God shatters the retribution theology of Proverbs. The world is not a safe, sanitized moral playground where the righteous are always rewarded. It is a world where Leviathan swims—a world containing elements of danger and chaos that only God can manage.

God's answer validates Job's "anti-proverb" stance: the mechanical "Act-Consequence" logic is too small to contain the reality of the Creator. Retribution is rejected as a universal law, though not as a possibility. God *can* reward and punish, but He is not *bound* by the friends' syllogisms.

---

## 8. Conclusion: The Synthesis

The Book of Job and the Book of Proverbs represent the two poles of the ancient Near Eastern wisdom tradition, a dialectic that can be traced back to the 3rd millennium scribal centers of Uruk and Ebla. Proverbs, the heir of *Shuruppak*, embodies the *Mashal* tradition: the optimistic belief that the world is ordered, the "act-consequence" nexus is reliable, and wisdom consists of learning the rules of the *me*. It operates in the mode of the Swedenborgian "Writings"—mediate, didactic, and practical.

Job, the heir of the "Suffering Righteous" laments (but radicalized by the claim of innocence) and the "Ancient Word" correspondences, embodies the counter-tradition. It functions as an "anti-Proverbs" polemic, using the very tools of wisdom (debate, lists, nature imagery) to deconstruct the rigid application of retribution theology.

The contest is resolved not by the victory of one over the other, but by the Divine Speech which subsumes both. God validates Job's honesty ("Job has spoken of me what is right," Job 42:7) while maintaining the created order that Proverbs celebrates. The result is a nuanced synthesis: Proverbs provides the *norm* for life, while Job provides the *exception* that preserves the mystery and sovereignty of God against the idolatry of a manageable moral universe.

In the end, the "fear of the Lord" is the beginning of wisdom, but Job teaches that this fear must be a response to the wild, whirlwind God, not a coin inserted into a cosmic vending machine.
