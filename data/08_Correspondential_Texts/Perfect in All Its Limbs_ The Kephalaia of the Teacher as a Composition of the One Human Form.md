# Perfect in All Its Limbs: The Kephalaia of the Teacher as a Composition of the One Human Form

## The Father's Limbs, the Mixed Man, the Perfect Man and the Last Statue Read in Gardner's Translation, Corrected to the Coptic

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis asks whether the *Kephalaia of the Teacher*, the Coptic Manichaean compendium of the fourth to fifth century, is composed as one human form: whether the human body is not merely an image in the text but the form of everything the text describes. It reads the *Kephalaia* in Iain Gardner's translation (1995), with the four corrections toward the Coptic made in the library's edition, *The Ancient Word* (version 1.1), cites only preserved text for each argument, and uses Swedenborg's account of the Greatest Man (1749–1763) as its vocabulary of recognition. The answer is yes, at every register. The Father is "established in his five light limbs," and he and his love are "a single living body." The two trees are bodies with the same five limbs, the bad tree "an exact negative" of the good. The King of Darkness is that body inverted, its parts "not resembling one another." The universe was built "after the likeness of a man," and "the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos." The soul's faculties are bound into the body from the inside out, mind in bone and consideration in skin, and are rebuilt as a "new man." The Pillar of Glory is both a column of ascent and "the Perfect Man," with the First Man's head upon it. History ends in the Last Statue, gathered and sculpted until "perfect in all its limbs," while what is "foreign to it" is cast out. Two features match Swedenborg in detail: the two orders of the degrees, column and solid body, and a completion that casts out the heterogeneous. Five, the faculty, and three, the degrees, run through each other as qualities of one body. The sums Gardner calls "forced" are the strain of describing a whole as a total. The form Swedenborg said the ancients knew "from the knowledge of correspondences" governs a text he could not have read.

> **Keywords.** Kephalaia of the Teacher, Greatest Man, Grand Man, Maximus Homo, Divine Human, Last Statue, Perfect Man, Pillar of Glory, new man, microcosm and macrocosm, discrete degrees, simultaneous order, King of Darkness, Manichaeism, correspondences, Ancient Word

## 1. Introduction

The library already holds several studies of the *Kephalaia of the Teacher*, the Coptic compendium of 122 chapters of instruction attributed to Mani (216–274 CE). [*The Ancient Word Recovered*](../00_Framework/The%20Ancient%20Word%20Recovered_%20Extracting%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) separated a correspondential teaching substrate from two later frames, Mani's own Pauline–Marcionite theology and the compiling community's hagiographic staging, and read the substrate through the Swedenborgian key. Among its findings it named the Last Statue the culminating concept of the substrate: a completed cosmic-human body built from gathered and purified spiritual substance (§8.3, Finding 19). [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md) dated what that substrate carries. It took its title from the chapter in which the *Kephalaia* calls its own many names a covering over "a single form, and a single name" (K. 178.13–23). [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) read the substrate's numbers as properties of the perceiving mind rather than as counts in the world. It found in the *Kephalaia* the meaning of five that Scripture leaves open: five as faculty, the radial order of one capacity from its centre to its surface. It traced that order from mind bound in bone to consideration bound in skin (§§5.3–5.4). [*Three Witnesses to One Architecture*](../06_Mythological_Studies/Three%20Witnesses%20to%20One%20Architecture_%20Genesis%201-11%20and%20the%20Correspondential%20Kephalaia.md) and [*Two Registers of One Perception*](../06_Mythological_Studies/Two%20Registers%20of%20One%20Perception_%20The%20Song%20of%20Solomon%20and%20the%20Regenerative%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) set the substrate beside Genesis 1–11 and the Song of Songs. Each of these studies assumes an answer to the question this thesis takes up; none of them asks it.

The question is whether the *Kephalaia* is composed as one human form. Every ancient cosmology uses images of the body, so the issue is not whether the text contains them. The issue is whether the human form is the form of everything the text describes, at every scale it describes. That would include the Father, the realm of light, the two trees, the adversary, the universe, the first human pair, the individual soul in its flesh, the church and the end of history.

Swedenborg made exactly this claim about heaven in 1758. "That heaven in its whole complex reflects a single man is an arcanum hitherto unknown in the world," and the angels "call heaven the Greatest Man and the Divine Man" (*Heaven and Hell* §59). Each society is a man in a lesser form and each angel in the least form (§§68–73), and all of it is from the Lord's Divine Human (§78). Hell in its whole complex "reflects a single devil" (§553). And he wrote that "by the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," a truth "which they derived from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319).

The companion theses take the *Kephalaia* substrate to be a surviving body of that ancient knowledge. If they are right, the Greatest Man should not appear in it as one doctrine among others. It should be the shape of the whole.

The question has to be answered from the text, and the text has to be reliable. This thesis reads the *Kephalaia* in Iain Gardner's English translation of the Berlin edition (Leiden: Brill, 1995). It cites the text by page and line of the Coptic as "K.", and Gardner's introduction and chapter commentaries by the page of his book. It rests no argument on the library's reconstructed Coptic readings. It treats Gardner's square brackets as the line between what the papyrus preserves and what an editor has supplied.

The library's typeset edition of the substrate, *The Ancient Word* (version 1.1), corrects four of Gardner's recurring renderings where they diverge from the Coptic, and every quotation here carries those corrections (§2.2). The edition's Persian names are not used; §15.2 gives them as a concordance.

Read in this way, the answer to the question is plain, and it is in Gardner's words. The bodies appear in this order:

- **The Father.** The Father of Greatness "ex[ists and is] established in his five light limbs," which are "mind, thought, teaching, counsel and [consideration]" (K. 64.20–23). The five fathers who go out against the darkness came "from the five limbs [of the Father]" (76.15–25). Love and the Father "are a single living body, the Father [a]nd his love" (156.3–4).
- **The two trees.** The good tree of the Gospel parable has five limbs, from consideration, which is the holy church, to mind, which is the Father (20.12–20), and the soul climbs to the Father through them (20.21–31). The bad tree has the same five limbs (21.28–30). Gardner calls it "an exact negative" of the good (p. 23).
- **The adversary.** The King of Darkness has "five shapes on him," on head, hands and feet, shoulders, belly and tail, each from one of his five worlds (30.33–31.2; 77.27–78.3).
- **The universe.** The enemy constructed the universe "after the likeness of a man": "His ribs are all the firmament[s]. His navel [is the] sphere of the stars and the signs of the zodiac," and "His heart is mankind" (90.20–91.1). Mani says outright that "this whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body," so that "the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos" (169.28–170.2; 170.16–19).
- **Adam.** Adam bears "the seal of the entire universe" (157.25–26).
- **The soul in its flesh.** Sin "bound the mind in bone; the thought in sinew; the teaching in vein; the counsel in flesh; the consideration in ski[n]" (95.17–19): the highest faculty in the innermost tissue and the lowest in the outermost. The Light Mind releases those members and will "construct a new man of them," with "five great living members" (96.25–30).
- **The Perfect Man.** The Pillar of Glory is "the Perfect Man; the one who bears up under all things" (35.10–12). The First Man is "the head" whose sons are "the limbs [of] his soul," and his head "is placed on the body of the Pill[ar of Gl]ory in the heights of the universe" (84.21–30).
- **The end.** At the end the counsel "will gather to it its own soul, and sc[ul]pt it in the Last Sta[t]ue." It gathers "the life and the li[gh]t that are in all things" and builds them "upon its body. The time when this Last Statue will be perfect in all its limbs, then it can become free and ascend" (54.14–21). The souls that would not be gathered weep because "their limbs have been severed" and they are "separated from the Last Statue" (149.29–150.4).
- **The names.** The many names under which all this is taught "are a single [ ... ] since the beginning." Stripped of their names, they "will gather together [and] make a single form, and a single name, unaltered and unchangeable for ever" (178.13–23).

Gardner reads the text without the correspondential framework, and he describes the same thing in his own vocabulary. "In Manichaeism," he writes, "what is true in the macrocosmos is also true for the microcosmos." "All the divine is ultimately one, so that the diversification represents levels of descent," and relationships between spiritual levels "will be an exact image of that relationship all the way back up the scale through the gods to the Father" (p. xxvi). He identifies the Perfect Man as "the representation of the church as the body of Jesus in its ascent to the Father" (p. xxxii). He describes the Last Statue as the gathered soul rising as "a divinity that in essence recapitulates the descent into the abyss of the First Man" (p. xxxiii). In his commentary on chapter 31 he states the whole schema in a few sentences: the First Man is "the head upon which the soul depends for its life"; the gathered soul is "his trunk"; "This trunk is the Pillar of Glory which reunites with the First Man at its head. At the end of time it is the Last Statue" (p. 86).

**The central finding** is that the human form is the constant of the composition. The same five-limbed form appears as:

- the Father;
- the realm and church of light;
- the cosmos;
- Adam;
- the regenerate individual;
- the body completed at the end of time.

The same form turned inside out appears as the King of Darkness. Every register the text describes is a body, and the bodies are one body at different magnitudes.

This is the Greatest Man as Swedenborg described it: the whole, the society and the angel, all from the Divine Human, with hell as the same form inverted. And it is set out in a text that Swedenborg could not have known, because it was not published in a European language until Polotsky and Böhlig's edition of 1940, nor in English until Gardner's of 1995.

**The central argument** is that the agreement is not a coincidence between two speculative systems. Two distinct features of the *Kephalaia*'s body have no reason to coincide with Swedenborg's description if the form were invented.

The first feature is the order of the members. The soul's five faculties are bound into the body from the inside out, mind in bone and consideration in skin. Swedenborg calls this order simultaneous: in it "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). In the same section he compares the successive order of the degrees to "a column divided into three stories, through which ascent and descent are made." In simultaneous order, he says, the column becomes like "a solid body" whose finest parts are in the centre. The *Kephalaia* applies both of his descriptions to a single figure. It calls the Pillar of Glory a column, through which the gathered light ascends, and it calls the same figure a man, the Perfect Man.

The second feature is the way the body completes itself. Swedenborg watched a society of heaven take shape: it "appeared at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man," and those who were not of it were cast out (*Heaven and Hell* §68). "Those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (§64). The Last Statue is likewise sculpted gradually from the remnant of all things until it "will be perfect in all its limbs," and it "sweeps out and casts from it the polluti[on] that is foreign to it" (K. 54.15–17).

Both features are structural facts about two bodies of text, and anyone can check them.

What the thesis does not find is any arithmetic architecture. The *Kephalaia* counts constantly, and in a few places it tries to build one number out of others. It makes twelve hours of a day out of five sons, five sons and two more (25.21–30). It makes twelve parts out of ten and two (49.14–50.5). It makes twenty-five limbs out of fives (262.25–263.5). These are exactly the places where Gardner, who has no stake in the question, finds the system strained. He calls them "somewhat artificial" (p. 29), "an undoubtedly forced calculation" (p. 54) and "rather convoluted calculations" (p. 268). He notes that the texts "have difficulty reconciling the sets of five and twelve that structure Mani's system" (p. 67).

Read as counts, five and twelve compete. Read as qualities they do not, because they are two qualities of the same body and nothing has to be added to anything. Five is the radial order of one faculty. Twelve is "the things of love and the derivative faith in one complex" (*Arcana Coelestia* §577). The strain the text shows when it sums is the strain of describing a whole as a total. The reading proposed here, and in *The Resolution of the Finite Mind*, removes that strain; it does not need the sums and does not rest on them.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading

**Correspondence**, in the sense developed by Swedenborg and used throughout this library, is the organic relation between a natural thing and the spiritual reality it expresses. It is grounded in what the natural thing does. Light corresponds to truth because light lets the eye distinguish one form from another, which is what the understanding does with ideas. Fire corresponds to love because fire is the active principle that gives light its existence, as love gives wisdom its existence. A garment corresponds to the truth that clothes a good because it covers and presents what it clothes. Because the relation runs through the function, a correspondence can be read: given the thing and what it does, the spiritual sense can be recovered, not assigned.

Four exclusions keep the term from sliding into its usual counterfeits:

- **Not allegory.** Allegory substitutes one term for another by convention, as scales stand for justice, and could substitute any other term as well. A correspondence cannot be reassigned without breaking the function.
- **Not metaphor.** Metaphor says that A is like B. Correspondence says that A is B at the natural degree: one reality seen at two levels, not two things compared.
- **Not symbol in the modern sense.** A symbol is a conventional sign pointing to an abstract concept. A correspondence is the spiritual reality itself present in its outermost form.
- **Not a Jungian archetype.** An archetype reduces a spiritual reality to a projection of the psyche. On this reading the psyche is itself an effect of the spiritual reality, not its source.

Three further properties matter here:

- **Correspondence is directional.** It runs from inside to outside, from cause to effect: the natural is the spiritual "in ultimates," never the other way round.
- **Correspondence has an opposite sense.** The same image expresses a good or an evil reality according to context. Fire can be love or the burning of self-love; water can be living truth or the flood of falsity. The opposite sense is not ambiguity. A system grounded in function inverts its images exactly where the function is turned against its end, and a system assigned by convention has no reason to invert them at all.
- **Correspondence is ordered by discrete degrees.** Love, wisdom and use, or the celestial, the spiritual and the natural, are not three points on one scale. They are three complete levels, each containing the one below it. Swedenborg says they stand one above another in successive order, and one within another in simultaneous order (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §§205–206). That second arrangement turns out to be the one the *Kephalaia* uses for the body (§7).

For this thesis one application of the doctrine governs all the others: the correspondence of the human body with the whole. Swedenborg's claim is not that the universe is *like* a man. It is that heaven *is* a man, and that the things of the human body exist because heaven's uses flow into them. "This inflow of heaven is into the functions and uses of the bodily members; and the uses, since they are from the spiritual world, take on a form by means of such things as are in the natural world, and thus present themselves in effect. From this is the correspondence" (*Heaven and Hell* §96).

On this view a member of the body is a use made visible, and the body as a whole is the form that many uses take when they serve one life. The test for whether a text is written in this correspondence is therefore not whether it mentions limbs. The tests are:

- whether its members are defined by what they do;
- whether the same members recur at every scale;
- whether the parts are ordered as the uses are ordered;
- whether the whole is one life rather than an assembly.

Two cautions follow from the framework's own rules. First, the persons of the *Kephalaia* — the Father, the First Man, the Living Spirit, the Ambassador, the Pillar of Glory, the Light Mind — are read here, as the library reads the persons of the Nag Hammadi texts, as states and functions rather than as characters. Each is identified by its relation and its work, and a shared name does not merge two figures. Second, the correspondential reading does not cancel the historical one. The *Kephalaia* is a Manichaean book with a dualist theology. Gardner's account of that theology is accurate at the natural level, and this thesis does not dispute it (§13.4).

The question asked here is a structural one: whether the human form governs the composition. The answer to that question can be checked by anyone who reads the text, whatever they think of correspondences. The meaning of the answer is where the correspondential reading does its work.

### 2.2 The Text: Gardner's Translation and What the Brackets Allow

The *Kephalaia of the Teacher* survives in a papyrus codex from the cache of Manichaean books found at Medinet Madi, south-west of the Fayum, and announced in 1933. Gardner dates the Coptic texts of that library to about 400 CE and describes them as translations of Syriac originals reaching back to Mani or to the first generations of his followers. They are written in the Lycopolitan (sub-Akhmimic) dialect (p. xviii). The Berlin pages were edited by H. J. Polotsky (K. 1–102) and A. Böhlig (K. 103–291), with the closing pages supplied by W.-P. Funk (K. 292–295) (p. xxi n. 4). Gardner's translation runs to the end of kephalaion 122 (K. 295.8) (p. xxi).

This thesis cites the text throughout as K. followed by the page and line of the Coptic, as Gardner prints them. A chapter is named by Gardner's number when the chapter matters to the argument. Gardner prefaces each chapter with a short commentary; that commentary, and his general introduction, are cited by the page of his book.

The papyrus is damaged, and Gardner's square brackets reproduce the reconstructions of the edited Coptic. Some of those reconstructions are certain from parallels; others are an editor's best guess. This thesis follows one rule: **no inference turns on a word inside a bracket**.

Where a word the argument needs is restored, the thesis says so and looks for a parallel in which the same word is preserved. Two examples show how this works:

- In chapter 6 the King of Darkness's head is "[lion-faced]" only by restoration (K. 30.34). In chapter 27 the same head "is lion-faced, wh[ich] came about from [the] world of fire" in preserved text (77.29–30), so the lion-faced head may be used.
- In chapter 38 the "twelve members" of the new man stand at the edge of a lacuna as "[the tw]elve members" (97.7). That passage is therefore not used as evidence that the new man has twelve members, although the Father's "twelve light limbs" are preserved (64.24).

Gardner's round brackets mark his own explanatory additions, and his angle brackets mark words added to the primary text. Both are treated as his, not as the papyrus's.

The library also holds its own working versions of the text, and they are used as follows:

- **The Coptic transcription.** The library's Coptic transcription of the codex is still being corrected against the manuscript, and its readings are not yet reliable enough to quote.
- **The typeset edition.** *The Ancient Word* (version 1.1) is built on Gardner's English and corrects four of his recurring renderings where the Coptic is unambiguous and his English loses a distinction the scribes made (preface, pp. 4–5). ⲧⲥⲃⲱ, the third of the five faculties, is "teaching," not "insight": the scribes wrote the native word for instruction and not the Greek loan φρόνησις. ⲡⲡⲣⲓ̈ⲉ, from a root meaning to shine forth, is "Radiance," not "Splendour," so Gardner's "Jesus the Splendour" is "Jesus the Radiance." ⲡⲉⲓⲛⲉ is "likeness" throughout, where Gardner varies between "image," "form," "shape" and "resemblance"; the Greek loan εἰκών keeps "image." The causative ⲧⲟⲩⲱ, "cause to stand up," is "raise," where Gardner varies between "release," "free," "save" and "bring up"; ⲃⲱⲗ ⲁⲃⲁⲗ keeps "release." Every quotation in this thesis is Gardner's text with these four corrections applied wherever the edition applies them to the same passage. Gardner's names and his brackets are kept: the edition's Persian names make a point about the substrate, not a text to build on.

The edition is also used, in §11, for its summary of chapter 3. Section 15.2 gives a concordance of the names used here with the edition's names, so that its readers can find their place: "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" appears there as "Hearkening, Hearkening," and "the Last Statue" as "the Final Body."

One further matter belongs to method rather than to the text. The library's stratigraphy divides the *Kephalaia* into three layers:

- a correspondential substrate (Layer 1);
- Mani's Pauline–Marcionite frame (Layer 2);
- the community's hagiographic frame (Layer 3).

Some of the body vocabulary studied here is plainly Pauline in its wording. Gardner derives "the new man" "ultimately... from Paul" (p. xxxi). He glosses "the Perfect Man" with Ephesians 4:12–13, the passage in which the church grows "unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fulness of Christ" (p. xxxii).

This thesis does not run the stratigraphy again. It asks a question that sits beneath the stratigraphy: whether the human form governs the composition as it stands. The answer has two consequences. First, a form that governs passages of every layer cannot be credited to the wording of any one of them. Second, a Pauline name for that form is evidence that Paul also knew it, not evidence that Mani borrowed it from him. Section 13.3 returns to this point.

### 2.3 The Recognition Vocabulary: Swedenborg's Greatest Man

Swedenborg is used in this thesis as a vocabulary of recognition, not as a standard against which the *Kephalaia* is graded. He published his account of the Greatest Man (*Maximus Homo*) between 1749 and 1763, nearly two centuries before the Medinet Madi codices were found. Where his description and the *Kephalaia* agree, the agreement is between two independent derivations of one structure. Where they differ, the difference is reported as such.

The test is for the state, never for the name. Swedenborg has no figure called the Pillar of Glory, and the *Kephalaia* has no phrase "the Greatest Man". What can be compared is what each text says a whole is, how its parts are ordered, and how it comes to completion.

The account is gathered in *Heaven and Hell* §§59–102 and in the series on the Grand Man that runs through the interchapters of *Arcana Coelestia* from §3624. The library's [*The Grand Man: An Exhaustive Anatomical Atlas of the Divine Human*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Grand%20Man_%20An%20Exhaustive%20Anatomical%20Atlas%20of%20the%20Divine%20Human.md) surveys its provinces organ by organ. This thesis cites Swedenborg's own sections. The account can be set out in seven propositions, each with its own section reference.

**First, heaven as a whole is one man.** "That heaven in its whole complex reflects a single man is an arcanum hitherto unknown in the world, but fully recognized in the heavens," and the angels therefore "call heaven the Greatest Man and the Divine Man" (*Heaven and Hell* §59). The arcanum is old: "the universal heaven is so formed as to correspond to the Lord, to His Divine Human; and... man is so formed as to correspond to heaven in regard to each and all things in him, and through heaven to the Lord" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3624).

**Second, the form is the same at every magnitude.** "In the most perfect form generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals, with simply such a difference as there is between like things of greater or less magnitude" (*Heaven and Hell* §62). Each society of heaven is therefore a man (§68), and so is each angel: "As heaven is a man in largest form, and a society of heaven in a less form, so is an angel in least" (§73). The likeness holds "because in the heavens the goods of all are from a single love, that is, from a single origin" (§72).

**Third, the members are uses, and a whole is one life.** "So many different things in man act as a one, because there is no least thing in him that does not do something for the general welfare and perform some use" (*Heaven and Hell* §64). The societies of heaven are arranged "as the members, organs, and viscera are in man" (§94), and the inflow of heaven "is into the functions and uses of the bodily members" (§96). *Divine Love and Wisdom* draws the consequence for the felt unity of a person: "man feels them all as one," and "the reason is that all these are in such a form that not one can be lacking" (§22).

**Fourth, the form is from the Divine Human, and the Divine is its head.** "It is from the Lord's Divine Human that heaven as a whole and in part reflects man" (*Heaven and Hell* §78). In all the heavens there is "no other idea of God than that He is a Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §11). "Because God is a Man, the whole angelic heaven in the aggregate resembles a single man, and is divided into regions and provinces according to the members, viscera, and organs of man" (§288). For the church, Swedenborg uses the image of head and body. "The body spiritual is the church; its head is God-Man"; a church that thought of several gods "would appear as one body with several heads; thus not as a man, but as a monster" (§24). And the human being is human only by reception: "the Lord alone is Man, and an angel and a spirit, and also a man on earth, are men in exact proportion to what they have from Him" (*Arcana Coelestia* §4219).

**Fifth, the degrees of the form are ordered by height in the whole and by depth in the body.** In general, "the highest or third heaven forms the head down to the neck; the middle or second heaven forms the breast down to the loins and knees; the lowest or first heaven forms the feet down to the soles, and also the arms down to the fingers" (*Heaven and Hell* §65).

These provinces are not three regions to be counted off. The three heavens are the three degrees of height: love, wisdom and use, "like first end, middle end which is called cause, and last end which is called effect" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §230). They are in every person by creation: "man, as regards the interiors pertaining to his mind, is a heaven in least form; therefore there are in man, by creation, as many degrees of height as there are heavens" (§231). They are in the least things as in the greatest: "there can be nothing so minute as not to have in it degrees of both kinds," not even "the minutest thing of an idea of thought" (§223), because "the Divine, by which they were created, is the same in things greatest and least" (§225). The head, breast and feet of *Heaven and Hell* §65 are where the degrees show in the outermost form. The degrees themselves are in every member.

The same degrees also have a second arrangement. In successive order they "may be compared to a column divided into three stories, through which ascent and descent are made"; in simultaneous order the column subsides, and "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference," as "in a solid body" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205).

Within the body, the parts with least life lie at the frame and at the surface. The societies to which the bones, cartilages and skins correspond "have little spiritual life in them, just as there is little life in the bones as compared with the soft parts which they enclose" (*Arcana Coelestia* §5560; see §5552). Yet the skins of a regenerate man appear "a beautiful weaving of spirals wonderfully intertwined," and those of the deceitful appear "like knottings together of mere serpents" (§5559).

**Sixth, the form completes itself by gathering, and it casts out what is foreign to it.** Swedenborg describes watching a society of heaven separate hypocrites from itself. "The entire society appeared at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man." Those "who were not in the man and did not make up the man were hypocrites; these were cast out" (*Heaven and Hell* §68). The rule behind the separation is use: "those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (§64).

The inmost of each person tends toward the same form from the beginning. "The primitive of man, which is from the soul of the parent, endeavors toward the formation of the whole man in the ovum and the womb," and "everyone is as it were the center of all, for he is the center of influxes from all through the heavenly form" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3633).

**Seventh, hell is the same form inverted.** There is no single devil: "by 'the devil' and 'Satan' there hell is meant," and every one of the myriads in hell "is a devil in accord with his opposition to the Divine" (*Heaven and Hell* §544). Swedenborg was not shown the form of hell as a whole, but was told that "as the entire heaven in one complex reflects a single man... so the entire hell in one complex reflects a single devil." At the gates of particular hells "a monster commonly appears that represents in a general way the form of those within" (§553). The forms there "were all forms of the love of self and the love of the world," which "are diametrically opposite" to the loves that constitute heaven (§554).

To these seven propositions belongs one historical claim, which is the framework's prediction for this thesis. "By the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," and "the ancients called man a microcosm, or little universe, from truth which they derived from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were, and from their communication with angels of heaven" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). "All things of the created universe, viewed in reference to uses, represent man in an image," and this "testifies that God is a Man" (§319, heading).

If the *Kephalaia* substrate is a remnant of that ancient knowledge, the propositions above should be recognisable in it. They need not appear in Swedenborg's words. They should appear as features of its composition:

- one life in many members;
- the same form at every magnitude;
- members defined by use;
- a head from which the whole lives;
- the degrees ordered from within outward;
- a completion that gathers and expels;
- an adversary that is the same form inverted.

Sections 3 to 10 look for each of these features in turn, and §12 gathers the result.

### 2.4 Numbers as the Quality of One Whole

A text about a body with limbs invites counting, and the *Kephalaia* counts at every turn: five limbs, five sons, five storehouses, twelve wisdoms, twelve judges, fourteen vehicles, eighteen thrones, twenty-five limbs, fifty days. The reading of these numbers decides what kind of structure the thesis can claim. It therefore has to be fixed before the text is read.

Swedenborg's rule is that numbers in a text written in correspondence signify things, not quantities. "All numbers in the Word signify things," and "multiplied numbers have a like signification as the simple numbers from which they arise by multiplication" (*Heaven and Hell* §73, notes, citing *Arcana Coelestia* §§5291, 5335, 5708, 7973). Of seven he says that, "in the internal sense," it "partakes not at all of the idea of number. For they who are in the internal sense, as angels and angelic spirits are, do not even know what number is, and therefore not what seven is" (*Arcana Coelestia* §716). Twelve "signifies faith, or the things of love and the derivative faith in one complex" (§577), and "wherever it occurs in the Word, signifies all" (§3272). Ten signifies remains (§576). The eighth day, as the first day of a new week, "signifies any beginning whatever" (§2044).

[*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) developed this rule into the framework's account of number (§2.4). On that account a number is not a count of things in the world. It is a property of the perceiving mind: the form in which the spiritual mind receives a whole that is in itself undivided. The celestial mind, which perceives the whole directly, has no use for number. The spiritual mind receives the whole through distinction, and its distinctions have irreducible dimensions, which the primes name:

- **two:** the bilateral distinction of good and truth;
- **three:** the vertical distinction of the degrees;
- **five:** the radial order of a faculty from its centre to its surface;
- **seven:** the arc of regeneration (§§4.4, 7.1).

The study found the meaning of five in the *Kephalaia*, where Scripture leaves it open. Five there is the number of faculty. It is the five intellectual qualities of mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration, ordered from innermost to outermost and bound into bone, sinew, vein, flesh and skin (§§5.3–5.4).

Two consequences of this account govern the present thesis.

**Composite numbers are read by their prime factors only.** The factors say which dimensions of one whole are in view; a higher power of the same prime deepens the resolution along that dimension without changing its meaning (*Resolution* §7.2). On this reading:

- twelve (2 × 2 × 3) is love and faith across the degrees, taken as one complex, as *Arcana Coelestia* §577 says;
- twenty-five (5 × 5) is the faculty, five, seen at a finer resolution;
- ten (2 × 5) is the faculty in its good and its truth;
- fourteen (2 × 7) is the arc of regeneration in its two sides;
- eighteen (2 × 3 × 3) is the bilateral and the vertical, resolved more finely along the vertical.

None of these numbers is a sum. No composite number is read here as the total of smaller sets that a text has assembled.

**The arc of seven is plainly a sequence, but it is not read as a sum.** Regeneration does pass through stages, and the six days before the seventh show it: "the six days of creation, and... the seventh, which is the celestial man, in whom is peace, rest, and the sabbath" (*Arcana Coelestia* §395). As a sequence, the seven is two triads and a rest, three layers of which the third is the centre. But the regenerated person in whom the sequence is complete is not seven of anything. The person is one body regenerated whole and at rest, each member of which is one part of that body. The successive order of the stages has become the simultaneous order of the person, in which "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). Seen in time, the seven is an arc. Seen as a person, it is one body, every element of which stands in its place in the whole, as in the Greatest Man.

This settles what the thesis may claim about structure. It does not claim that the *Kephalaia* is built on an arithmetic plan: not five plus five plus two making twelve, not sums that come to seven, eighteen or twenty-five, not any architectural integer whose components can be totalled. The body the thesis finds is not a total of members. It is one life, whose members are its uses, and its numbers are the qualities in which that life is perceived.

The *Kephalaia* sometimes does sum, and §11 shows that those are exactly the places where Gardner finds the system forced. The thesis does not explain those sums away. It reports them, reports Gardner's judgement of them, and shows that the body stands without them.

| Two | 2 | The bilateral: good and truth, will and understanding |

## 3. The Body of the Father: Five Limbs and One Living Body

The *Kephalaia* nowhere describes the Father of Greatness as an abstraction. When Mani is asked how the Father "is established and determined," the answer is anatomical. Chapter 21 opens:

> Hear [this lesson t]hat I will recount to you. Very great is [land of] light in the presence of they who will hear and know it, due to the first Father of Greatness. How he ex[ists and is] established in his five light limbs, which are [ ... the store]houses that have neither limit nor measure to the[m ... ] mind, thought, teaching, counsel and [consideration ... ] his twelve light limbs that are his twelve wisdoms. Now, there are five great light lim[bs] in each one [ ... ] (K. 64.17–26)

The papyrus is broken at the edges of the passage, but its structure survives. The Father "exists" and "is established" in limbs: five of them, named as the five intellectual qualities, and twelve of them, named as twelve wisdoms. "Five great light lim[bs]" are found "in each one." The list of five ends in a lacuna at "consideration," but nothing hangs on the restoration. The same five qualities, in the same order and with the same last term, are preserved elsewhere in the codex: in the binding of the soul (95.17–19), in the limbs the First Man sent forth (85.1–2), and in the five virtues of the new man (96.30–97.3).

Gardner's comment on the chapter is exact: "The kingdom of light is a manifestation of the Father himself, which he pervades by his qualities or limbs" (p. 67). In his introduction he puts it more strongly: the realm of light "indeed is an extension of himself," and the Father "resides in five intellectuals or limbs: mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration" (p. xxvi).

What the chapter goes on to say confirms that a living body is meant and not a list of attributes. From the Father "five grea[t] springs of praise gush for[th]" (64.26–27). Three survive in legible form: "his light that enlig[htens]," which "gushed forth from him and pour[ed]" into the aeons (64.29–30); "the perfume of the [ ... ]," which "pervades all the aeons of greatness" (64.31–32); and "his living voice" (64.33). Light, perfume and voice are what a living body sends out and what another body receives through eye, nostril and ear. The fourth and fifth springs are too broken to read, and the thesis builds nothing on them. Of the fifth only a fragment survives, but it uses the language of the Last Statue: it "was sculpted in them... se[a]led in the[m]" (65.4–5).

A single short chapter then says where the saving powers come from. Chapter 25, "[Concerning the Advent of Five Fathers from the Five Limbs of the Father]," reads in full:

> Once again the apostle speaks concerning [the advent of five fathers fro]m the Father, one after another. [He] speaks t[hu]s: [The Ambas]sador [came] from his mind; the Beloved of the Lights [from his thought]; the Mother of Life from his teaching; [Jesus] the Beloved from his counsel; the Virgin of Lig[ht f]rom his consideration. So in this way were summone[d these five] fathers. These five advents came from the five limbs [of the Father]. One came from another and was manifested in its season. (K. 76.17–25)

The powers that go out from the Father into the work of salvation are not separate gods summoned from elsewhere. They are the Father's own limbs going out. Each comes from one faculty of his body, and they come "one after another," "one... from another," each "in its season." The successive order of the outgoing — one season after another — is the unfolding of an order that already exists simultaneously in the Father's body as limbs.

This is the first appearance in the text of the pattern traced in §2.3: what is a sequence in time is a body in the whole.

A third chapter shows that the relation of the Father to his realm is repeated at every level of the system. In Chapter 20, "The Chapter of the Name of the Fathers," Mani explains why several divine figures may be called "father." The reason is the same each time. Each father has a "greatness" in which "he lives" and "is established":

- **the Father of Greatness:** "His greatn[ess is the gr]eat earth where he lives, he being established [i]n it" (63.30–31);
- **the Third Ambassador:** his greatness is "the light ship of living fire" (63.35–64.1);
- **Jesus the Radiance:** his greatness is "the ship of living waters where he lives" (64.2–3);
- **the Pillar of Glory:** its greatness is "the five gods of ho[ly ... ] which [li]ve and are established in them" (64.5–7);
- **the Light Mind:** "His gre[atness] is the [holy] church; becaus[e] he lives [and is established i]n it" (64.8–10).

Several of these clauses are partly restored, but the formula — a father, his greatness, his living and being established in it — survives in each. Gardner's summary is that the name "can also properly be used of the derived gods in their own realms of greatness" (p. 67).

Read structurally, the chapter says that the system is made of one relation repeated at different magnitudes. In each case there is one who dwells and the dwelling in which he lives and is established: a life and its body. The Father and his land, the Ambassador and his ship, the Pillar and its five gods, the Light Mind and the church each stand in that relation. This is Swedenborg's "generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals" (*Heaven and Hell* §62), stated as a rule of naming.

Chapter 63, "Concerning Love," turns the relation into an identity:

> [Lo]ve is the Father of Greatness, who dwells in his glorious land. The entirety of the divine has been revealed [there]in! These two are a single living body, the Father [a]nd his love; because he has given himself for every thing, as he exists in his aeons. Indeed, due to this, they called the Father, the Lord of the totality, "love." For he gave the victory to his aeons and his limbs. (K. 156.1–7)

The chapter then applies the same formula one level down:

> [These] two, the Mind and the church, a single body is also their likeness; because, again, the apostle too shall gi[ve] his [own] self for his church... Likewise, too, the beginning of ri[ght]eousness is made of love for the church, according both to body and to spirit. While in the body, in the church here; so in the spirit, in the heights above. (K. 156.10–19)

Three points in this passage carry the argument:

- **The ground of the unity.** The Father and his love are not two beings in alliance. They are "a single living body," and what makes them one body is the Father's giving of himself: "he has given himself for every thing." The body of the Father is constituted by self-giving, and his limbs are where the giving goes.
- **The repetition at every level.** The relation repeats exactly at the level of the church. "The Mind and the church, a single body is also their likeness," and again the ground is self-giving: the apostle "shall give his own self for his church," as the Gospel saying about laying down one's life for one's friends confirms (156.14–16, Gardner noting John 15:13).
- **Body and spirit as two degrees.** The one body exists "according both to body and to spirit," below "in the church here" and above "in the heights." These are not two bodies. They are the same body in two degrees.

Two further passages complete the picture of the Father as the head and inmost of a body. In Chapter 60 the corporeal body is used to explain the visible and the hidden in the divine: "Just as [the] corporeal body has limbs that are hidden, so it also has others that are visi[b]le... So it is also with this entire greatness, as we resemble it" (151.9–14). The Father is the body's hidden governor: "Now, thus the directive of the bo[dy] that lives within, hidden in the storehouses inside the body and unseen. So it i[s] also how the Father, the God o[f] truth, exists; as he too is hidden in his kingdom" (151.17–21). Gardner's summary calls this "the analogy of the corporeal body" and glosses the Father as "the directive faculty... of the body, hidden in his own kingdom" (p. 158).

Chapter 39 ends the history of salvation in the Father's own measure. On the third great day "the Father will unveil over them his [likeness]," receives them "in to his hidden treasur[y]," "gives [ ... ] his soul," and will "perfect their stature in sa[m]e measure as his first aeons. And he sets each power [i]n its aeon, in its dwelling-place" (103.10–22). The end of the story is a body brought to the stature of its source, with every member in its own place.

**Spiritual reading.** The correspondential reading of these chapters states plainly what the text shows in pieces: the Father of Greatness is God as Man. Swedenborg's "In all the heavens there is no other idea of God than that He is a Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §11) is the doctrine that Chapter 21 presents as anatomy.

The five limbs are the Divine love and wisdom in the radial order in which a mind receives them, from the inmost (mind) to the outermost (consideration). The twelve limbs that are twelve wisdoms are the same Divine considered as love and faith across the degrees in one complex (*Arcana Coelestia* §577). The two sets are not rival inventories to be reconciled (§11). They are two ways in which one body is perceived.

The land of light, which Gardner calls "an extension of himself," is heaven made by the Divine. "It is from the Lord's Divine Human that heaven as a whole and in part reflects man" (*Heaven and Hell* §78). That is why the Father "exists" and "is established" in his limbs: the limbs are not his possessions but his presence.

The saying that the Father and his love "are a single living body... because he has given himself for every thing" is the correspondential centre of the chapter. Love is the Divine's own being, and love gives itself. The body of heaven exists because the Divine has given itself into receivers, which is influx. Its limbs are the places where that giving is received. The same law holds one degree down: the Mind and the church are one body because the one who leads the church gives himself for it. Swedenborg puts the same relation as head and body: "The body spiritual is the church; its head is God-Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §24).

The five fathers who come "from the five limbs" are the Divine proceeding. In the text's order the powers go out one after another; in the Father they are already present at once as members. This is successive order unfolding from simultaneous order. The Divine does not acquire powers in the course of salvation. It sends out what it already is.

The chapter on the names of the fathers makes the pattern a rule. At every magnitude a life dwells in a greatness and is established in it, and at every magnitude that relation is a human one. The end the text anticipates — the Father unveiling his image and perfecting the stature of every power in its own dwelling-place — is the Greatest Man complete.

| Natural object | Correspondence |

| The five light limbs (mind to consideration) | The Divine love and wisdom in the radial order of a faculty, inmost to outermost |

## 4. The Two Trees: The Ladder of the Limbs and Its Exact Negative

The first teaching chapter of the *Kephalaia* is an exposition of a Gospel parable: "The good tree shall give [good fr]ui[t]; also [the] bad [tr]ee shall give b[ad] fruit... [One kn]ows [each tree by] its fruits" (K. 17.5–9; Gardner notes Luke 6:43–44). Gardner observes that the parable had already served Marcion as "a proof-text for dualism," and that from Marcion's exegesis "it must be traced to Mani" (p. 22).

Mani begins by rejecting the sects' readings of the two trees, which compare them with plants growing "on this composite earth" (17.16–20; 17.33–35). Then he gives his own reading, and it is anatomical. The trees have limbs. The same text later says, from the other direction, that "the body is like the earth, wh[ile these] limbs als[o ... ] of it are like the trees" (281.6–8). In chapter 2 the trees are bodies.

The good tree is introduced as a body clothed in the soul's own substance. It is "the intellectuals that are cloaked upon its body," and "the light elements that are mingled, being compounded in the totality" (20.1–3). Its fruit is "glorious Jesus the Radiance, the father [of] all [the apo]st[l]es," and "the taste of the fruits of the [good] tree [is the] holy [c]hurch" (20.4–6). Then its limbs are named:

> [ ... ] it has five limbs. [They are consideration, counsel, teaching, thought, mind. I]ts consideration is the ho[ly] church. [Its counsel] is [the Pil]lar of Glory, the Perfe[ct] Man. [Its teaching is the Fir]st Man who dwells in the ship of [living] wat[ers]. Its thought is the Third Ambassador [who dwells in] the ship of living fire, that shines in [ ... A]lso, the min[d] is the Father who dwells in great[ness, who is perfe]ct in [the] aeons of light. (K. 20.11–20)

The list at the head of the passage is restored, and so are two of the five equations. But the preserved words give the series without doubt: consideration, then "[the Pil]lar of Glory, the Perfe[ct] Man," then the First Man in a ship, then the Third Ambassador in the ship of living fire, then "the min[d]," which "is the Father."

The passage that follows removes any remaining doubt about the order, because it shows the souls climbing the limbs one by one:

> It is consideration that shall [ ... ] they shall be raised up [ ... ] is counsel; [they sh]all be raised up to teaching, which is the First Man [who d]wells in the ship of the night; fr[om] teaching they shall ris[e u]p to thought, which is the Ambassador who dwells in [the sh]ip of the day. And he too, great glor[ious] thought, [shall bring] them in to mind; which is the Father, the Go[d] of truth, the great Mind of all the [ae]ons of glory. (K. 20.24–31)

The good tree, then, is a ladder of five limbs, and each limb is two things at once. Each is a faculty of the soul: consideration, counsel, teaching, thought, mind. Each is also a station of the cosmos through which the purified light passes: the church, the Pillar, the ship of the night, the ship of the day, the Father.

The soul's ascent through its own faculties and its ascent through the heavens are one movement described twice. Gardner sees this and values it highly: "The limbs of the good tree are the five intellectual qualities through which the soul ascends to the Father and the aeons of glory (20.21–31). This equation of the internal mental process of salvation with the eschatological journey through the heavens indicates a living spirituality in Mani's teaching that is often missed by commentators" (p. 22).

In the terms of §2.3 this is the successive order of the degrees: "a column divided into... stories, through which ascent and descent are made" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). The second rung of that column is the figure the text calls "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man." Section 8 shows that the same figure is both the column of the ascent and a man.

Then Mani names the bad tree:

> [ ... The] bad [tree] has five limbs. Th[ey are consid]eration, counsel, teaching, thought, [mind]. [It]s c[onsideration is] the law of de[at]h from which the sect[s] take instruction. I[t]s counsel is [t]ransmigration [ ... ] in typ[e after type]. Also, its teaching is the furnaces of [fire ... ge]henna; which is full of [s]moke. Thought [ ... ve]ssel. Its mind is the [ ... ] the lump, the last b[o]nd, the [ ... ], they that Satan ca[st away]. (K. 21.28–36)

The bad tree has the same five limbs as the good, with the same names. It is not a different kind of body; it is the same body with every limb turned the other way:

| Limb | In the good tree | In the bad tree |
|---|---|---|
| Consideration | the holy church | the law of death from which the sects take instruction |
| Counsel | the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man | transmigration in type after type |
| Insight | the First Man in the ship of living waters | the furnaces of fire, gehenna, full of smoke |
| Thought | the Third Ambassador in the ship of living fire | [lost] … vessel |
| Mind | the Father, the great Mind of all the aeons | the lump, the last bond |

The movement through the limbs is also turned round. On the good tree the souls "shall be raised up"; on the bad tree they "are swall]owed i[n] to the lump, and they are bound," and they "will arrive at that place; and they have no repentance therein" (22.15–20). Gardner's summary is again exact: "As an exact negative of this the evil tree is also made up of five intellectual qualities. However, in this case the mental process is directed towards sin and darkness; and the corresponding eschatological journey takes this soul to the ultimate death where it is compounded with the final 'lump' beyond all hope of redemption" (p. 23).

The chapter closes with the sentence Gardner calls "an important semi-creedal assertion of belief in absolute dualism (22.35–23.13)" (p. 23):

> Blessed is [every one ... ] these [t]wo trees, and separates them on[e] from [an]other. He understands that they did not arise out of one another, nor did [th]ey come from one another. They did not come from one! The person who can separate them out may go up to the aeon of light... but whoever will not separate out [ ... ] from [o]ne another, he falls to the land [of darkness]. (K. 22.35–23.9)

This sentence is taken up in full in §13.4. Here only the structural fact matters. The same chapter that denies the two trees a common origin also gives them the same anatomy, limb for limb, and Gardner, without any correspondential interest, calls the result "an exact negative." Whatever the text says about origins, it says about form that there is one form, held in two opposite orientations.

**Spiritual reading.** On the correspondential reading the two trees are the human mind in its two possible orientations. Each tree has the five faculties that every human mind has, from the outermost (consideration) to the inmost (mind), so each is the human form.

The good tree is the mind turned toward the Lord, and in it the faculties are a way up. What is received in the outermost faculty is raised through the inner ones to its source. This is regeneration seen as ascent: the natural mind (consideration, the church in which the catechumens' alms are "purified") opening into the spiritual and then into the celestial. Swedenborg's angels "look to Him as the Only One, the source of all things" (*Heaven and Hell* §64). The good tree's top limb is "the Father, the God of truth, the great Mind of all the aeons."

The bad tree is the same mind turned toward itself. In it each faculty is filled with its opposite:

- **consideration** becomes a law of death, instruction that kills;
- **counsel** becomes the endless round of re-embodiment, the will turning from form to form without rising;
- **teaching** becomes the furnace and the smoke, fire in its opposite sense, the burning of self-love that blinds instead of giving light;
- **mind**, the inmost, becomes "the lump, the last bond," the ruling love of self binding everything beneath it into one mass.

These are what Swedenborg describes as the forms of hell: "forms of the love of self and the love of the world," "diametrically opposite" to the loves of heaven (*Heaven and Hell* §554).

The fruit of each tree is its state made visible. The good tree's fruit is the Radiance and its taste is the church; the bad tree's taste is "these evil people, the sects... bound in law after law" (21.19–22). One knows the tree by its fruit because the fruit is the tree in ultimates.

The beatitude on the one who "separates them" names the decisive act of the regenerating mind. It must distinguish what belongs to the Lord from what belongs to self, and refuse to let the second pass for the first. Swedenborg makes the same distinction in his own terms: "none but good uses were created by the Lord," and "evil uses arose out of hell" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §336). Good does not grow into evil, and evil does not grow into good. "They did not arise out of one another." The one who sees this "may go up." The one who confuses them falls.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| A tree with five limbs | The human mind in its five faculties, from consideration (outermost) to mind (inmost) |
| The good tree | The mind turned toward the Lord; regeneration as ascent |
| The limbs as a ladder of ascent | The degrees in successive order (*DLW* §205) |
| Consideration = the holy church | The natural mind receiving and purifying; the church as the outermost receiver |
| Counsel = the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man | The column of ascent; the body of the regenerate (see §8) |
| Insight = the First Man in the ship of the night | Interior understanding in the natural light (the moon) |
| Thought = the Ambassador in the ship of the day | Spiritual understanding in the light of the sun |
| Mind = the Father | The inmost, the Lord as the source of the mind's life |
| The fruit (the Radiance) and its taste (the church) | The state of the tree made visible in ultimates |
| The bad tree with the same five limbs | The same mind turned toward self; the opposite sense of every faculty |
| The law of death | Instruction that kills: truth falsified into a rule against life |
| Transmigration in type after type | The will circling in natural forms without ascent |
| The furnaces of fire, gehenna, smoke | Fire in its opposite sense: the burning of self-love that darkens |
| The lump, the last bond | The ruling love of self binding the whole into one mass |
| Separating the two trees | Discrimination of good from evil; the judgement of the regenerating mind (*DLW* §336) |

## 5. The Anti-Body: The King of Darkness and the Monster at the Gate

If the human form governs the composition, the adversary should be a body as well, and a body of a particular kind: the same form, turned against itself. The *Kephalaia* gives the King of Darkness two full descriptions, in chapters 6 and 27. Gardner judges the chapter 6 account "highly schematic." He says it shows "a certain attention to what can be termed the psychosymbolism of evil," with "a stamp that is unmistakably Mani's" (p. 34).

The opening of chapter 6 sets out the realm from which the King comes, and it is a realm of fives that do not match:

> Five s[to]rehouses have arisen since the beginning in the land of darkne[ss! The] fiv[e] elements poured out of them. Also, from the five e[le]ments were fashioned the five trees! Again, from the five tre[es] were fashioned the five genera of creatures in each world, male and female. And the five worlds thems[el]ves [ha]ve five kings therein, and five spiri[ts, five] bodies, five [tastes]; in each world, they n[ot] resembling [one another]! (K. 30.17–24)

Then the King himself:

> Now, regar[ding the King] of Darkness, there are five shapes on him! His head [is lion-faced; his] hands and feet are demo[n- and devil]-faced; [his] shoulders are eagle-faced; while h[is] belly [is dragon-faced]; his tail is fish-faced. These five shapes, the marks of his [fi]v[e] worlds, exist on the King of the realms of Darkness! (K. 30.33–31.2)

Several of the animal names in chapter 6 are restored. Chapter 27 repeats the description with its reasons, and between the two chapters each shape is preserved at least once:

> Now, f[ive forms] exist in his body, corresponding to (the archetype) [of the five] creations that exist in the five [wo]rlds of darkness. Indeed, his head is lion-faced, wh[ich] came about from [the] world of fire. His wings and his [shoulders] are eagle-faced, after the likeness of the children of the wind. [His hands] and [h]is feet are daemons, after the likeness [of the children] of the [w]orld of smoke. His belly is [dragon]-faced, [after the likeness of the] world of darkness. His tail [is the] form of the fish, which belongs to [the world of the ch]ildren of water. (K. 77.27–78.1)

Where a chapter is broken, the other supplies the word. The kings of the separate worlds confirm each shape in preserved text. The King of Fire "is lion-faced, the fore[mo]st of all the beasts" (33.9–10); the King of Wind "is eagle-face" (33.17); the King of Water "is fish-face" (33.25); the King of Darkness "is a dragon" (33.33). In chapter 27 the King "shall crawl on his [bell]y like the children of dark[ness]" (78.9–10). Gardner's note on "corresponding to (the archetype)" gives the literal sense as "[the appearance] of the seal" (p. 79 n. 32).

The point that matters for the argument is not that the King has a body. It is the kind of body he has. Every member of it comes from a different world. Its head is a lion, its shoulders are an eagle, its hands and feet are demons, its belly is a dragon and its tail is a fish. Chapter 6 states the principle in a single clause: the five worlds and their kings, spirits, bodies and tastes exist "in each world, they n[ot] resembling [one another]" (30.24). The King is a body whose parts do not resemble one another or the whole. He is an assembly of incompatible forms, held together by force.

The rest of the two chapters develops this body as the inverse of a living one:

- **What it is made of.** It is made of the five "other properties": "his darkness... his putridity... his ugliness... his bitterness, his own soul... his burning" (31.3–7).
- **Who shaped it.** It did not grow; it was made from outside. "Matter, who is the though[t] of death," is "the o]ne w[ho] sculpted him from the nature of the land of darkness" (31.9–11), and so he is "h[ar]der than every [ir]on, copper and steel and [lea]d" (31.12–13).
- **What it can see.** It has no access to interiors. The King knows the speech of his worlds, but "their heart is not manifested to him. He k[n]ows not their mind nor their thought... Rather, he only knows and apprehends what is before his eyes" (32.10–13); "he knows not what is far from him" (32.26–27).
- **What its life is.** It has no life of its own: "There is no life [in him] whatsoever, but his 'life' is the bile of [anger] that is app[arent] on his face" (78.31–33).
- **What its members do.** Its members do not hold together as members. "When it pleases him to move, he spreads all his limbs out and walks. When it comes to mind, he with[draws] his limbs and takes them in, and is rolled (?) to his companions; and he falls to the ground like a grape and a great iron ball!" (32.14–19).

The last image is exact. A living body keeps its members in their places. The King's body can draw its members in until it is a single mass, a ball of iron. This is what the bad tree's inmost limb already named: "the lump, the last bond" (21.34–35).

Finally, the anti-body is not located only at the beginning of time. Its members are present now, in human life:

- the spirit of the King of Smoke "reigns today in the principalities and authorities of the earth... humiliating mankind with tyranny" (33.5–8);
- the spirit of the King of Fire reigns "in the greater ones and the leaders," and "is found in these sects that worship [fi]re" (33.13–16);
- the spirit of Wind is "idolat[ry]" in "every temple" (33.21–24);
- the spirit of Water "reigns today in the sects of error; these that [d]ip the baptism of the waters" (33.29–31);
- the spirit of Darkness "speaks till today in the soothsayers" (34.2–3).

Mani ends the chapter by turning to his own: "Concerning this I tell you, my [bret]hren and my limbs, the perfect faithful, the holy elect... Put behind you the service of their five bodies. Live not in them" (34.6–11). In chapter 27 the warning is personal: "do not [lay upon] yourselves... the shapes of this ruler... Hold yourselves secure from his midst and [h]is wicked teachings that dwell in your body" (79.2–6).

Two bodies compete for the same persons as their members. The elect are the apostle's "limbs." They are warned not to live as limbs of the five dark bodies, whose shapes can be "laid upon" a person and whose teachings "dwell in your body."

**Spiritual reading.** Swedenborg never saw the form of hell as a whole. He was told that "as the entire heaven in one complex reflects a single man... so the entire hell in one complex reflects a single devil" (*Heaven and Hell* §553). He was also told that there is no single devil apart from the inhabitants of hell: "by 'the devil' and 'Satan' there hell is meant," and "everyone of them is a devil in accord with his opposition to the Divine while he lived in the world" (§544). The King of Darkness answers to both statements.

As the description of chapters 6 and 27, he is the whole of hell in one form. As the spirits that reign "today" in tyrants, in leaders, in idolatry and in soothsaying, and as shapes that a person may "lay upon" himself, he is made of human beings in their opposition. His members are those who serve "their five bodies." His body exists in so far as people live in it.

The form of that body is the precise inversion of the Greatest Man. In heaven "generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals" (*Heaven and Hell* §62), because all are "from a single love" (§72). The King is a body whose parts are "n[ot] resembling [one another]," because each comes from its own world and its own appetite. This is the monster of *Divine Love and Wisdom* §24: a body whose parts take their direction from several heads instead of one, "thus not as a man, but as a monster." It is also the monster that appears at the gates of the hells, which "represents in a general way the form of those within" (*Heaven and Hell* §553).

The animals are correspondences, and in this context they are in their opposite sense:

- **The lion** at the head is ruling power as domination, "the fore[mo]st of all the beasts."
- **The eagle** at the shoulders is the soaring of the intellect in its opposite sense, reasoning that flies over truth and seizes it.
- **The demon** at hands and feet is the active and the walking life in the service of falsity.
- **The dragon** at the belly is the appetite that receives and digests, in the sensual mind that crawls.
- **The fish** at the tail is the lowest natural, the merely factual, in its cold and sliding form.

Each part is the use of a human member perverted, and the body as a whole has no use but itself.

The other features of the anti-body are what Swedenborg reports of hell's inhabitants:

- **Their faces.** Faces "hideous, and void of life like those of corpses," and speech "like the speech of anger or of hatred or of revenge" (*Heaven and Hell* §553). The King has no life but "the bile of anger" on his face.
- **Their loves.** These forms are "all forms of the love of self and the love of the world" (§554).
- **Their sight.** A love of self knows only what is before its eyes. It cannot see into another's heart, because it has no interiors open to receive another's life, and so the King "knows not their mind nor their thought."
- **Their making.** The love of self is not a growth from within but a hardening imposed from outside. The King is "sculpted" by "the thought of death," just as, at the other end of the book, the Last Statue sculpts itself from the life it gathers (§9).
- **Their collapse.** In the end the love of self draws every member into itself until nothing remains but a mass. The King "withdraws his limbs" and falls "like a grape and a great iron ball," and the bad tree's inmost limb is "the lump, the last bond."

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| The King of Darkness as one body | Hell in its whole complex as "a single devil" (*HH* §553) |
| The spirits of the kings reigning "today" in persons and institutions | Every inhabitant of hell a devil according to his opposition (*HH* §544) |
| Five shapes from five worlds, "not resembling one another" | The parts unlike the whole: the monster, the inverse of the Greatest Man (*HH* §62; *DLW* §24) |
| The lion-faced head | Ruling power as domination (opposite sense) |
| The eagle-faced shoulders and wings | Reasoning that soars over truth to seize it (opposite sense) |
| Demon hands and feet | Action and conduct in the service of falsity |
| The dragon belly | Sensual appetite; the mind that crawls |
| The fish tail | The lowest natural, cold and sliding (opposite sense) |
| Sculpted by Matter, "the thought of death"; hard as iron | The love of self as a hardening imposed from without |
| Knows only what is before his eyes; the heart hidden from him | A mind without open interiors |
| No life but "the bile of anger" on his face | Faces void of life; speech of anger (*HH* §553) |
| Spreading and withdrawing the limbs; falling like an iron ball | The love of self contracting every member into one mass; the lump |
| "My brethren and my limbs" warned not to live in the five bodies | Human beings as members of heaven or hell according to their life |

## 6. The Universe Built After the Likeness of a Man

Three chapters of the *Kephalaia* describe the visible universe as a human body, each from a different direction:

- **Chapter 38** begins from the universe and finds a man in it.
- **Chapter 70** begins from the human body and finds the universe in it.
- **Chapter 64** finds both in Adam, on whom "the seal of the entire universe is stamped."

A short fourth chapter, 60, uses the body to say which parts of the divine are seen and which are hidden. Gardner's chapter notes leave no doubt about the theme. Chapter 38 "demonstrates the correlation between the macrocosmos and the microcosmos of the human body" (p. 93); in it "the universe is constructed in the form of a human" (p. 94). Of chapter 70 he writes that "the basic theme here is the correspondence between the micro- and macro-cosmos" (p. 179).

**Chapter 38: the universe as a man.** A disciple asks how the Light Mind, a great and immeasurable god, can "come and appear in the smallness of the bo[dy]" (89.32–33). Mani answers by describing the great body first:

> All the error, when the en[e]my of the lights constructed it, he constructed after the likeness of a man. The head of the universe is the beginning of the garments. [H]is neck is the nape of the garm[ents]. His stomach [is the] five unfolded ones (?), which a[re ... ] of the garm[e]nts. His ribs are all the firmament[s]. His navel [is the] sphere of the stars and the signs of the zodiac. And, also, [the par]ts [that] come from his navel to his hip are [ ... ] that come from the sphere t[o the corne]rs of the four worlds. His loins are the th[ree eart]hs that are below [ ... u]pon the head of the Porter... His heart is mankind. His liver is the four-footed animals. His lung is the race of birds that [fly in] the air. His spleen is the race of fish that swim in the waters. His kidneys are the world of reptiles that creep [up]on the earth. His outer skin is the wall that [ ... which] surrounds the piercing and the great fire... His veins [ ... ] all [the spri]ngs and wells. (K. 90.20–91.11)

The description runs from head to feet and then from heart to skin. The outer frame of the body is the frame of the cosmos: head, neck, ribs, navel, hips, loins, shins and feet laid along the heavens, the firmaments, the zodiac, the four worlds and the earths. The inner organs are the living things that fill the frame. The heart is mankind, and the liver, lung, spleen and kidneys are the four other kinds of animal life: beasts, birds, fish and reptiles.

Mani then names what keeps this body alive and in order: "F[iv]e g[od]s are fastened in him. [ ... ] they are his soul and his life" (91.15–16). Five "sleepless guardians" stand watch in it (91.18), each identified with one of the five faculties:

- the Keeper of Splendour, "the mind" (91.20; "set firm in the great mind," 92.12–13);
- the King of Honour, "who is the thought" (92.24–25);
- the Adamant of Light, "who is the teaching" (91.25);
- the King of Glory, "who is the coun[sel ... ] patience" (91.27–28), "the great counsel" (93.9–10);
- the Porter, "who is the consideration" (91.31).

At the centre of the whole the Ambassador "placed in them the great Mind; who is the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" (92.5–6). Gardner's summary is that "the five sons of the Living Spirit are the intellectual qualities, who act as sleepless guardians, each firmly established in its place," and that "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man, is the Mind in the universe" (p. 94).

Mani then turns from the great body to the small one:

> So also is this body! A mighty power lives here, even if it is small in its stature. Nevertheless, sin dwells within, and the old man who is lodged in it... until the Light Mind finds how to humble this body, and drive it [according to] his pleasure. Just so, in the w[atch-]districts of his great outer brothers, they who are masters in the zone. In them, in the great body: the earthquake and treachery happened from time to time. So, also, is the watch-district of the Mind, which is the body of the flesh. (K. 94.17–26)

The comparison holds both ways. The universe is "the great body," the individual is "this body," and the Light Mind keeps watch in the small body as the guardians keep watch in the great one.

The preceding short chapter, 37, has already given the principle of construction. The Living Spirit "suspended the zones on their zones," hanging "the five powers of darkness on the five shining men, the sons of the M[a]n," while "the entire edifice is suspended from" the five sons of the Living Spirit (89.4–11). The universe is a series of layers each hung from the one within it.

**Chapter 70: the body as a universe.** Chapter 70 begins from the other end, and its title states its thesis: "Concerning the Body: It was constructed after the Pattern of the Universe." Mani's opening sentence states the principle in both directions:

> This whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body; [as the formation of this body of flesh ac]cords to the pattern of the universe. Its head is like the first-fruits of the five garments. And, from its neck down to the site of its heart, it resembles the pattern of the ten firmaments. And again, the heart accords to the wheel of the rotating sphere. And, from its heart down to its intestines (?), is like this atmosphere that extends from the sphere down to the earth... And also, from its intestines (?) down to its loins is like the three earths. And also, its shinbones to the pattern of the space in which the Porter stands. Its footsoles to the great earth upon which the Porter is stood... Its liver to the vessel of fire. Its flesh to the vessel of darkness. Its blood to the vessel of water. Now, this is how the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos in its firmaments, in its orderings, in its mountains, its walls and its vessels. (K. 169.28–170.20)

The first clause, "This whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body," is preserved apart from two letters. The closing summary, "this is how the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos," is preserved entire.

The chapter then does what chapter 38 did, but in the regenerate person. The universe has "five great camps," each kept by one of the five sons of the Living Spirit (170.21–171.11). The threes stand within the five. In the first watch the Keeper of Splendour has "his authority over the three heavens." The fourth watch "is the three wheels," and the King of Glory's "[auth]ority lies over the three earths upon the hea[d] of the Porter" (170.23–171.7). "This is also the case with this body that the e[le]ct wear. There are another five camp[s] there, and the Light Mind is watching over them, and the new man who is with him" (171.29–172.4). Each camp in the person is a member brought under order, and each such mastery "resembles" one guardian:

> The elect person who will control and subdue the configuration of his face, which is above his body, and guide it towards the good; he resembles the mystery of the Keeper of Splendour... Whoever will become master [over] his heart and subdue it; he is the sign of the great King of Honour... Whoever will become master to his genitals and subdue his lust; he is the mystery of the Adamant of Light, the one who subdues Matter. Whoever will subdue the stomach and become master to the fire that is in him, and also purify the nourishment that comes in to him; he resembles the likeness of the King of Glory, the one who turns the wheels, sending the life above. Whoever will become master over the ruling-power that is beneath in his feet, and bind it with the chain of peace; he is like the Porter. (K. 172.4–20)

Then two further correspondences close the series. "The teaching of wisdom, which circulates in that body," is "to the likeness of the Virgin of Light, who goes up and comes down." "Love and joy, faith and truth, in which the person should l[iv]e," are "to the likeness of these two light ships. For, the living s[o]ul should go up in them" (172.20–29).

**The order is by use, not by position.** Taken together, chapters 38 and 70 give the same five at three registers: a guardian of the cosmos, a faculty of the mind and a member of the regenerate body. Each is drawn from the preserved statements of the two chapters:

| Guardian (son of the Living Spirit) | Faculty (ch. 38) | His watch in the universe (ch. 70) | Member mastered in the elect (ch. 70) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keeper of Splendour | mind | the battlement of all the works; the three heavens | the face, "above his body" |
| King of Honour | thought | the seven firmaments | the heart |
| Adamant of Light | teaching | from the firmament down to the earth; the sphere and atmosphere | the genitals and lust; "subdues Matter" |
| King of Glory | counsel, patience | the three wheels of wind, water and fire; the three earths | the stomach, its fire and its nourishment; "sending the life above" |
| Porter | consideration, wisdom | the great earth and its four fastenings | the feet, bound "with the chain of peace" |

The order of the members in the last column is not anatomical. In the body the stomach lies above the genitals, but the text sets the genitals third and the stomach fourth. It does so because it assigns each member by what it does. The Adamant "subdues Matter," so he answers to the mastery of lust. The King of Glory "turns the wheels, sending the life above," so he answers to the stomach that purifies "the nourishment that comes in to him." What the five have in common is their use, not their position in space.

**Chapter 64: Adam.** Between the great body and the small stands the first human being. Chapter 64 explains that "three great things that are distinct were reveal[ed] in Ad[am] the first human" (157.3–4):

- "The image of the exalted one was placed upon him," for his makers "sealed him after the shining form of the image that had been displayed to them above" (157.7–9).
- He "was shaped in his structure" by light, so that "the formation of his soul fits over the correct distribution of the elements" (157.12–18).
- "The teachings and counsels and the seal of all the powers above and below" were "gathered" and "sealed" in him. "(Adam) and his consort Eve became a dwelling and home for the signs of the zodiac and the stars; and the months, the days and the years. For the seal of the entire universe is stamped upon Adam" (157.20–26).

The rulers who formed him intended to use the seal against the light. Among their seven purposes, Adam was to be "a seal-ring and a new impression, so [that] all births who will be begotten might be brought forth after his s[eal]" (158.12–14). Adam is the macrocosm in the form of a single man, and the form passes to everyone born of him.

**Chapter 60: the hidden and the visible.** The short chapter 60 uses the same body for a different purpose. "J[u]st as [the] corporeal body has limbs that are hidden, so it also has others that are visi[b]le... So it is also with this entire greatness, as we resemble it" (151.9–14). The members of the divine body are then assigned:

- the Father is "the directive of the bo[dy] that lives within, hidden in the storehouses inside the body and unseen" (151.17–21);
- the sun and moon are its "two eyes that are visible and seen, and are even the body's source of illumination" (151.22–27);
- the living soul is the part of the body that "can be grasped and mastered" and "receive blows and wounds" (151.28–152.1);
- Jesus the Radiance is the tongue, "that speaks, proclaims, reprimands and reveals about everything happening in this body," who "manifests and reveals about everything, both the external and the internal" (152.1–10).

**Spiritual reading.** These chapters state the doctrine Swedenborg attributed to the ancients: "By the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," and they knew it "from the knowledge of correspondences" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). The *Kephalaia* does not merely compare the universe to a body. Its claim is that the universe "reflects the pattern of the human body" and that the human body "accords to the pattern of the universe." That is the claim of correspondence, which says that the two are one form at two magnitudes, and it is what Swedenborg means when he writes that "all things of the created universe, viewed in reference to uses, represent man in an image" (§319, heading).

The text even agrees with Swedenborg about the key to the image. Swedenborg says that the image "is not seen when these things are viewed in their forms, it is seen only when they are viewed in respect to uses" (§322). The *Kephalaia* assigns the members of the regenerate body to the guardians by use, against their position, and the image holds because it is read by use.

Chapter 38's statement that the enemy "constructed" the error "after the likeness of a man" has to be read at the level where the text means it. The enemy does not originate the form; he reproduces an image that is shown to him. The rulers formed Adam "after the shining form of the image that had been displayed to them above" (157.8–9). What they build is a reflection, built from below, of what was displayed from above.

Nor does what they build live from itself. The life of the great body is not the enemy's: "F[iv]e g[od]s are fastened in him... they are his soul and his life" (91.15–16). The ruler of darkness himself has "no life [in him] whatsoever" (78.31–32).

This is spiritual physics, and it is Swedenborg's. "God alone, consequently the Lord, is Love itself, because He is Life itself and angels and men are recipients of life" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §4). A form, however it is shaped and by whomever, is a receiver. It is alive only with the life that flows into it, and that life is the Lord's. The darkness can take the image the light displays and reproduce its reflection. It cannot make the form, and it cannot make the reflection live. Swedenborg makes the same observation about hell, which turns the form against its uses and yet, "among themselves," appears "as men" (*Heaven and Hell* §553).

The form, then, is the Lord's in the *Kephalaia* as in Swedenborg. The universe of the *Kephalaia* is the human form in the state the text calls mixture: the natural degree, in which the life of the spiritual is present but bound. Its "soul and life" are the five sons of the First Man, and it is held in order by five guardians who are the faculties of a mind.

In the language of the framework this is the natural mind in which the spiritual is present but not yet free. Its faculties stand as watchmen at their posts, and in its watch-districts "the earthquake and treachery happened from time to time." Swedenborg's account of the natural is the same in kind. The natural is the outermost of the simultaneous order, the circumference within which the higher degrees are present (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205), and it is where the work of regeneration is done.

That is why chapter 70 can treat the mastery of each bodily member as the likeness of a cosmic guardian. To govern the face, the heart, desire, appetite and gait is to bring the outermost of the human form into order under its inmost, which is what the guardians do for the universe.

The single correspondences agree with Swedenborg's account of the provinces:

- The head of the universe is "the beginning of the garments," and the head of the body is "the first-fruits of the five garments": the inmost and highest, as the head is the third heaven in the Greatest Man (*Heaven and Hell* §65).
- The heart is mankind in the great body and "the wheel of the rotating sphere" in the small. In both, it is the centre about which the life of the whole turns.
- The stomach, whose master will "purify the nourishment that comes in to him," answers to Swedenborg's societies of "various purifications of good and truth" (§96).
- The feet, standing on the great earth, are the lowest good on which the whole rests (§96).
- Jesus the Radiance as the tongue "that... reveals about everything... both the external and the internal" is Swedenborg's province of the mouth and tongue, "the ability to converse from understanding and perception" (§96).
- The Father as the hidden "directive" inside the body is the inmost from which the whole lives unseen.

Adam, finally, is the Greatest Man in least form. "The seal of the entire universe is stamped upon Adam" is the *Kephalaia*'s way of saying what Swedenborg says of every angel: "As heaven is a man in largest form, and a society of heaven in a less form, so is an angel in least" (*Heaven and Hell* §73). Adam bears "the image of the exalted one" because the human form is the image of the Divine. Those who formed him wanted to rule "through him" because whoever holds the human form holds the key to the whole. On the correspondential reading, the rulers' seven purposes (158.1–23) are the love of self's attempt to make the human form its own instrument. The good "induce him to life because of their likeness and shape placed upon him" (157.28–29). The form is theirs before it is anyone else's.

## 7. The Soul in the Little Body: Binding, Release and the New Man

The question that opens chapter 38 is about the individual, and once Mani has described the great body he answers it in the small one. The disciple's question had already used the body's language. The Light Mind, he said, "enters withi[n the body of] the flesh and binds the ol[d ma]n with his five counsels, he [set]s his five [couns]els upon him in the five limbs [of his] body" (89.26–29). How, he asks, can one who is "a great God, unchanging and im[m]easurable... come and appear in the smallness of the bo[dy]?" (89.32–33).

Mani's answer comes in three movements. The first explains how the soul came to be bound in the body. The second explains how the Light Mind releases it and builds a new man. The third explains how the new man can be lost again, limb by limb. Each movement is told in the five members.

**The binding.** "Sin took this body... It took its [bod]y [from the five b]odies of darkness. (Sin) constructed the body. Yet, its [sou]l i[t] took from the five shining gods" (95.13–16). Then comes the sentence on which the whole account turns:

> [(Sin) bou]nd (the soul) in the five members of the body. It bound the mind in bone; the thought in sinew; the teaching in vein; the counsel in flesh; the consideration in ski[n]. (K. 95.16–19)

Sin then overlays the soul's faculties with its own, faculty upon faculty: "its mind upon the mind of the soul; [its] thought upon the thought of the soul; its teaching upon the teaching [of] the [s]oul; its counsel upon the counsel of the soul; its consider[atio]n upon the consideration of the soul" (95.20–23). Sin's "five [an]gels and authorities" lead the soul "to all the sins of lust, to the [wor]ship of idols, to erroneous opinions" (95.23–28). And the soul "assumed error and forgetfulness. It forgot its essence and its race and its kindred; kn[o]wing not the door of the place to pray to him" (96.2–5).

The order of the binding is the centre of the passage. [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) first drew attention to it (§5.4). The five faculties are listed from the highest to the lowest, mind to consideration. The five tissues are listed from the innermost to the outermost: bone, which lies deepest and bears the frame; sinew, which binds bone to bone; vein, which carries the blood through the flesh; flesh; and skin, the surface. The highest faculty is bound in the innermost tissue and the lowest in the outermost. What was a ladder in the two trees of §4, consideration at the bottom and mind at the top, is here a solid body, mind at the core and consideration at the surface.

This is Swedenborg's distinction between the two orders of the degrees, applied to the body. In successive order the degrees "may be compared to a column divided into three stories"; in simultaneous order "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference," as "in a solid body" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). The *Kephalaia* gives its five faculties both arrangements, the ladder in chapter 2 and the body in chapter 38, and the second is the first turned inward.

**The release.** The Light Mind comes, "finds the soul," and undoes the binding member by member, in the same order. Each member of the soul is released and the matching member of sin is bound in its place:

> He shall loosen the m[ind of the soul and release] it from the bone. He shall release the th[ought of the soul] from the sinew; and s[o] bind the thought [of the sin in] the sinew. He shall release the teaching of the soul from the vei[n]; and so bind the teaching of the sin in the vein. [He sh]all loosen the counsel of the soul, and release it from the flesh; and so bind the counsel of the sin in the flesh. He shall release the consideration of the soul from the skin; and bin[d] the consideration of the sin in the skin. This is how he shall release the members of the soul, and make them free from the five members of sin. Conversely, these five members of sin, which were loose; he shall bind them. (K. 96.13–25)

The release does not leave the soul bodiless. It is followed at once by a construction:

> He shall set right the members of the soul; form and purify them, and construct a new man of them, a child [o]f righteousness. [And] when [he] fashions and const[r]ucts and purifies the new man; then he shall bring forth five great living members out from the five great memb[ers]. And he places them in the members of the new man. He shall place his m[ind], which is love, in the mind of the new man. Also, the th[oug]ht, which is faith, he shall pl[ace in] the thought [of the] new [man]... His teaching, which [is perfection, he shall place] in the teaching of the new man. His counsel, which [is] patience, he shall place in his counsel. Also, [w]isdom, which is his consideration, in the consideration of the new man. (K. 96.25–97.3)

Gardner comments that "this 'new man', a concept ultimately to be derived from Paul, will now display the five virtues of love, faith, perfection, patience and wisdom" (p. xxxi). The new man is not a disembodied soul. He is a man with five members, and each member is the Light Mind's own faculty placed in the matching faculty of the person. The result is a body governed from within: "the new man reigns [b]y his love, by hi[s] faith, by his perfection, [b]y his [p]atience, and by his wisdom. Yet, his king [ ... the] Light [Min]d, he w[ho is] king over the totality" (97.19–22).

**The loss.** The third movement is the most remarkable, because it shows the same body coming apart. Sin can "mount up with its foolishness; and it disturbs the consideration, and it clouds the wisdom and the understanding of the person" (97.26–28). If the person does not accept the correction of his brethren, sin climbs from member to member, inward:

- **from consideration to counsel.** "Then shall sin mount up again, another time, from the consideration to the counsel. It takes his patience f[rom him]" (98.3–5).
- **into the will.** Sin rises again "and clothe him with lust and vanity and pride" (98.17–18).
- **to thought.** It mounts "to his thought," and "causes his f[ai]th and his truth to le[a]ve him" (98.23–26).
- **to mind.** Sin "shall rise up and assume his mind; and disturb his mind, which formerly was calm. It shall disrupt his love... It shall take from his heart love of the church, and fill it full of hatred" (99.2–6).

The order of the loss is the order of the binding read from the outside in. Sin takes the outermost member first and the innermost last, as Resolution observed. What the text says about the person at each stage is about a member leaving a body:

> He separates from his teacher and his brethren. [He sh]all always [w]ant to go in and to come out alone. He shall want to eat and to drink alone, a solitary man. [He sh]all always want to walk alone. Indeed, this is the [si]gn that the familiarity of his brethren does not act on him. (K. 98.18–22)

At the end "that person shall [himse]lf become a vessel of loss, and he separates fr[om] the church... The Mind, who was in him, sh[a]ll disperse from him and go to the apos[t]le who sent him... He becomes an earthly man" (99.10–17). A member who wants to eat, drink and walk alone has ceased to be a member. The text's sign of the fall is not a doctrinal error but separation from the body.

**The three images.** Chapter 114 adds the vertical dimension to the same person. "Thr[ee] images occur in the elect person":

- "the [s]piritual image, which is the new man, which the Light Mind shall form in him; and it goes [i]n to him and dwells in him";
- "the rem[n]ant and remainder of the new man, which is the p[sy]chic image that is bound in the flesh, as the old man";
- "the [corp]oreal image [that is] added to them all" (269.18–25).

The living soul that enters the elect rises through the three images. It "shall divest itself of the body, which is not its own, in the corporeal." It divests itself "of the souls that are not its own" in the psychic, namely "anger and desire and... foolishness and envy and strife." In the spiritual image it is "joined with [lo]ng-sufferingness, the perfection of faith and love that reigns over them all" (270.13–21). There a light virgin "scu[lpt]s it and adorns it with the new man [wi]t[hi]n. It is sealed with all the limbs of this light vir[gin] who is present and dwells in the new man. So, this is [h]ow this living limb shall be [puri]fied and live" (270.4–9).

Two verbs in this passage matter for §9: the new man is "sculpted," and the purified soul is "this living limb." Gardner's summary calls the light virgin here "the manifestation of the soul in all her chastity and power" (p. 275).

**Accommodation.** One more passage of chapter 38 answers the disciple's question about how a great god fits into a small body, and it does so with the body's own logic. The guardians of the universe, "while they are great, [y]et have they contorted themselves... they have become small, to the measure of the task [th]at is apportioned to each o[n]e [of t]hem. Again, this too is like [the] Mind, himse[lf] a great one, and exalted; but he is bent over and [has] become small, to this little worthless [bo]dy" (99.25–29).

Once accommodated, the Mind does in the body what the guardians do in the universe. "Behold the mightiness and the activity of the Light Mind: how vast he is over all the watch-districts of the body!" (100.1–2). The elect one, "as he stands on the earth," may be found "[r]ising up in his heart and ascending to the Father," or descending "in his teaching and his consideration" to the land of darkness; "his heart shall run and touch everything" (100.8–14). But the Mind can be seen only by one who can see him: "Whoever has an open and percept[ive] eye, [he can] appear to him" (100.16–17).

**Spiritual reading.** These passages are the *Kephalaia*'s doctrine of regeneration, and they are written entirely in the body.

The binding of the five faculties into the five tissues is the human mind incarnated in the order of degrees. The inmost of the mind, which receives love, is lodged in the inmost frame of the body, and the outermost of the mind, which meets the world, is lodged in its surface. Swedenborg's account of the tissues agrees in an unexpected detail. The bones and the skin correspond to societies of "little spiritual life," "just as there is little life in the bones as compared with the soft parts which they enclose" (*Arcana Coelestia* §5560). The highest faculty therefore lies bound in the tissue of least life. That is exactly what "binding" means: the inmost held in the most fixed, the living soul in the bone. Release is not escape from the body but the reversal of that condition, so that the bone and the skin become the ordered ultimates of a living interior instead of its prison. Swedenborg describes the result for the outermost tissue. In the regenerate, the skins appear as "a beautiful weaving of spirals wonderfully intertwined," and in the deceitful, as "knottings together of mere serpents" (§5559).

The overlay of sin's five members on the soul's five is the proprium in its opposite sense. It is not a second nature added from nowhere. It is a counterfeit of each faculty laid over the true one: a mind of sin over the mind, a thought of sin over the thought. The regenerating work does not destroy the faculties of sin but binds them. It loosens the soul's members and fastens the counterfeit in the place the soul has left. This is the framework's account of the selfhood: a vessel which, while it claims what flows through it, binds the life it holds, and which, when it is brought into order, becomes the ultimate in which that life stands.

The new man is the regenerate person as a body. Its five members are love, faith, perfection, patience and wisdom, set in mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration, and its king is the Light Mind. This is what Swedenborg calls a man "in exact proportion to what they have from Him" (*Arcana Coelestia* §4219). The new man is a man because each of his members has received the Mind's corresponding member, and he reigns because the Mind reigns in him. A new man of five members with the Mind as king is an angel "in least form," "a recipient... a heaven, and an angel" (*Heaven and Hell* §73).

The fall is the reverse, and the text describes it as Swedenborg describes the separation of the heterogeneous. Sin enters at the outermost faculty and works inward until it reaches love. At every stage the person withdraws from the body of the church: he wants to eat alone, to walk alone, to go in and out alone. This is the love of self as a member's desire to be a whole by itself. Swedenborg's rule for the Greatest Man is that "those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (*Heaven and Hell* §64), and that whoever wills good to others "not for the sake of the common good but for the sake of self" does not perform use (§64). The *Kephalaia*'s separated member becomes "an earthly man," "a vessel of loss," and the Mind that was in him returns to the one who sent it.

The three images of chapter 114 are the discrete degrees of the one person: the corporeal, the psychic and the spiritual. The soul ascends through them by putting off at each level what is "not its own." What it puts off in the psychic image is anger, desire, envy and strife: the loves of self in their natural forms. What it is joined with in the spiritual image is long-suffering, "the perfection of faith and love that reigns over them all."

The accommodation of the great Mind to "this little worthless body" is the Divine's way with every receiver. The Divine makes itself small "to the measure of the task," as the guardians do in the universe. Swedenborg's "everyone is as it were the center of all, for he is the center of influxes from all through the heavenly form" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3633) is the same thought from the other side. The whole can be present in the least because the least has the form of the whole.

## 8. The Perfect Man: Column and Body, Trunk and Head

One figure in the *Kephalaia* has two names, and the text almost never uses one without the other. Gardner's index lists the passages in which "the Pillar of Glory" occurs and those in which "the Perfect Man" occurs. In six passages the two titles stand together as one designation: "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" (20.15; 35.10; 37.3; 92.6; 155.11; 177.4). In a seventh the Pillar is restored beside a preserved "Perfect Man" (24.22–23). The text treats the column and the man as one thing. This section asks what each name contributes, and why the text will not let them part.

**The column.** As a pillar, the figure bears weight and carries souls upward. Its titles make both functions explicit:

- "the one who bears up under all things; the great Pillar of blessing; the great porter, who is greater than all the porters" (35.10–13);
- "the first rock," which "bore up under the [en]tire uni[verse], and became first of all the weight-carriers in [it]s powers. In its firmness it has been set upright, and has made fast every[t]hing above and below" (155.10–16);
- "helper of the Keeper of Splendour. It bore up under all [burden]s" (93.21–22).

Its other function is passage. In chapter 2 it is the second limb of the good tree, the counsel through which the souls rise from the church toward the ships and the Father (20.14–15; 20.24–25). In chapter 71, on "the Gathering in of the [E]lements," it is a station in the chain of ascent: "the summons [to the] purified Mind, which is the intellectual. The intellectual to [the P]illar of Glory, [the Pillar of Glory to the First M]an, the Man to the Ambassador, the Ambassador [to] the aeons of greatness" (176.2–6).

Gardner summarises the doctrine in his introduction. The freed light "ascends up the Pillar of Glory, the great porter of souls, to the moon" (p. xxxii). In his notes he describes the light elements as they "finally ascend perfected from the Pillar to the moon and thence to the sun" (p. 41), and the soul's path as "to ascend up the Pillar to the moon (the First Man), then to the sun (the Ambassador), and thus to the aeons" (p. 185).

**The man.** As a man, the same figure has a body, a mind and a head:

- In the universe of chapter 38 it is the Mind: "The Ambassador placed in them the great Mind; who is the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" (92.5–6).
- In chapter 72 it has a body perfected by the faculties: "The garments that are named 'great garment' are the five intellectuals that [make per]fect the body of the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" (177.2–4).
- Chapter 74 places a fire "on the body of the Perfect Man" (181.10–11).
- In the elements, "the happiness is [the Pillar of Glory], the Perfect Man" (24.21–23).
- Called "father," its "greatness" is "the five gods" in which it lives (64.5–7).
- It is the place where Jesus became visible: among the vehicles he boarded, "the third is the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man, since he shone forth there. He came below, he appeared in the world" (37.3–4).

It has a head, and the head is the First Man. Chapter 31, "Concerning the Summons, in which Limb of the Soul it descended to the First Man," gives the anatomy plainly:

> After the fashion of this fleshly body: as the root of all the lim[bs] hang upon the head, so that should one of the person's limbs be cut off, while the head exists he has hope for [ ... ] but if his head should be cut off, the entir[e] body [w]ill d[ie] and he is lost. This is also the case for the Firs[t] Ma[n]. He is the head, while his sons attach to it the l[i]mbs [of] his soul. Or, conversely, like the l[i]ving air, on [w]hich all flesh entirely lives as it breathes... His head is placed on the body of the Pill[ar of Gl]ory in the heights of the universe. (K. 84.21–30)

"Pill[ar of Gl]ory" stands at a line-break with both ends preserved, and the restoration is certain. The chapter continues with the First Man's five faculties, "the mind, thought, teaching, counsel and c[on]sideration that he produced and sent forth from hi[m to] do his will," which, entwined among the rebels, are "like the limbs of its body" (85.1–5). Then the gathering is described as a building: "The Man him[s]elf... gathered his soul i[n ... ] he built it in its place like this to[w]er... shaped it and beautified it skillfully... They were gathered in. They came, [s]et firm onc[e] more, in the image of their father" (85.11–18).

The chapter begins by citing a scripture "about the Man that he spread himself through the [ ... ] aeon as to a wooden house" (84.12–13). Gardner's commentary draws the whole picture into four sentences. "The First Man is the head upon which the soul depends for its life." "The Man gathers in the soul, and builds it up, so that as his trunk it eschatologically ascends back to the land of light." "This trunk is the Pillar of Glory which reunites with the First Man at its head." "At the end of time it is the Last Statue" (p. 86).

The two titles are therefore exact. As a pillar the figure is the column of ascent, set upright from earth to heaven, bearing the universe and carrying the souls up through its stories to the ships. As a man it is a body under a head, perfected by five faculties, with the First Man's head set upon it in the heights. Gardner, reading historically, gives the Pauline source of the second title: "It is the Perfect Man (see Ephesians 4:12–13), the representation of the church as the body of Jesus in its ascent to the Father" (p. xxxii). The Ephesians passage speaks of the building up of "the body of Christ: till we all come... unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fulness of Christ." It continues that the church is to "grow up into him in all things, which is the head... from whom the whole body fitly joined together and compacted by that which every joint supplieth... maketh increase of the body" (Ephesians 4:12–16).

**One figure in two orders.** The reason the text does not separate the two names is given most clearly by Swedenborg's account of the two orders of the degrees. In successive order the degrees "may be compared to a column divided into three stories, through which ascent and descent are made. In the upper rooms are things most perfect and most beautiful; in the middle rooms, things less perfect... in the lowest, things still less perfect." Simultaneous order "has another appearance": the degrees are as "if in a solid body composed of these three degrees: in the middle or center are the finest parts, round about this are parts less fine, and in the extremes which constitute the circumference are the parts composed of these" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). It is, he says, "like the column mentioned just above subsiding into a plane, the highest part of which forms the innermost of the plane" (§205).

The *Kephalaia*'s Pillar is the column: "set upright," it carries the light from the earth through the moon and the sun to the aeons. Its Perfect Man is the solid body: the great Mind at its centre, the five intellectuals perfecting it, the head upon it. The text gives one figure both of Swedenborg's descriptions and calls it by both names. The column is the regenerating soul's path seen in time, stage above stage. The man is the same reality seen whole, with every stage present at once as a member.

This is the distinction the framework draws between seven as a sequence and seven as a regenerated body (§2.4), and the text draws it in a title.

**Spiritual reading.** The Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man, is the body of the regenerate. The framework would say: the church in heaven and on earth, gathering under its head. The First Man, who descended into the darkness, was overcome and was raised, is read in the library's companion theses as the Divine Human in its descent and combat. Here he is the head, and the Pillar is the body that hangs from him.

This is Swedenborg's order exactly: "The body spiritual is the church; its head is God-Man... the one God is the head, and the church is the body, which acts under the command of the head, and not from itself" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §24). The First Man's head is "placed on the body... in the heights of the universe," as "the highest or third heaven forms the head" of the Greatest Man (*Heaven and Hell* §65). The limbs live from the head. A limb may be lost and the person survive, but if the head is cut off "the entir[e] body [w]ill d[ie]." This is the *Kephalaia*'s form of the doctrine that "the Lord alone is Man," and that the members are men "in exact proportion to what they have from Him" (*Arcana Coelestia* §4219).

That the Pillar "bore up under the [en]tire universe" and "made fast every[t]hing above and below" is the doctrine that the world subsists from heaven. "It is from this correspondence that man has permanent existence, for from heaven alone does man have permanent existence" (*Heaven and Hell* §94). The Greatest Man does not float above the world. It is the form by which the world is held in being, as a person's body is held together by its life.

That the Pillar is "the great porter of souls" says that the same form is the way by which the purified life of the world returns to its source. The great Mind placed at the centre of the universe (92.5–6) is the presence of the Divine Human in the natural degree: the Mind in the world, as the Light Mind is the Mind in the person.

Finally, the Perfect Man is not yet complete. Its body is "perfected" by the five intellectuals as they are purified (177.2–4), and the gathered soul is "built... like this tower," "set firm once more, in the image of their father" (85.11–18). In the time of mixture the Pillar is the Greatest Man in formation. Swedenborg saw such a formation in a single society, "at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man" (*Heaven and Hell* §68). What the Pillar becomes at the end is the subject of the next section.

## 9. The Last Statue: The Body Perfect in All Its Limbs

The *Kephalaia* ends its history of the world with a body. Gardner's index lists "the Last Statue" fifteen times across the codex and "the Statue" nine times more. One of the first and two of the second rest only on restoration (Gardner, index, s.v.). The figure appears whenever the text reaches the end of a series. It also marks the end of time in the chapter on the three seasons, in the chapter on the three days and the two deaths, in the chapter on the elements that weep, and in the chapter on the sun's setting.

The Coptic uses the Greek loanword *andrias*, the ordinary word for a statue of a human figure. Gardner's summary of the doctrine in his introduction is that "this soul will gather up and rise as the Last Statue, a divinity that in essence recapitulates the descent into the abyss of the First Man" (p. xxxiii). His comment on chapter 31 places it at the end of the line traced in §8: the gathered soul is the First Man's "trunk," "this trunk is the Pillar of Glory which reunites with the First Man at its head. At the end of time it is the Last Statue" (p. 86).

The fullest description is in chapter 16, where the counsel of life is the fifth of the five powers that "went forth against the Darkness":

> T[hen the summons and the obed]ience, the great counsel that came [to] the el[emen]ts, which are set in conjunction. It [mixed w]ith them, i[t was es]tablished [i]n silence. It bears u[p ... ] un[til the end t]ime when it can arise and stand f[ir]m in the g[reat] fire. It will gather to it its own soul, and sc[ul]pt it in the Last Sta[t]ue. You will a[l]so find it sweeps out and casts from it the polluti[on] that is foreign to it. However, the life and the li[gh]t that are in all things it gathers in to it, and builds upon its body. The time when this Last Statue will be perfect in all its limbs, then it can become free and ascend from that great struggle thr[ough] the Living Spirit its father, the one who comes and brings a [ ... l]imb. He raises it up from within this gathering(?), the melting do[wn] and destruction of all things. (K. 54.9–24)

Every verb in this passage describes the forming of a body:

- **gathering.** It "will gather to it its own soul"; it gathers in "the life and the li[gh]t that are in all things."
- **sculpting.** It will "sc[ul]pt it in the Last Sta[t]ue."
- **building.** It "builds upon its body."
- **expelling.** It "sweeps out and casts from it the polluti[on] that is foreign to it."
- **completing.** Only "the time when this Last Statue will be perfect in all its limbs" is the time when "it can become free and ascend."

The father who brings it up comes bearing "a [ ... l]imb." The completion of the body is the condition of the ascent, not a consequence of it.

Mani follows the description with a simile, and the simile explains how a body can be gathered out of everything without being everything:

> Now, th[is co]unsel, tha[t] summons <and> obedience, resembles thus cooled butter. If a pers[o]n will take an[d se]t it in hot milk, it melts [dow]n and soak[s] in with this hot milk. It m[elts] in another [time i]n silence and quiet to the mids[t ... ] yet also, of the power and the taste of this m[ilk ... ] is drawn to (the butter), and (the milk) sets and becomes [cold ... this mi]lk it shall clarify and discard. However, the oil and the grease and the "beauty" of the [m]ilk it shall gather in to it. Again, this too is what the advent of that thought is like; the counsel of life, which is the summons and [o]bedience that came to the el[e]ments. This is the one that at the end c[an a]rise and stand firm, and will gather [i]n to it all [the] lives, a[nd] the light that has [remai]ned behind [in] everything. It can bui[ld] them upon [it]s body, it will [gath]er them in to it [ ... ] in its likeness for ever and ever. (K. 54.24–55.11)

The butter dissolves into the milk "in silence and quiet." As the milk cools, the butter draws to itself what is of its own kind, "the oil and the grease and the 'beauty' of the [m]ilk," and lets the rest go. The counsel does the same with the world. It is mixed into everything without noise, and at the end it draws to itself every life and every light of its own kind and builds them "upon [it]s body... in its likeness for ever."

The same doctrine recurs through the book, always in the same terms:

- **the remnant.** In the chapter on the five things, "the fift[h] is the way that the Last Statue will be sculpted [from] the remnant of all th[in]gs. The light separates [ou]t to its place, and goes up and rules in its kingdom" (75.24–26). In the chapter on the three days, "the remainder left behind there can build and add to that Last Statue" (104.3–5).
- **the judge.** The twelfth judge is "the great counsel," which "makes a separation between the good and the evil ones. At the end also it can gather itself together and sculpt its own self in the Last Statue" (81.1–6).
- **sunset.** In the chapter on the Ambassador, sunset is a sign of the end, "for, when all the light will be purified and redeemed i[n] the universe at the last, the collector of all things, the Last Statue, will gather in and sculpt itself. It is the last hour of the day, the time when the Last Statue will go up to the aeo[n of] light" (165.5–11).
- **the span of history.** History as a whole is measured by the Statue. It runs "from the coming down of the Fi[rst Man] till the going up of the Last Statue" (71.15–16), and the three seasons run "to the season when the Statue will ascend an[d] the entire light... will be puri[fied]" (55.20–23).

What is not gathered is severed. In chapter 59 the elements, "the sons of the First Man," weep four times, and the fourth weeping, at the end, is the worst. It is for the souls of whom the text says that "their limbs have been severed":

> [The f]ourth time when they will weep is when the [S]tatue will be taken up on the last day, and they will weep [for] the souls of the liars and blasphemers... because their limbs have been severed [ ... ] of the darkness. And also [th]ose souls, when the Statue will go [up and th]ey are left alo[n]e, they will weep in that they will rema[in] b[eh]ind in affliction for ever. For they wi[ll be cut off] and separated from the Last Statue... They go i[n to] this [darkne]ss and are bound with the darkness; just as they desired it an[d] loved it, and placed [t]heir treasure with it. At that very moment, when the Last Statue rises u[p], the[y wi]ll weep. And then they will scream out loud because they will be severed from the company of this great Statue. (K. 149.29–150.11)

The same loss is the "second death" of chapter 39. Sinful souls are "stripped of the shining light," "separated from the living air, from which they receive [livin]g bre[ath]," and "separated and swept away from this Last Statue" (104.6–12). Gardner puts the theology plainly: "Some of the divine itself can never be redeemed" (p. 156).

Read as a whole, the Last Statue doctrine says:

- The end of history is a single human figure.
- It is built from everything living that has been gathered out of the world.
- It is sculpted by the same counsel that was mixed into the world at the beginning.
- It is completed limb by limb, and it rises only when it is "perfect in all its limbs."
- It casts out what is "foreign to it."
- Those souls not built into it are "severed" from it as limbs are severed from a body. They go where "they desired... and loved... and placed their treasure."

This is the body the Father's limbs went out to rescue (§3), the body the Pillar carried through the time of mixture (§8), and the body each new man was a member of (§7). It is now complete.

[*The Ancient Word Recovered*](../00_Framework/The%20Ancient%20Word%20Recovered_%20Extracting%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) named the Last Statue the culminating concept of the substrate, a completed cosmic-human body built from gathered and purified substance (§8.3, Finding 19). The chapters read here confirm that judgement in Gardner's text. The confirmation does not depend on any restored reading, since "perfect in all its limbs," "builds upon its body," "sculpt its own self in the Last Statue," and "severed from the company of this great Statue" all stand in preserved text.

**Spiritual reading.** The Last Statue is the Greatest Man completed: heaven as a single man, formed from all who have received life and have become what they received. Every feature the text gives it is a feature Swedenborg gives to heaven's human form.

*It is one body formed from many.* Heaven "in its whole complex reflects a single man" (*Heaven and Hell* §59). The Statue is built from "the life and the light that are in all things."

*It is formed gradually and completes itself.* Swedenborg watched a society take the human form: "at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man" (§68). The Statue is "sculpted" through the whole time of mixture and rises only when it is "perfect in all its limbs." The *Kephalaia*'s verb for this is sculpting, and its noun is a statue of a man. In Swedenborg's telling the inmost of a person "conspires" toward the human form from the very beginning: "the primitive of man... endeavors toward the formation of the whole man in the ovum and the womb" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3633). The counsel of life, mixed silently into the world at the start like butter into milk, is the inmost of the whole conspiring toward its form. The Statue is what the counsel was always becoming.

*It gathers what is of its own kind and lets the rest go.* The simile of the butter is exact on this point. The butter takes "the oil and the grease and the 'beauty'" and discards the rest. Swedenborg's societies are "conjoined according to uses in a like form," and "those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (*Heaven and Hell* §64). In the society he watched forming, "those that were in that man and made up the man were such as were in the good of that society; the others who were not in the man and did not make up the man were hypocrites; these were cast out" (§68). The Statue "sweeps out and casts from it the pollution that is foreign to it." "Foreign" is the *Kephalaia*'s word for Swedenborg's "heterogeneous."

*Those who are severed go where they loved.* The text insists that the lost go into the darkness "just as they desired it and loved it, and placed their treasure with it." This is Swedenborg's doctrine that the two loves of self and the world "constitute the hells" as love to the Lord and the neighbour constitute the heavens (*Heaven and Hell* §554). The severing is not an external sentence carried out on unwilling members. A member severed from the body is one whose love was elsewhere. The weeping of the elements for them is the grief of the whole for the parts that would not be parts, which the text calls the worst of all its weepings.

*It is the Divine's own image completed.* The counsel builds the gathered lives "upon its body... in its likeness for ever and ever," and Gardner reads the Statue as recapitulating "the descent... of the First Man." The body that rises is the image of the head that descended. Swedenborg's "it is from the Lord's Divine Human that heaven as a whole and in part reflects man" (*Heaven and Hell* §78) is the same claim: the completed heaven has the human form because its source is the Divine Human, and its completion is the restoration of that image in what was received. On the third great day the Father will "perfect their stature in sa[m]e measure as his first aeons" (103.20–21).

## 10. Many Names, One Form

The bodies traced in §§3–9 carry many names: the Father, the five fathers, the First Man and his sons, the Living Spirit and his sons, the Ambassador, the Pillar of Glory, the Light Mind, the new man, the Last Statue. A reader could take them for a crowd. Two consecutive chapters near the middle of the codex say how the crowd is to be understood, and both answer in terms of one form.

Chapter 71, "Concerning the Gathering in of the [E]lements," gives the order in which everything will be gathered at the end:

> In what way shall [the] elements be gathered in, from one to another? The [lig]ht shall be gathered in [to] the fire, and the fire itself gathered [in to the water, and the wa]ter gathered in to the wind, and the [win]d gathered to the air, and the air gathered to the answer, [an]d the answer gathered to the summons. Also, the summons [to the] purified Mind, which is the intellectual. The intellectual to [the P]illar of Glory, [the Pillar of Glory to the First M]an, the Man to the Ambassador, the Ambassador [to] the aeons of greatness. They are gathered in like this; but, [they] will be gathered on one occasion only, and go up a[b]ove to the place of rest for ever and ever. (K. 175.29–176.8)

The chain has three movements:

- **The elements.** The five elements are gathered "from one to another": light into fire, fire into water, water into wind, wind into air.
- **The faculties of response.** Air is gathered into the answer, the answer into the summons, the summons into the purified Mind.
- **The persons.** The Mind is gathered into the Pillar, the Pillar into the First Man, the First Man into the Ambassador, the Ambassador into the aeons.

Each thing is taken up into what contains it, until the whole is in the aeons of greatness. The chain is told as a sequence, but the last sentence corrects the sequence: "they will be gathered on one occasion only." What was described step by step happens all at once. The successive order of the ascent resolves, at the end, into a single act, as a column resolves into a body (§8).

Gardner reads the chapter as "the eschatological path taken by each soul: to answer the summons of salvation, to become one with the Light Mind, to ascend up the Pillar to the moon (the First Man), then to the sun (the Ambassador), and thus to the aeons" (p. 185). The path of the one soul and the gathering of the whole are the same path.

Chapter 72 takes the names themselves as its subject. A disciple has heard the Teacher use many names for what seems to be one thing: "great garments," "rags," "worn and torn apart," "images and... tattered clothes," "light element," "Cro[ss] of Light," "couns[el] of life," "the soul that is slain." He asks: "What are all these names? Look, they are written and sealed, according to memory, by a multitude of scriptures... What are they? Or how have they changed with all these names and become set in altered forms?" (176.15–29).

The Teacher answers name by name. Each name is a state of one power, defined by where it is and what is happening to it:

| Name | What the Teacher says it is (K. 177.2–178.12) |
|---|---|
| "Great garment" | "the five intellectuals that [make per]fect the body of the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man"; purified by the Ambassador's coming |
| "Rags" | "the devoured enlightening power," "compounded and entangled in [the bo]dy of the universe" in the rulers of the firmaments and in the earths below |
| "Great worn and torn apart garments" | "this entangled living power," "reaped, cut, and devoured," "overpowered by the five worlds of the flesh" |
| "Images and the great tattered clothes" | "the three great garments of the wind, the fire, and the water," which "the Living Spirit cloaked upon his body" and with which "he set in order things in the universe" |
| "Elements" | "this [pow]er that remains in all things below," which "rises up [in] the wombs of all the earths, gathering together" |
| "[Light] Cross" | "this bound 'light power'" spread "upon the earth, in the dry and the moist" |
| "Counsel of life" | "the summons and the [obed]ience. It moves in all these powers that we have named. It awakens them, gives them ease, and drives them to their movement" |
| "The soul [that is s]lain" | "this power of fruit, vegetables and seeds," threshed, plucked and cut down |

Then the Teacher states what all of them are:

> For all these names [ ... ] are a single [ ... ] since the beginning, it [ ... ] as it enlightens; but they separated into all these parts in this first contest. They became set in all these altered forms, and these many names. Of course, if now all these var[iet]ies are laid bare, and stripped of all these appearances, [and] parted from all these names; they will gather together [and] make a single form, and a single name, unaltered and unchangeable for ever in the land of their first essence, from which they were sent forth against the enemy. (K. 178.13–23)

The noun after "a single" is lost in a lacuna, and this thesis does not supply it. The rest of the sentence is preserved, and it says enough. The many names are "parts" of one thing that "separated" at the beginning. They are "altered forms" and "appearances." Laid bare and "parted from all these names," they "will gather together [and] make a single form, and a single name."

Gardner's summary is that Mani "emphasises that they will all return to a single form and a single name at the final victory" (p. 186). [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md) read this chapter as the text's testimony to its own stratification, with names laid over a prior unity. The present thesis adds what that unity looks like.

Every name in the chapter is a garment, and the chapter's first answer says what the garments are for. The "great garment" is "the five intellectuals that [make per]fect the body of the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man." The garments of wind, fire and water are those "the Living Spirit cloaked upon his body." The power was "the armour (or the five sons) that the First Man had assumed at his descent," as Gardner notes (pp. 185–186). The garments are worn by a body. When the names are stripped, what remains is "a single form": not an abstraction or a substance, but the one body the garments clothed.

Chapter 63 has already given the rule for such unities. The Father and his love are "a single living body," and the Mind and the church are "a single body" (156.3–11). Chapter 39 has given the end: the Father will "perfect their stature" and set "each power [i]n its aeon, in its dwelling-place" (103.20–22). Chapter 16 has given its shape: the Statue "perfect in all its limbs" (54.19–20).

Chapter 72 adds that every name the book has used names a part of that form, in one of its states. The great garment is the form being perfected; the rags and the slain soul are the form devoured and scattered; the counsel of life is the form's own movement, which "awakens" its scattered parts. The single form at the end is the form the names were always parts of.

**Spiritual reading.** The doctrine of chapter 72 is Swedenborg's doctrine of the one and the many in the Greatest Man, stated from the side of language. Heaven is innumerable societies and angels, each with its own quality, yet it "reflects a single man," and the angels in it "act as a one" (*Heaven and Hell* §§59, 64). The reason is that "the goods of all are from a single love, that is, from a single origin" (§72). In the human body "many and numberless things" are felt "as one," because "all these are in such a form that not one can be lacking," a form "recipient of life from God-Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §22). Swedenborg draws the conclusion for the Divine itself: in God-Man "infinite things are one distinctly" (§22).

The *Kephalaia*'s many names are the one life received in many states. In the opening contest the life entered the natural degree and was received there in every condition: perfected in the regenerate, entangled in the firmaments, devoured by the flesh, spread through the dry and the moist, ripening in fruit and seed. Each condition received its own name, and the names were "written and sealed... by a multitude of scriptures." This is accommodation. The same reality is given the form in which each place and each people could receive it.

Laid bare, the many are one form, because the life that took all these forms was one life from one source, and its form is the form of its source. The "single name" that remains is the single quality of that life. "In the land of their first essence" the many return to what they were "since the beginning."

The chain of chapter 71 is the inward path by which this return happens. Each lower thing is gathered into the one that contains it: the elements into the response, the response into the Mind, the Mind into the body, the body into its head, the head into the source. It is the simultaneous order of *Divine Love and Wisdom* §205 seen as a movement, the outermost gathered into the inmost.

The chain's last word, "on one occasion only," says that the gathering is not finally a sequence of events. It is the coming together of a form that was already one in its source. That is why the end of the book is not a list but a statue: "a single form, and a single name, unaltered and unchangeable for ever."

## 11. What the Numbers Say: Five, Twelve and the Sums the Text Calls Forced

The *Kephalaia* counts on almost every page. Its chapter titles promise four days and four nights, five storehouses, fourteen vehicles, twelve judges, eighteen thrones and fifty lord's days. Section 2.4 fixed the reading these numbers receive in this thesis: a number names a quality of one whole; a composite number is read by its prime factors; nothing is read as a total of smaller sets. This section applies that reading to the two numbers that dominate the body, five and twelve. It also looks at what the text itself does when it tries to relate them. The finding is that the text uses numbers in two different ways, and that Gardner's own judgement separates them cleanly.

**Five is the faculty.** Wherever the *Kephalaia* speaks of the limbs of a body, there are five of them, and they are the same five. The table gathers the registers traced in §§3–8:

| Register | The five | Locus |
|---|---|---|
| The Father | "his five light limbs": mind, thought, teaching, counsel, [consideration] | 64.20–23 |
| The five fathers | from the Father's mind, thought, teaching, counsel, consideration | 76.20–24 |
| The good tree | consideration (church), counsel (Pillar), teaching (First Man), thought (Ambassador), mind (Father) | 20.11–31 |
| The bad tree | consideration, counsel, teaching, thought, [mind], each in its opposite | 21.28–36 |
| The King of Darkness | five shapes from five worlds | 30.33–31.2; 77.27–78.3 |
| The guardians of the universe | mind, thought, teaching, counsel, consideration | 91.19–31; 92.12–93.10 |
| The members of the elect | face, heart, genitals, stomach, feet | 172.4–20 |
| The soul in the flesh | mind in bone, thought in sinew, teaching in vein, counsel in flesh, consideration in skin | 95.16–19 |
| The new man | love, faith, perfection, patience, wisdom in mind, thought, teaching, counsel, consideration | 96.29–97.3 |
| The First Man's soul | "the mind, thought, teaching, counsel and c[on]sideration" | 85.1–2 |

The five is never a count of five separate things that happen to be grouped. It is one body's faculty in its radial order, from the inmost to the outermost. The same names recur at every register because the same form recurs. [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) drew this meaning of five from these chapters (§§5.3–5.4), and the survey here confirms it at every register in which the text gives a body.

**Twelve is the whole.** Where the *Kephalaia* names a twelve directly, it names the whole of a greatness:

- **the Father's "twelve light limbs that are his twelve wisdoms"** (64.24–25);
- **the Father's twelve "great rich Gods of great[ness]," which "he spread out to the four climes, three b[y] three before his face"** (25.15–19);
- **the Ambassador's "twelv[e v]irgins"** (25.20–22);
- **Jesus the Radiance's "twelve wisdoms"** (25.31–33);
- **the twelve judges, who "came from the great Judge"** (81.7–9).

In each case the twelve is the full complement of what belongs to one figure, the whole of his greatness in its articulation. That is what Swedenborg says twelve means: "the things of love and the derivative faith in one complex" (*Arcana Coelestia* §577); "this number, wherever it occurs in the Word, signifies all" (§3272).

The way the text spreads the Father's twelve is exact. It is not an assembly of twelve units but a whole arranged "to the four climes, three b[y] three": four quarters, each holding three. Read by its prime factors, twelve is two-by-two (the four quarters) and three (the three in each). The bilateral and the vertical cross to articulate one greatness. The text draws this as a figure, not a sum.

**Three runs through the five.** The *Kephalaia* does not set five against three. It places the three inside every register of the five, and the five inside the three.

Chapter 3, "The Interpretation of Happiness, Wisdom and Power," names three things — happiness, wisdom and power — and finds all three at each of five levels:

- the Father in the land of light;
- the ship of living fire;
- the ship of living waters;
- the elements;
- the holy church (23.31–25.2).

In the elements the three are themselves fivefold: "The happiness is [the Pillar of Glory], the Perfect Man. Wisdom is the [five sons of the] Living Spirit; and great powe[r is ... the fi]ve sons of the First Man" (24.21–25). The chapter closes on the whole: "[ ... ] five happinesses, the five wisdoms, [a]nd five powers [ ... ] in the five chur[ches]" (25.3–4).

Gardner calls this "a schema that will be repeated throughout the Kephalaia." In it "these principles are successively identified with the various levels of divinity (here five) as it descends into time and the universe" (p. 27). The library's edition heads the chapter "The Three Divine Attributes on Every Plane." It reads the three as blessedness, illumination and "their conjunction in act," "present at every successive degree of the mind" (*The Ancient Word* v1.1, p. 13).

The same interlocking appears wherever the text gives a body:

- The five watches of chapter 70 hold the threes within them. The Keeper of Splendour's watch has "authority over the three heavens," and the King of Glory's watch "is the three wheels," with authority "over the three earths" (170.23–171.7).
- The elect person whose five faculties are bound in five tissues also carries "thr[ee] images," the corporeal, the psychic and the spiritual (269.18–25).

This is Swedenborg's doctrine of degrees, not a departure from it. The three degrees are love, wisdom and use, "like first end, middle end which is called cause, and last end which is called effect" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §230). In chapter 3 they appear as happiness, wisdom and power. They are in everything, greatest and least: "there can be nothing so minute as not to have in it degrees of both kinds" (§223).

Five is a second structural quality of the same whole: the radial order of a faculty, which the text expresses in five aspects, mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration. Two is a third: good and truth, which Swedenborg gives as two expressions of one quality.

None of these is a count of separate parts, and none competes with another. They are the dimensions in which the spiritual mind perceives one living form. Swedenborg names the three exactly and leaves five vague, giving it only as "much" or "some," as [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) showed (§§5.2, 5.5). The *Kephalaia* names the five exactly and shows the three running through it. Read together, they describe one body.

**Where the text adds, Gardner finds it forced.** There are a few places where the *Kephalaia* does not name a twelve but tries to make one, by adding sets the system already has. Each time, Gardner, who has no interest in a correspondential reading of number, judges the result strained.

- **The third day.** In chapter 4 every one of the four great days must have twelve hours. For the first, second and fourth days the twelve are given directly: the Father's twelve gods, the Ambassador's twelve virgins, Jesus's twelve wisdoms. For the third day, the Pillar of Glory, the text builds the twelve by addition: "Its twelve hours are the five sons [of the F]irst Man; the five sons of the Living Sp[ir]it... together with the summons and the obedience, which are counted to their ten brethren. These are the twelve light hours of the th[ird da]y" (25.25–30). Gardner comments: "The schematic elaboration of days and hours is somewhat artificial... The purpose seems to be to enable Mani to link together a number of details around the theme of 'twelve'" (p. 29). Of the nights, whose twelve hours are again assembled from fives (26.3–17), he says they "appear mostly artificial, since Manichaean doctrine regarding the land of darkness and the worlds of flesh is dominated by pentads" (p. 29).
- **The five greatnesses.** In chapter 16 the text says that the four greatnesses that came from the Father "were appointed to ten parts, and established in total twelve" (49.17–19). It reaches the twelve by counting the five sons of the First Man and the five of the Living Spirit, and then "another two [ad]ded to them who are the summons and [the obedien]ce. They are the father and the mother" (49.32–50.3). Gardner calls this "an undoubtedly forced calculation" (p. 54).
- **The fifty days.** In chapter 109 the fifty fasts of the catechumens are derived from twenty-five limbs doubled. The fifty of the elect are derived from forty days in the desert, seven in the house of Simon the leper and three in the tomb (262.24–264.14). Gardner: "He engages in some rather convoluted calculations to reach these totals" (p. 268).

Gardner names the underlying difficulty in his note on the Father's limbs: the texts "have difficulty reconciling the sets of five and twelve that structure Mani's system" (p. 67). The sums are the text's attempt at a reconciliation. It shows that the pentads and the twelves are one by adding pentads until a twelve comes out. The attempt strains exactly as Gardner says, because it treats a whole as a total.

On the reading of §2.4 there is nothing to reconcile. Five is the faculty of the body and twelve is the body as a complex whole. The same body has both qualities, as a person has both five senses and a whole life, and neither has to be built out of the other.

This thesis therefore makes no structural claim that depends on these sums:

- It does not claim that the *Kephalaia* is built on a plan of five plus five plus two.
- It does not claim that the hours of the third day disclose an architecture.
- It does not claim that the fifty days encode a design.

It reports the sums, reports Gardner's judgement of them, and observes that the body traced in §§3–10 does not need them. They are the natural-degree rendering of a whole, assembled by a mind that perceives through counting: the spiritual mind's distinctions written down as arithmetic.

**Where the text multiplies, it describes the form.** The *Kephalaia* has a second, quite different way of handling number. It speaks not of adding sets but of a set within each member of another:

- the Father has his five light limbs, "and... there are five great light lim[bs] in each one" (64.25–26), the clause breaking off before it says in each of what;
- "In each one of these five garments that the [First] Man summonsed there are five powers" (262.25–26), which the text calls "twenty-five limbs" (263.3);
- the Father's twelve are spread "three b[y] three" in each of the four climes (25.18–19).

These are not totals. They describe one form repeated within its own members. The limb has the form of the body, five within each of five. The quarter has the form of the whole greatness, the degrees in each direction.

This is the property Swedenborg identifies as the mark of the most perfect form. "Generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals" (*Heaven and Hell* §62), and "there is a likeness of the whole in the part and of the part in the whole" (§73). Twenty-five is five seen within five: the faculty at a finer resolution, carrying the same meaning, as the framework's rule for powers of a prime requires (*Resolution* §7.2). Where the text uses number in this way it is not straining. It is describing the self-similarity of the human form, and this is the only numerical structure this thesis claims: the whole present in each part, not the whole assembled from parts.

**At the end, counting stops.** The *Kephalaia* itself says what becomes of number when the body is complete. On the third great day, when the Father has perfected the stature of all his powers and set "each power [i]n its aeon, in its dwelling-place," the text adds: "From then on they will not count that season amongst them; nor the number of the days, nor the hours, generation after generation, for [e]ver and ever. The Father will not be hidden from them, from [this] tim[e] on" (103.25–29). When the Father is no longer hidden, the days and hours are no longer counted. Number belongs to the time of mixture, to the mind that perceives the whole through distinction. When the whole is seen, it is not counted.

Swedenborg says the same of the angels of the inmost heaven: seven "in the internal sense partakes not at all of the idea of number. For they who are in the internal sense, as angels and angelic spirits are, do not even know what number is" (*Arcana Coelestia* §716). [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) calls this the return to one: the primes are scaffolding, "necessary during construction, removed when the building stands" (§7.3). The *Kephalaia*'s building is a statue, and when it stands the counting ends.

## 12. The Central Finding: One Human Form at Every Register

Section 2.3 drew seven propositions from Swedenborg's account of the Greatest Man and one historical claim, and asked whether each would be recognisable in the *Kephalaia*. They would not have been recognisable if the text had looked otherwise. Several outcomes would have counted against the thesis:

- the adversary as a formless force;
- the five limbs changing their names from register to register;
- the soul bound into the body in an arbitrary order;
- an end that dissolved all form instead of completing one;
- a gathering that took everything without distinction.

The text does none of these things. The table sets the propositions beside what the *Kephalaia* says in preserved text and what Gardner, reading historically, says about it.

**Every proposition is recognisable, and in preserved text.** None of the rows depends on a restored word for its argument-bearing clause. Where a key word is restored in one passage, as with the King's "lion-faced" head or the Pillar's name at 84.29, the same word is preserved in a parallel, and the parallel is cited. Gardner's own descriptions, written without any interest in Swedenborg, name the same features in each row: the realm as the Father's limbs; macrocosm and microcosm as one; the head and the trunk; the ladder of faculties; the gathered soul rising as a Statue; evil as an exact negative.

**Two features fit Swedenborg's account in detail rather than in general.** They are the strongest parts of the finding, because a general resemblance between two pictures of a cosmic man would be cheap, and these are not general:

- **The order of the tissues.** The soul's faculties are bound into the body in simultaneous order: the highest faculty in the innermost tissue, the lowest in the outermost. This is Swedenborg's second arrangement of the degrees, "in the inmost... in the middle... in the circumference" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). The text also has his first arrangement, the ladder, in its two trees, and it names one figure by both: a column and a man.
- **The completion of the body.** The Last Statue is completed by gathering what is like it and casting out what is "foreign to it," and those who are not gathered are severed "just as they desired it and loved it." This is Swedenborg's account of the formation of a society of heaven, in which the man appears "by degrees" and the heterogeneous are cast out because their love is for self (*Heaven and Hell* §§64, 68).

**Two apparent divergences dissolve when the text is read at its own level.**

*The first is number.* It can look as though Swedenborg divides the Greatest Man into three heavens — head, breast and feet (*Heaven and Hell* §65) — while the *Kephalaia* divides its great body into five watches (170.21–171.11). But neither text is dividing a space.

- **Swedenborg's three.** His three heavens are the three degrees of love, wisdom and use. They are present in every person "by creation" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §231) and in the least thing as in the greatest (§§223, 225).
- **The *Kephalaia*'s five.** Its five watches are the five faculties: one quality in five aspects.
- **The three inside the five.** The text places the three inside the five. The first watch holds "the three heavens"; the fourth holds "the three wheels" and "the three earths." Chapter 3 finds happiness, wisdom and power at each of five levels (§11).

Three and five are two structural qualities of one body, as good and truth are two expressions of one quality. Swedenborg names the three exactly and the five vaguely; the *Kephalaia* does the reverse.

*The second is attribution.* Chapter 38 says that the enemy "constructed" the universe "after the likeness of a man" (90.20–22). Swedenborg says that the universe represents man because God is a Man (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). But the *Kephalaia* does not give the enemy either the form or the life:

- The rulers reproduce "the shining form of the image that had been displayed to them above" (157.8–9).
- The life of the great body is the five gods "fastened in him," "his soul and his life" (91.15–16).
- The ruler of darkness has "no life... whatsoever" (78.31–32).

The form is shown from above and reproduced below, and the copy lives only with the life the light puts into it. This is Swedenborg's own physics of life: the Lord alone is life, and every form is a recipient of it (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §4). Read literally, the text says that darkness built the world. Read at the level of its own anatomy, it says what Swedenborg says.

**The central finding** can now be stated without qualification. The *Kephalaia of the Teacher*, read in Gardner's translation corrected to the Coptic, presents everything it describes in one human form. It does so at every scale: the Father, the realm of light, the two trees, the universe, Adam, the individual soul, the regenerate person, the church, the Pillar and the end. It presents the adversary as that form inverted. It orders the form's members from within outward and by use. It ends with the form completed by gathering and purged of what is foreign to it.

This is the Greatest Man as Swedenborg described it. The text was unknown in Europe until 1933, when the Medinet Madi find was announced, and it was published in 1940. Swedenborg described the Greatest Man between 1749 and 1763.

## 13. Discussion

### 13.1 What the Kephalaia Preserves

The first thing the *Kephalaia* preserves is the human form as a way of composing, not as one doctrine among others. Swedenborg states the Greatest Man as a doctrine, in a chapter of *Heaven and Hell* and in interchapters of the *Arcana*. The *Kephalaia* nowhere states it in a single place, yet the form governs every subject the book takes up. Asked about the Father, the text gives limbs. Asked about a parable, it gives two bodies. Asked about the adversary, it gives a monster with five shapes. Asked about the universe, it gives a man from head to feet. Asked how a god fits into a person, it binds the faculties into the tissues. Asked about the end, it gives a statue. A body is the form the text's thinking takes when it thinks about anything.

The second thing it preserves is the five as faculty, consistently named. Swedenborg's doctrine of degrees is threefold, and Scripture, as [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) showed, leaves the meaning of five open (§5.2). The *Kephalaia* gives five a fixed content: mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration, always in that radial order. It holds that content steady across ten registers, from the Father to the new man (§11). Where Swedenborg tells us that the degrees stand within one another in the body, the *Kephalaia* tells us which faculty stands in which tissue. It does not set this five against Swedenborg's three. It runs the three through the five: happiness, wisdom and power at each of five levels, and three heavens, three wheels and three earths within the five watches (§11). Swedenborg is exact about the three and vague about the five; the text is exact about both and shows how they interlock.

The third thing is both orders of the degrees, kept distinct and joined in one figure. The ladder of the good tree and the solid body of chapter 38 are the same five faculties arranged successively and simultaneously. The Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man, is a single figure with a name for each arrangement. Swedenborg describes both orders and the relation between them in *Divine Love and Wisdom* §205. The *Kephalaia* writes the relation into its titles.

The fourth is the dynamics of the body. Swedenborg gives the form of heaven and describes, in one vivid scene, a society taking that form and casting out its hypocrites (*Heaven and Hell* §68). The *Kephalaia* gives much more on how a body is made and unmade:

- the binding of the soul into the flesh, member by member;
- its release by the Light Mind, member by member;
- the reconstruction as a new man with five living members and a king;
- the loss of that new man from the outside in, until the member wants to eat, drink and walk alone;
- the gathering of all living things into the Statue, the casting out of what is foreign, the severing of limbs;
- the weeping of the whole for the parts that would not be parts.

None of this is in conflict with Swedenborg; much of it is more detailed than anything he wrote.

The fifth is the anatomy of the anti-body. Swedenborg was not shown the form of hell as a whole, and he says so (*Heaven and Hell* §553). The *Kephalaia* describes it twice, and its description has the features Swedenborg's principles predict:

- **dissimilar parts.** Each member comes from a different world.
- **no interiors.** "Their heart is not manifested to him."
- **no life of its own.** Its only life is "the bile of anger."
- **made from outside.** It is sculpted by "the thought of death."
- **a lump.** It tends to collapse into a single mass.

It also places the members of that body in the present: in tyranny, in idolatry, in soothsaying, and in whoever lets its shapes be "laid upon" him.

The sixth is the principle of microcosm and macrocosm, stated as a principle: "This whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body"; "this is how the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos" (169.28–170.17). Swedenborg wrote that the ancients called man a microcosm "from truth which they derived from the knowledge of correspondences" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). The *Kephalaia* is a text in which that truth is still taught as the ancients taught it. It is taught, moreover, as Gardner noticed, with an "equation of the internal mental process of salvation with the eschatological journey through the heavens" that "indicates a living spirituality... that is often missed by commentators" (p. 22).

### 13.2 What Swedenborg Supplies

What Swedenborg supplies is the reason for the form. The *Kephalaia* shows the human form everywhere but does not say why everything should have it. Its own answer is given in the language of its myth. The image is displayed from above, the powers below reproduce it, and the life in every copy is the light's (§§6, 12). Swedenborg states the same answer as doctrine. "In all the heavens there is no other idea of God than that He is a Man," and "from this fact that God is a Man, all angels and all spirits, in their complete form, are men. This results from the form of heaven, which is like itself in its greatest and in its least parts" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §11). The human form is everywhere because it is the form of the Divine love and wisdom. Whatever receives that love and wisdom receives them in that form.

This is why the *Kephalaia*'s Father is a body, why the realm of light is "an extension of himself," and why each father lives in a greatness that is his body. It is also why the same form can be found in the universe, in Adam and in the regenerate person: each receives the same life and takes the form of what it receives.

Swedenborg supplies, second, the principle that makes the self-similarity intelligible. The parts of heaven are like the whole "because in the heavens the goods of all are from a single love, that is, from a single origin" (*Heaven and Hell* §72). The *Kephalaia* shows the self-similarity — five within each of five, three in each of four quarters, a father and his greatness at every level — without naming its cause. Swedenborg's single love is the cause. A text whose every register repeats the same form is a text that knows, whether or not it says so, that every register lives from one source.

Third, he supplies the key that makes the correspondences readable: use. "The image itself is not seen when these things are viewed in their forms, it is seen only when they are viewed in respect to uses" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §322). Read by use, the *Kephalaia*'s assignments are exact:

- the genitals answer to the guardian who "subdues Matter";
- the stomach answers to the one who "turns the wheels, sending the life above";
- the tongue is the one who "reveals about everything";
- the bones hold the mind, and the skin holds the consideration.

Read by position, some of them look arbitrary, which is why commentary that reads them as an anatomy chart finds the text confused.

Fourth, he supplies the reason the adversary has the same form. Hell is made of human beings, "everyone of them... a devil in accord with his opposition to the Divine" (*Heaven and Hell* §544), and its forms "were all forms of the love of self and the love of the world" (§554). Evil has no form of its own to give. It can only take the human form and turn every member against its use. That is what the *Kephalaia*'s bad tree and its King are: the five faculties turned the other way, and five members from five worlds, each a human use perverted.

Fifth, he supplies the account of number that dissolves the text's one persistent strain. The *Kephalaia* tries to reconcile its fives and its twelves by addition, and Gardner rightly finds the results forced. Swedenborg's rule, that numbers in the internal sense signify things and not quantities and that the angels "do not even know what number is" (*Arcana Coelestia* §716), shows why the strain arises. Two qualities of one body were being treated as two counts to be made equal. The *Kephalaia* itself knows that counting ends when the Father is unveiled (103.25–29). Swedenborg's doctrine explains why it must.

Finally, Swedenborg supplies an independent witness. He derived the Greatest Man from his own experience and published it between 1749 and 1763. He could not have known the *Kephalaia*. Where his account and the text agree in detail — in the two orders of the degrees, in the completion that casts out the heterogeneous, in hell as one inverted form — the agreement is between two derivations that share no literary channel. It is not between a source and its copy.

### 13.3 Implications for the Ancient Word Hypothesis

The Ancient Word hypothesis, as the library develops it, holds that a body of knowledge written in correspondences existed before the Hebrew scriptures. It holds that this knowledge was carried by the *Bene Qedem*, and that it survives in part in the substrate of the *Kephalaia*. [*The Ancient Word Recovered*](../00_Framework/The%20Ancient%20Word%20Recovered_%20Extracting%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) argued for the third claim. [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md) argued that the architecture the substrate carries is older than Mani.

The present thesis adds a specific test of the hypothesis, drawn from Swedenborg himself. He did not only say that an Ancient Word existed. He said what its people knew: that "man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," and that the ancients knew this "from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319).

If the *Kephalaia* substrate is a remnant of that knowledge, the human form should govern it, and it should govern it in Swedenborg's sense rather than as a loose analogy. The previous sections show that it does. This is a prediction that could have failed. A text can describe a cosmos without a body. A text can mention body imagery without ordering it by use, or without giving the adversary the inverted form. The *Kephalaia* does none of these.

Three further observations bear on the hypothesis.

**First, the form is not the property of one layer.** It governs chapters the library's stratigraphy treats as substrate, such as the body–universe correspondence of chapters 38 and 70. It also governs material Gardner attributes directly to Mani: the pentads of darkness, whose "stamp... is unmistakably Mani's" (p. 34). And it governs passages whose vocabulary is openly Pauline, such as "the new man" (p. xxxi) and "the Perfect Man," which Gardner glosses with Ephesians 4:12–13 (p. xxxii).

A form that runs through all three layers cannot be credited to the wording of any one of them. The names belong to their layers; the form belongs to all of them. *The Garment and What Wears It* took its title from chapter 72's image of names as garments. The present finding is that what wears the garments is a body.

**Second, the Pauline names are evidence for the form, not against its antiquity.** It might be objected that "the new man" and "the Perfect Man" are Pauline, and that the body structure of the *Kephalaia* is therefore borrowed from Paul. But the Ephesians passage Gardner cites is itself a statement of the same form: a body "fitly joined together" that "maketh increase of the body," growing "unto the measure of the stature of the fulness of Christ" under its head (Ephesians 4:13–16). Paul did not invent the human form of the church; he is one more witness to it.

The *Kephalaia* goes beyond Paul in the respects that matter to this thesis:

- the faculties are named and ordered;
- the cosmos is the same body;
- the adversary is the inverse body;
- the completed body is gathered from all living things.

The overlap with Paul shows that the form was available to more than one writer of the period. It does not show that Paul was the source.

**Third, the form is independently attested on the Iranian side.** [*The Dāmdād Nask and the Bundahišn*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20D%C4%81md%C4%81d%20Nask%20and%20the%20Bundahi%C5%A1n_%20A%20Philological%20Reconstruction%20of%20the%20Lost%20Sasanian%20Encyclopedia.md) reconstructs a Zoroastrian doctrine of the "small world" and the "great world." It maps the human body onto the universe in *Bundahišn* chapter 28, and it notes that Mani gave the same correspondence a negative valuation at the level of his myth (§§IV, 4.3; see §13.4). [*The Book of Zoroaster and the Children of the East*](The%20Book%20of%20Zoroaster%20and%20the%20Children%20of%20the%20East_%20Material%20Evidence%20for%20the%20Ancient%20Word%20Transmission%20Hypothesis%20from%20the%20Nag%20Hammadi%20Apocryphon%20of%20John.md) and [*The Unnamed Cartography*](The%20Unnamed%20Cartography_%20A%20Systematic%20Validation%20of%20Swedenborgian%20Body-Part%20Correspondences%20Against%20the%20365-Angel%20List%20of%20the%20Apocryphon%20of%20John.md) study a body-creation list in the Sethian Apocryphon of John that the text itself attributes to "the book of Zoroaster."

The *Kephalaia*, the *Bundahišn* and the Apocryphon belong to different communities. They agree that the human body and the universe are one form, and two of them point to a Persian source. The present thesis has not re-read the Iranian material and rests nothing on its details. It notes only that the human form it finds in the *Kephalaia* is not isolated.

The Ancient Word hypothesis gains from this thesis one thing it did not have before: a demonstration, in the text and in Gardner's words, that the substrate has the shape Swedenborg said the ancient knowledge had. The hypothesis is not proved by this. A body of knowledge can have the right shape and still have a history other than the one the hypothesis proposes. But the prediction was specific, it was made in 1763, the text was unavailable to its maker, and the prediction holds.

### 13.4 The Problem of the Two Roots: "They Did Not Come from One"

The strongest objection to reading the *Kephalaia* as a single human form comes from the text itself. At the end of its first teaching chapter it says of the two trees: "He understands that they did not arise out of one another, nor did [th]ey come from one another. They did not come from one!" (23.2–3). Gardner calls this "an important semi-creedal assertion of belief in absolute dualism" (p. 23). His introduction is explicit about what the dualism is: "the dualism is not the hellenistic one of spirit and matter, but of two essences, even substances. The divine life is visible, has spatial and quantifiable elements; and evil matter is the active principle of lust" (p. xxvi). Manichaeism called itself the religion of "the two principles and the three times" (p. 59). This is the historical-critical reading of the text, and it is accurate at its level. This thesis does not dispute it.

What the thesis disputes is that the creed settles the question of form. Three observations show how the two readings stand together.

**First, the text's own denial is narrower than co-eternity.** What the sentence denies is that the trees "arise out of one another" or "come from one another": that good produced evil, or evil good. It also denies that both "come from one." The first denial is Swedenborg's doctrine too: "none but good uses were created by the Lord," and "evil uses arose out of hell" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §336). He warns specifically against believing that good and evil uses "are together from creation" (§339).

On this point the creed and the framework agree. The evil tree did not grow out of the good one, and the good does not bear bad fruit. Where they part is on the further claim that darkness has its own root, uncreated and co-eternal. Swedenborg's account has no second root. Hell is made of human beings, "from the human race" (*Heaven and Hell* §544), and its forms are the forms of the love of self and the world (§554).

**Second, the text's anatomy says something its creed does not.** If darkness were a second substance with a nature of its own, nothing would require it to have the human form. Yet the *Kephalaia* gives darkness no form of its own. Every form it gives darkness is the human one inverted:

- The bad tree has "five limbs" with the same names as the good tree's, each filled with its opposite (21.28–36).
- The King of Darkness has a body of five members (30.33–31.2).
- That body was "sculpted" by "the thought of death" (31.9–11).
- It has no life in it "whatsoever" but "the bile of [anger]" (78.31–33).
- Its members are taken from the five creatures of five worlds that do not resemble one another (30.24).
- Its spirits reign in human tyrants, human idolatries and human soothsayers (33.5–34.3).
- Its shapes can be "laid upon" a person by that person (79.2–3).
- Its final state is "the lump, the last bond" (21.34–35).

This is the anatomy of a privation, not of a substance. It is the human form with its interiors closed, its members turned from their uses, its life replaced by anger, and its tendency toward collapse into a mass. Gardner's own phrase for the bad tree, "an exact negative" (p. 23), names the relation. A negative has no image of its own; it is the image with every value reversed.

**Third, the two readings stand on different planes, and both stand.** At the natural plane the *Kephalaia* teaches two principles. The doctrine shapes its myth, its ethics and its practice, and it is what made Manichaeism a distinct religion. At the plane of correspondence the same text describes one form and its opposite: a human body turned toward its source, and the same body turned toward itself.

The library has already read the descent model of the *Kephalaia* in this way. [*Descent Remembered as Ascent*](../06_Mythological_Studies/Descent%20Remembered%20as%20Ascent_%20The%20Correspondential%20Origin%20of%20the%20Evolutionary%20Idea.md) argued that with the correspondential key "the two trees are not two substances but one rational faculty in two orientations" (§6). The anatomy assembled in §§4–5 of this thesis is the textual basis for that reading.

The *Kephalaia*'s creed does not refute the reading. The creed belongs to the frame. The anatomy belongs to the form the frame is written in. A Manichaean reader would take the beatitude on separating the two trees as the recognition of two eternal natures. A correspondential reader takes it as the discrimination of what is from the Lord from what is from self. The text supports both readings, at different levels, and the second does not cancel the first.

What looks like a further divergence is the same point seen from the side of the natural world. Chapter 38 says that the enemy built the universe "after the likeness of a man" (90.20–22). Chapter 64 says that the rulers formed Adam to use him (157.32–158.23). Read literally, the *Kephalaia* would here attribute the human form of the natural world to darkness.

Read at the level of its anatomy, it does not:

- **The form comes from above.** The rulers formed Adam "after the shining form of the image that had been displayed to them above" (157.8–9). The form is shown from above, and they reproduce its reflection.
- **The life comes from the light.** The life of the great body is "F[iv]e g[od]s... fastened in him... his soul and his life" (91.15–16).
- **Darkness has no life of its own.** The ruler of darkness has "no life [in him] whatsoever" (78.31–32).

The darkness can reproduce what the light displays, but it cannot make it, and it cannot make it live. This is Swedenborg's physics of life. The Lord "is Life itself," and "angels and men are recipients of life" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §4). Every form, by whomever it is shaped, is alive only with the life that flows into it.

[*The Dāmdād Nask and the Bundahišn*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20D%C4%81md%C4%81d%20Nask%20and%20the%20Bundahi%C5%A1n_%20A%20Philological%20Reconstruction%20of%20the%20Lost%20Sasanian%20Encyclopedia.md) reads Mani's microcosm as a negative valuation of the Iranian one (§4.3). At the natural plane of Mani's myth that is accurate: the body is a prison and the world a trap. At the level of the form it is not. The form and its life are from above in the *Kephalaia* as in Swedenborg; what the darkness adds is only the binding.

### 13.5 Limitations and Lacunae

**Dependence on one translation.** The argument rests on Gardner's English, corrected in the four renderings the library's edition checks against the Coptic (§2.2). Beyond those four, the thesis has not checked the key terms against the Coptic. The library's own transcription is not yet reliable enough for that, and the printed Coptic of Polotsky and Böhlig was not consulted here. Gardner's index confirms that "the Last Statue," "the Statue," "the Perfect Man" and "the Pillar of Glory" render fixed Coptic expressions, and that the Statue is the Greek loan *andrias*. But word-level questions remain open:

- the range of the Coptic word Gardner renders "limb" and "member";
- whether "construct," "build" and "sculpt" render one verb or several;
- the force of "perfect" in "perfect in all its limbs."

A study of the Coptic vocabulary of the body, once a sound transcription exists, would test the finding at a level this thesis could not reach.

**Lacunae.** Several passages used here are damaged, and the thesis has tried to say where:

- **The noun at 178.13.** The noun that "all these names" are "a single [ ... ]" of is lost. The thesis does not supply it.
- **The Father's limbs.** The clause "five great light lim[bs] in each one" (64.25–26) breaks off before saying in each of what.
- **The springs.** The fourth and fifth springs of the Father's praise are illegible (64.34–65.5).
- **The new man's twelve members.** These stand at a lacuna (97.7) and are not used.
- **Restored identifications of the Pillar.** At 24.22, 25.23 and 176.4 the identification is restored. The thesis relies instead on the preserved parallels at 35.10, 37.3, 92.6, 155.11 and 177.4.
- **The King's head.** The lion-faced head is restored in chapter 6 and preserved in chapter 27.

None of the thesis's arguments turns on a restored word. But the overall picture is drawn from a damaged codex, and passages now lost may have said more, or something different.

**Selection.** The thesis reads the chapters in which bodies appear. It does not claim that every one of the 122 chapters is about a body. Many are about fasting, alms, the sun's benefits, the apostles, the sects or the conduct of the elect.

The claim is narrower and stronger: every *register* the text describes — the divine, the cosmic, the human, the ecclesial, the eschatological, the infernal — is given the human form when the text describes it. A chapter that takes up a register without a body would count against the claim. None has been found, but the survey of the codex was not exhaustive.

**Composition and redaction.** Gardner notes that the *Kephalaia* is a compendium. Chapter 70 "appears to be redacted from a number of separate sources or Manichaean lessons" (p. 179). The larger part of chapter 16 "is in effect a separate and unrelated kephalaion" (p. 54). The thesis's "composition" means the body of teaching as compiled, not the plan of a single author. That the human form governs material from different sources and lessons strengthens the finding that it is the form of the teaching. It also means the finding does not establish a single compositional intention.

**Stratigraphy not re-run.** The thesis does not assign each body passage to the substrate, to Mani's frame or to the community's frame. It notes that the form appears in material of all three kinds: substrate chapters, pentads Gardner calls Mani's, and openly Pauline names. It argues from this that the form is not the property of one layer. Which elements of the body vocabulary are older than Mani is left to [*The Ancient Word Recovered*](../00_Framework/The%20Ancient%20Word%20Recovered_%20Extracting%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) and [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md).

**The readings of persons.** The structural finding — that the text gives every register a human form — can be checked by anyone. The spiritual readings that accompany it depend on the framework:

- the First Man as the Divine Human in descent;
- the Pillar as the church in formation;
- the King of Darkness as hell in one form;
- the Last Statue as the Greatest Man completed.

These readings are consistent with the text's own functions for these figures, and with Swedenborg. They are interpretations of those functions, not reports of them.

**The comparison with Swedenborg.** Swedenborg is used as a vocabulary of recognition. Two places could look like disagreement: five watches against three heavens, and a world built by the enemy. On reading, both turn out to be agreements (§12).

Swedenborg's Greatest Man is heaven. The *Kephalaia* applies the form as fully to the natural degree in its mixed state. Swedenborg also sees the form there (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319), so this is a difference of emphasis, not of doctrine. A full comparison would also bring in Swedenborg's long series on the individual provinces of the Grand Man in the *Arcana*, which this thesis cites only at its opening (§3624) and in a few places on the bones and skin (§§5552–5560).

**Numbers.** The reading of number as quality is the framework's, set out in [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) and grounded in Swedenborg. Gardner's judgements that the text's sums are "artificial," "forced" and "convoluted" are cited as independent indications that the sums are not where the text's coherence lies. They are not proof of the framework's reading. A reader who rejects the framework's account of number will still find the body in the text. Such a reader will simply not have an explanation for why the sums strain.

**Iranian material not re-read.** The thesis mentions the *Bundahišn* microcosm and the body-creation list of the Apocryphon of John through the library's companion studies, without re-reading the primary sources. The library's edition replaces "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" with "Hearkening" and "the Last Statue" with "the Final Body," on Iranian grounds set out in its translation notes. This thesis has not verified the Middle Persian or Parthian names behind those substitutions. It takes no position on them beyond noting that the body titles Gardner prints are the ones the argument reads.

## 14. Conclusion: Perfect in All Its Limbs

This thesis asked whether the *Kephalaia of the Teacher* is composed as one human form. It answered from Gardner's translation with the edition's four corrections to the Coptic, citing preserved text, and testing each register against Swedenborg's description of the Greatest Man. The answer is yes, in every register the text describes.

**The Father** is a body. He "ex[ists and is] established in his five light limbs" of mind, thought, teaching, counsel and consideration, and in "twelve light limbs that are his twelve wisdoms." The powers that go out from him come "from the five limbs." He and his love "are a single living body... because he has given himself for every thing." At every level of the system a father lives "established" in his greatness, as a life in its body. This is God as Man, from whose Divine Human, in Swedenborg's words, "heaven as a whole and in part reflects man."

**The two trees** of the Gospel parable are two bodies with the same five limbs. The good tree is a ladder by which the souls rise from the church to the Father. The bad tree has the same faculties turned to death, transmigration, gehenna and "the lump, the last bond." Gardner calls it "an exact negative." This is the human mind turned toward its source or toward itself.

**The King of Darkness** is the same form inverted. He has five shapes from five worlds that do not resemble one another, a body "sculpted" by "the thought of death," no interior and no life but "the bile of anger." His members reign today in human tyranny and idolatry, and he can fall in upon himself "like a grape and a great iron ball." This is Swedenborg's hell "in one complex," "a single devil," the monster at the gate whose parts do not answer to a single head.

**The universe** was built "after the likeness of a man," with ribs as firmaments, navel as zodiac and heart as mankind. Five guardians, each a faculty, keep its watch-districts. In it "the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos." The elect who master face, heart, desire, appetite and feet resemble those guardians, member for member, by use rather than by position. Adam bears "the seal of the entire universe." This is the doctrine that the ancients, Swedenborg wrote, derived "from the knowledge of correspondences."

**The soul** is bound into the little body in Swedenborg's simultaneous order: mind in bone, thought in sinew, teaching in vein, counsel in flesh, consideration in skin. The Light Mind looses those members and will "construct a new man of them," with five living members — love, faith, perfection, patience and wisdom — and reigns in him as king. The new man is lost again from the outside in, until the member wants "to walk alone," "becomes an earthly man," and is separated from the body of the church.

**The Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man**, is one figure under two names. It is a column that "bore up under the [en]tire universe" and carries the light upward to the ships and the aeons. It is also a man, the "great Mind" placed in the universe, its body perfected by the five intellectuals, with the First Man's head "placed on the body of the Pill[ar of Gl]ory in the heights." Swedenborg describes the degrees as a column in successive order and as a solid body in simultaneous order. The *Kephalaia* gives one figure both descriptions, and the body is the church gathering under its head.

**The Last Statue** is the end of history and the completion of that body. The counsel of life, mixed into the world "in silence" like butter in milk, will "gather to it its own soul, and sc[ul]pt it in the Last Sta[t]ue." It gathers "the life and the li[gh]t that are in all things" and "builds upon its body." It "sweeps out and casts from it the polluti[on] that is foreign to it." It rises only when it "will be perfect in all its limbs." Those who are not gathered weep because "their limbs have been severed," and they go where "they desired it an[d] loved it." This is Swedenborg's society taking shape "by degrees in a human form... and at last clearly as a man," casting out the heterogeneous.

**The names** under which all this is taught are, in chapter 72, garments over one thing. Stripped of "all these appearances," they "will gather together [and] make a single form, and a single name, unaltered and unchangeable for ever."

**The numbers** confirm the finding where they are read as the text reads them best. Five is everywhere the faculty, the same five at every register. Three, the degrees, runs through the five: happiness, wisdom and power at each of five levels, three heavens within the first of five watches. Twelve is the whole of a greatness, spread "three by three" to the four quarters. Where the text multiplies — five within each of five, three in each of four — it describes the whole present in each part, the mark of the most perfect form. Where it adds to make a twelve or a fifty, Gardner finds it "artificial," "forced" and "convoluted," and the thesis builds nothing on those sums. When the Father is unveiled, the text says, "they will not count that season amongst them; nor the number of the days, nor the hours."

The central claim of the thesis can therefore be stated with full force. The human form is the constant of the *Kephalaia*. The Father, the cosmos, Adam, the individual, the church and the end are one body at different magnitudes, ordered from within outward and by use, completed by gathering and purged of what is foreign to it. The adversary is that body inverted. Gardner describes the same structure in his own terms: "what is true in the macrocosmos is also true for the microcosmos"; "this trunk is the Pillar of Glory which reunites with the First Man at its head. At the end of time it is the Last Statue." And it is the Greatest Man as Emanuel Swedenborg described it between 1749 and 1763: heaven as a single man, the same in its greatest and its least parts, from the Divine Human, with hell as a single devil.

Swedenborg also wrote that the ancients knew man as a microcosm "from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were." He wrote this more than a century and a half before a cache of Manichaean books, apparently from a wooden chest in a ruined house, came to light at Medinet Madi. The book this thesis has read from that find teaches exactly that knowledge, in exactly that form. Its Father has limbs, its universe has a heart, its soul has bones, and its last word is a statue of a man.

The names are many. The limbs are many. The body is one, and at the end it is perfect in all its limbs.
