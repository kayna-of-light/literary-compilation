## Key Findings

### The Expeditions (1902-1914)
- Four German archaeological expeditions to Turfan (Xinjiang, China)
- Led by Albert Grünwedel and Albert von Le Coq
- Financed by Friedrich Krupp (arms manufacturer) and Kaiser Wilhelm II
- Returned with 40,000+ text fragments and thousands of artworks
- 46 crates (1st expedition), 118 crates (3rd), 156 crates (4th)

### What They Found
- **The lost Manichaean religion was attested for the first time in its own writings.** Middle Iranian texts in Manichaean script — previously unknown to Western scholarship.
- Berlin Collection: the greatest number of Manichaean original texts from Central Asia in the world
- Cave temple networks — both Buddhist and Manichaean
- Manuscripts in ~15 languages with ~30 scripts
- Oldest fragments: 2nd century CE Indian drama (pushed back the date of Indian drama by centuries)
- Friedrich Müller's 1904 decipherment of "Manichaean Estrangelo" = beginning of modern Manichaean studies

### The Geographic Connection to Swedenborg
Swedenborg wrote in 1771: "The Ancient Word is still preserved among the peoples who dwell in Great Tartary" (TCR §279).

"Great Tartary" was the 18th-century European designation for Central Asia — precisely the Turfan/Xinjiang region where these expeditions found the Manichaean manuscripts 131 years later.

The Uyghur Khaganate adopted Manichaeism as state religion in 762/763 CE. The Kingdom of Qocho (Khocho) preserved Manichaean texts in cave temple-libraries — exactly the sites the German expeditions excavated.

Swedenborg had ZERO access to these texts. They were not discovered until 1902. Yet his geographic claim points to the exact region.

### The Lost Library — A Devastating Detail
Le Coq recounted: A local peasant told him that FIVE YEARS before the first expedition arrived (i.e., ~1897), he had found "great cartloads" of Manichaean manuscripts in a temple ruin, many ornamented with pictures in gold and colours, but **out of fear he threw the whole library into the river.**

An entire Manichaean library — destroyed by the very person who found it. This parallels the Nag Hammadi story where Muhammad Ali's mother burned codices in the oven. In both cases, the texts survived burial for centuries only to face partial destruction upon discovery by people who did not know what they held.

### The Pattern: Discovery and Partial Destruction
| Discovery | Texts Survived | Texts Lost |
|-----------|---------------|------------|
| Nag Hammadi (1945) | 45 texts in 13 codices | Codex XII largely burned in oven |
| Turfan (~1897) | Nothing from this find | "Great cartloads" thrown in river |
| Turfan (1902-1914) | 40,000+ fragments | Unknown losses from prior local finds |
| Dead Sea Scrolls (1947) | 981 manuscripts | Unknown — some damaged before scholars saw them |

## Significance for the Research

1. **Swedenborg's geographic claim is confirmed.** He said the Ancient Word was preserved in "Great Tartary." The German expeditions found it there, exactly 131 years later.

2. **The timing pattern continues.** The Turfan finds (1902-1914) are the earliest of the major manuscript discoveries. They precede Nag Hammadi (1945) and DSS (1947) by decades — but the Manichaean texts could not be READ as containing the Ancient Word substrate until the computational extraction of the Kephalaia in 2024.

3. **The "earth helping the woman" pattern.** The texts survived in cave temples sealed by sand and climate. The desert preserved what human institutions could not.

4. **The partial destruction pattern raises a question.** If providence is at work in the preservation and timing, how do we account for the losses? The peasant's cartloads thrown in the river, the mother's burning in the oven. Is partial loss itself part of the pattern — enough survives for the reconstruction, but the losses remind us that nothing in the natural plane is guaranteed?
