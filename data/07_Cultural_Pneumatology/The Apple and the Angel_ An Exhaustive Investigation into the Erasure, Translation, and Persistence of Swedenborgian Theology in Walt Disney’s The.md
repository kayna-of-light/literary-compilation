# **The Apple and the Angel: An Exhaustive Investigation into the Erasure, Translation, and Persistence of Swedenborgian Theology in Walt Disney’s *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed***

## **I. Introduction: The Cartography of a Cultural Palimpsest**

In the vast archive of American folklore, few figures occupy a space as paradoxical and contested as John Chapman, the itinerant nurseryman known to history and myth as Johnny Appleseed. To the secular historian, Chapman is a shrewd nurseryman and landholder—by some estimates he owned as much as 1,200 acres in Ohio and Indiana—who navigated the complex economic landscape of the expanding Northwest Territory (Schmidt 2006). To the theologian, specifically within the Church of the New Jerusalem, he is the American St. Francis, a barefoot ascetic and zealous missionary who distributed the revelations of the 18th-century Swedish mystic Emanuel Swedenborg along with his apple seeds. Yet, to the collective consciousness of the American public, he is almost exclusively the figure codified by Walt Disney Productions in the 1948 animated segment *Johnny Appleseed*, a component of the package film *Melody Time*, released on its own in the 1950s as *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* (American Film Institute n.d.).  
The Disney iteration of Chapman—a scrawny, pot-wearing, hymn-singing wanderer accompanied by "Johnny's own private guardian angel" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948)—is a masterpiece of mid-century animation, defined by the modernist folk aesthetic of Mary Blair and the earnest vocal performance of Dennis Day. However, for scholars of religious history and cinema, this film presents a significant problem of representation. The historical Chapman was defined by his specific sectarian allegiance; he did not merely carry "a book," but Swedenborg's volumes, which he divided into pieces to circulate among pioneers as "news right fresh from heaven" (Haley 1871). The Disney film, by contrast, depicts Johnny carrying a single, black-bound volume, visually coded as a standard Bible, with no mention of the New Church or its distinct doctrines.  
This perceived sanitization has given rise to a persistent critical claim: that Disney’s *Johnny Appleseed* represents a total erasure of the character’s Swedenborgian identity in favor of a "generic," non-denominational Protestantism suitable for a mass market (*The Animating Spirit: An Exhaustive Analysis* §5.1). This report undertakes a rigorous, forensic investigation into that claim. By triangulating archival production records, the biographical backgrounds of the creative team, the regulatory environment of post-war Hollywood (specifically the Hays Code), and the theological structure of the film’s narrative, this inquiry seeks to determine whether this erasure was an act of ignorance or a calculated strategic maneuver.  
The investigation reveals a complex reality. The substitution of Swedenborgian tracts with a generic Bible was undeniably a strategic choice, driven by the commercial necessity of the "package film" era and the constraints of the Motion Picture Production Code. However, deeper analysis suggests that while the *label* of Swedenborgianism was excised, the *structure* of the theology was preserved and even canonized. Through folklore consultant Carl Carmer—whose own retelling of the legend came from the account that names Chapman's faith—and the oversight of a culturally literate story team, Disney created a film that functions as a covert theological text. From the depiction of the afterlife as a "kingdom of uses" to the characterization of the guardian angel as a frontiersman in human form, the film retains a high fidelity to Swedenborgian doctrine, translated into the vernacular of American tall tales. Furthermore, the film's color stylist, Mary Blair—a long-time friend and close working associate of Hal Adelquist, the studio's correspondent with the Swedenborgian librarian Florence Murdoch—gave these spiritual concepts a visual form that permeated the fabric of the film.

## **II. The Historical Reality: John Chapman as Missionary of the New Jerusalem**

To understand the magnitude of the adaptation and the specific nature of the "erasure," one must first establish the theological reality of the historical John Chapman. He was not a vague pantheist or a simple nature-lover; he was "a most earnest disciple of the faith taught by Emanuel Swedenborg" (Haley 1871), a distinct theological agent operating within a specific, often controversial, religious framework.

### **2.1 The Theology of Emanuel Swedenborg**

The intellectual architecture of Chapman’s life was provided by Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772), a Swedish scientist-turned-mystic who claimed to have received a direct revelation from the Lord, allowing him to travel freely between heaven, hell, and the world of spirits. The "New Church" (or Church of the New Jerusalem), founded on his writings, proposed a radical restructuring of Christian thought that diverged sharply from the Calvinist and Evangelical orthodoxy of the American frontier.  
Key tenets of Swedenborgianism that are relevant to the Chapman mythos include:

* **The Doctrine of Correspondences:** Swedenborg argued that the natural world is a causal mirror of the spiritual world. Every physical object is the "ultimate" form of a spiritual truth. An apple tree is not just a tree: "fruit trees and flowers correspond to intelligence and wisdom," and in heaven "the trees bear fruit that are in accordance with the good of love" (*Heaven and Hell* §176; hereafter *HH*). A seed corresponds to faith grounded in charity, and in the supreme sense to "the Divine truth itself" (*Arcana Coelestia* §§1025, 3038). This worldview imbued Chapman’s horticulture with sacramental weight.  
* **The Maxim of Use:** Salvation is not achieved by *sola fide* (faith alone), but by the conjunction of Charity (Love) and Faith in the performance of "uses" (good works). Swedenborg wrote, "the Lord's kingdom is a kingdom of uses" (*HH* §387). The spiritual value of a man is determined by his utility to his neighbor.  
* **Eschatology and Angelology:** Swedenborg rejected the idea of angels as a separate species created before humanity. He taught that "in the entire heaven there is not a single angel who was created such from the beginning, nor in hell any devil who was created an angel of light and cast down; but that all, both in heaven and in hell, are from the human race" (*HH* §311). Death is merely a continuation of life: man "does not die; he is merely separated from the bodily part that was of use to him in the world" (*HH* §445), and the individual continues to pursue their "ruling love." If a man loved usefulness on earth, he would practice usefulness in heaven.  
* **The Nature of Scripture:** Swedenborg believed the Bible contained an internal spiritual sense, which his writings unlocked.

### **2.2 Chapman’s Evangelism: The Tracts**

Chapman’s method of evangelism was inextricably linked to his nursery business. Haley thought him "probably not only the first colporteur in the wilderness of Ohio"—a distributor of religious literature—but one with "no tract society to furnish him supplies." The volumes of Swedenborg were expensive and physically heavy. To maximize their reach, Chapman "divided his books into several pieces, leaving a portion at a log-cabin, and on a subsequent visit furnishing another fragment," creating a primitive lending library system. He famously introduced his readings not as "sermons" but as "some news right fresh from heaven," producing "his few tattered books, among which would be a New Testament," to read and expound (Haley 1871).  
Historical accounts confirm that Chapman was a zealous advocate for these specific doctrines. He would not willingly harm any living thing: he put out his campfire rather than let mosquitoes burn in it, and grieved over the one rattlesnake he killed "in the heat of my ungodly passion," because everything that lived was to him "endowed with so much of the Divine Essence that to wound or destroy it was to inflict an injury upon some atom of Divinity" (Haley 1871)—a reverence that Swedenborg's doctrine grounds in the correspondence of animals with human affections (*HH* §110). He held that two spirits "of the feminine gender" had revealed to him "that they were to be his wives in a future state if he abstained from a matrimonial alliance on earth" (Haley 1871), a folk interpretation of Swedenborg’s doctrine of Conjugial Love.

### **2.3 The Discrepancy**

The Disney film depicts Johnny carrying a single, black-bound book with a gold cross on its cover, visually indistinguishable from a standard Bible. The narrator lists it among his emblems as "a holy book," and the angel calls it "pretty darn good readin'" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948; Silverman 2012). There are no loose tracts, no mentions of Swedenborg, and no exposition on the internal sense of scripture. This substitution—the replacement of a specific, controversial sectarian library with a universal symbol of Protestant piety—is the central act of erasure under investigation.

## **III. The Production Context: *Melody Time* and Post-War Hollywood**

The production of *Melody Time* (1948) occurred during a specific window of constraint and transition for Walt Disney Productions. Understanding this context is crucial to determining whether the erasure of Swedenborg was an act of ignorance or strategy.

### **3.1 The Package Film Era and Economic Constraint**

Following World War II, the studio was in a precarious financial position. The foreign markets had been lost during the war, and expensive features like *Fantasia* and *Bambi* had failed to recoup their costs (Gabler 2006). To maintain a theatrical presence without the expense of a full narrative feature, Disney produced "package films"—anthologies of short animated segments set to music. *Melody Time* was the last of these (before the partial return to form with *Ichabod and Mr. Toad*). It had begun as an anthology of American folk heroes, of whom only Johnny Appleseed and Pecos Bill survived into the finished film (Gabler 2006).  
The package format demanded narrative economy. The "Johnny Appleseed" segment runs approximately seventeen and a half minutes (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948). In this compressed format, complex exposition regarding 18th-century Swedish mysticism would have been narrative poison. The filmmakers needed visual shorthand. A "Bible" reads instantly as "faith"; a stack of Swedenborgian tracts requires explanation.

### **3.2 The Hays Code and Religious Representation**

In 1948, the Motion Picture Production Code (Hays Code) was the dominant regulatory force in Hollywood. While the Code did not explicitly ban the depiction of specific religions, it contained strict clauses regarding religion (Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America 1930):

* **Clause VIII.1:** "No film or episode may throw ridicule on any religious faith."  
* **General Principles:** "The sympathy of the audience should never be thrown to the side of crime, wrong-doing, evil or sin."  
* **Clause VIII.3:** "Ceremonies of any definite religion should be carefully and respectfully handled."

Swedenborgianism, with its denial of the physical resurrection (*HH* §§312, 445), its non-Trinitarian view of God (Swedenborg argued the Trinity existed within the person of Jesus Christ, not as three persons; *True Christian Religion* §170), and its claims of spirit communication, was viewed by many mainstream denominations as heretical or "spiritist." To depict Johnny explicitly preaching Swedenborgianism would have invited controversy and potential censorship. A generic "Christian" Johnny was a safe, Code-compliant hero.

### **3.3 The Shift to "Americana" and Civic Religion**

The post-war period saw a rise in "Americana" in Disney’s output (*So Dear to My Heart*, *Davy Crockett*). As the Cold War dawned, there was a cultural push to define "Americanism" against "Godless Communism" (Davis 2014). This resulted in the promotion of a generalized "Judeo-Christian" civic religion—a faith defined by "moral and spiritual values" rather than sectarian dogma. Walt Disney himself subscribed to this view, writing in 1963 that "Deeds rather than words express my concept of the part religion should play in everyday life" (Disney 1963). A Johnny Appleseed who represents "Faith" (broadly defined) fit this national project; a Johnny Appleseed who represents the Church of the New Jerusalem did not.

## **IV. The Creative Intelligence: Investigating the Creators**

The claim that the erasure was due to "ignorance" crumbles when we examine the personnel involved in the production. The team assembled for *Melody Time* possessed deep knowledge of American folklore and religious history.

### **4.1 Carl Carmer: The Folklore Consultant**

The most significant evidence of the studio’s awareness is the credit of **Carl Carmer** as "Folklore consultant" for the film (American Film Institute n.d.). Carmer (1893–1976) was not a casual hire; he was a preeminent historian, author, and academic, known for works like *Stars Fell on Alabama* (1934) and *The Hurricane’s Children* (1937). The studio had considered building its folklore anthology with his help, and *Johnny Appleseed* was one of the legends planned for it (Isojärvi 2024).

* **Carmer’s Expertise:** Carmer was an expert on American regional myths, and he had told the Appleseed legend himself from the tradition of W. D. Haley’s 1871 article in *Harper’s New Monthly Magazine*. That article is the primary source for the "Johnny Appleseed" mythos and *explicitly* details Chapman’s Swedenborgian faith: it calls him "a most earnest disciple of the faith taught by Emanuel Swedenborg," quotes his "news right fresh from heaven" line and describes his method of dividing the books (Haley 1871). In *The Hurricane's Children*, Carmer's Johnny would "stretch himself out on the floor, take out the Bible he carried inside the coffee sack, and read aloud what he called 'news fresh from heaven'—the Sermon on the Mount" (Carmer 1937). Haley's phrase is there; Haley's Swedenborg is not.  
* **The Consultant’s Role:** As a consultant, Carmer’s job was to provide the studio with the "real" story, which the story team would then adapt (or distort) for entertainment. The substitution the film makes—a Bible in place of Swedenborg's books—is already in its folklore consultant's own retelling, eleven years before the film. His presence confirms that the studio had access to the Haley tradition, and with it to the full historical profile of the man; the "generic Bible" arrived with the folklore, not from ignorance of the faith behind it.

### **4.2 The Story Team: Hibler and Penner**

The segment was written by a team including **Winston Hibler**, **Erdman Penner**, and **Joe Rinaldi**, the story men Wilfred Jackson's diary names when the picture began in 1946 (Care 2016).

* **Erdman Penner:** Penner was born in 1905 in Rosthern, Saskatchewan, the son of a pioneer doctor and his French wife (Smith n.d.), and came to Disney in 1935 as a storyman. The decision to homogenize Johnny’s faith was made by writers whose studio could say that the story had been painstakingly researched (Means 2011), rather than by writers who simply didn't know the difference.  
* **Winston Hibler:** Later the voice of Disney’s *True-Life Adventures*, Hibler was known for his literary literacy. He adapted the script for the record albums as well (*Johnny Appleseed* 1949; Ehrbar 2017), showing a deep engagement with the narrative mechanics of the story.

### **4.3 Mary Blair: The Visual Correspondence**

**Mary Blair**, credited with Claude Coats and Dick Kelsey for the color and styling of *Melody Time* (American Film Institute n.d.), stood close to the studio's handling of the Swedenborgian material.

* **The Fact:** Hal Adelquist, who answered Florence Murdoch about the film's angel, wrote in 1966 that "Mary and I are friends and close working associates for many years, including the Disney daze" (Adelquist, 23 May 1966, reproduced in Amidi 2010). The "Mrs. Mary Blair" confirmed into the Swedenborgian Church at the Wayfarers Chapel on December 23, 1973 was a namesake, Mrs. William Blair of Torrance (*The Messenger*, February 1974; M. Blair 1974; General Convention 1976); the artist had moved to Soquel in 1970 (Canemaker 2014).  
* **The Analysis:** Blair worked inside the circle that knew Chapman's faith and defended its depiction to a Swedenborgian. Did her work on *Johnny Appleseed* draw on that knowledge? Her visual design suggests that it did.  
* **Visual Correspondence:** Blair’s art style in the film—characterized by flatness, luminosity, and a distinct lack of separation between the "natural" and the "supernatural"—mirrors the Swedenborgian doctrine of *Influx*. The clouds in the film are not just vapor: "them clouds ain't really clouds at all. They're apple blossoms, if you please, from John's heavenly orchard of apple trees" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948), blurring the line between the sky and the orchard. Whatever her own religious life, her aesthetic sensibilities were singularly aligned with the Swedenborgian worldview.

## **V. The "Generic Bible" Strategy: Strategic Ambiguity**

If the creators knew Chapman was Swedenborgian, why the "Generic Bible"? The evidence points to a strategy of **Strategic Ambiguity**.

### **5.1 The Prop as Symbol**

In animation, visual economy is paramount. A book labeled "Arcana Coelestia" is a confusing detail that requires verbal exposition. A black book with a gold cross is a universal symbol for "Piety." By using the visual language of the Bible, Disney communicated Johnny’s *character* (good, faithful, literate) without engaging his *dogma*.

### **5.2 "The Blessings Three": A Coded Creed?**

While the book was generic, the script introduced a specific theological triad that served as a covert nod to Swedenborgianism. The film's songs were written by **Kim Gannon** and **Walter Kent** (American Film Institute n.d.; Silverman 2012); its closing verse tells how, wherever Johnny's shadow fell, "you'll find he left his blessings three" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948), and the studio's own record album names them: "Wherever Johnny stopped, he left LOVE, FAITH and the APPLE TREE" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1949).  
*"Love, Faith and the Apple Tree."*  
This phrase, sung at the close of the film and spelled out in the studio's album text, is not merely a rhyme. It is a precise translation of the Swedenborgian **Trinity of Essentials**—the "three essentials" that, Swedenborg says, "make one as soul, body, and operation make one in man" (*True Christian Religion* §166):

1. **Love (Charity):** The Will / The desire to do good.  
2. **Faith (Truth):** The Understanding / The knowledge of God.  
3. **The Apple Tree (Works/Use):** The external manifestation of Love and Faith in the natural world.

Swedenborg teaches that "charity and faith make one as good and truth do" (*Divine Providence* §82). By adopting this triad, the filmmakers preserved the *structural* core of Chapman’s theology while stripping away the sectarian terminology.

### **5.3 The Florence Murdoch Correspondence**

The most definitive proof that the studio navigated this issue consciously lies in the correspondence between Disney and **Florence Murdoch**. Murdoch was a devout Swedenborgian, librarian at the Cincinnati Church of the New Jerusalem, and holder of one of the largest Johnny Appleseed collections in the world (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.).

* **The Protest:** On May 26, 1948, the day before *Melody Time* opened, Murdoch wrote to Walt Disney after seeing an ad depicting Johnny’s angel wearing a **coonskin cap** (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.; Means 2011). She expressed concern that the studio was taking too much liberty with spiritual concepts (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.), and asked whether "guardian angel" could become "spirit of the frontier" (Means 2011). However, she was **pleased** that the film would include what Johnny called his "Blessings Three": "love and faith and an apple tree" (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.).  
* **The Defense:** **Hal Adelquist**, manager of Disney's Story Department, replied on June 23, 1948. He wrote that the story had been painstakingly researched, and that the guardian angel was in keeping with the known facts of a frontiersman who believed his tree planting divinely inspired and that heavenly spirits walked among us (Means 2011)—Haley's Chapman, who "claimed to have frequent conversations with angels and spirits" (Haley 1871). "We trust that after you have viewed the picture you will agree that we have treated the angel with sincere respect and endowed him with a certain dignity," he wrote, and he called the short the closest the studio had come "to a sermon on... brotherly love and unselfishness" (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.).  
* **The Outcome:** This exchange proves the studio was not ignorant. They had done the research and made deliberate choices to adapt the theology into a form that was respectful but chemically altered for mass consumption. Murdoch's eventual acceptance of the film—seven years later she wrote that she admired its treatment of Johnny (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.)—suggests that even the New Church saw the "Blessings Three" as a valid translation.

## **VI. Theological Forensics: Traces of Swedenborg in the Animation**

If we look past the "generic Bible," does the film contain actual Swedenborgian elements? The research suggests that the narrative structure of the film is deeply, if inadvertently, Swedenborgian.

### **6.1 The Angel as "Pioneer Spirit"**

In standard Christian iconography of the 1940s, angels were distinct created beings, often depicted as cherubs or winged seraphim. Disney’s angel for Johnny is radically different.

* **The Depiction:** He is a translucent, rugged pioneer in frontier clothes and a coonskin cap who arrives in a golden whirlwind and speaks in the vernacular. The narrator introduces him as "Johnny's own private guardian angel. Might look mighty queersome to you and me, but you know, that's just the way John figured he'd be" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948).  
* **Swedenborgian Alignment:** Swedenborg explicitly taught that **angels are not a separate species**: "all, both in heaven and in hell, are from the human race" (*HH* §311). They retain their human form, personality, and idiosyncratic traits.  
* **The Insight:** By depicting the angel as a "frontier spirit"—a frontiersman in form, dress and speech, the angel Johnny "figured" for himself—Disney provided a more accurate *Swedenborgian* depiction of an angel than a traditional "Biblical" angel would have been. The studio defended the figure on the ground of Chapman's own belief that heavenly spirits walked among men (Means 2011).

### **6.2 The Kingdom of Uses (The Ending)**

The film’s conclusion is the most explicitly Swedenborgian sequence in the Disney canon.

* **The Scene:** An elderly Johnny dies asleep under a tree in his blossoming orchard. The angel wakes him—"Come on, get up"—and when Johnny asks who is sleeping there in the evening dusk, answers: "that's just your husk, John, your mortal husk." Johnny: "You mean to say I'm, I'm passed away?" He protests that he cannot go, with "crops to harvest, seeds to sow." The angel replies: "Down here on earth, your work is through. But yonder, well, we need you, Johnny… We're kind of short on apple trees" (*Johnny Appleseed* 1948). Johnny gathers his book and his seed bag and heads with the angel into a sky of blossom, to plant the "heavenly orchard" whose petals are the clouds.  
* **The Theology:** Traditional Protestantism often views heaven as a place of eternal rest or worship. Swedenborgianism views heaven as a **Kingdom of Uses**. Spirits continue to perform useful services that correspond to their "ruling love" on earth. A heaven that *needs* apple trees, and a soul that finds joy in planting them, is a distinctly Swedenborgian concept. The scene stages Swedenborg's account of death point by point: the man "does not die; he is merely separated from the bodily part that was of use to him in the world" (*HH* §445); the newly dead are "smitten with a kind of surprise, saying that they were alive, and that the thing that had served them in the world was what was being buried" (*HH* §452); and in heaven "the trees bear fruit that are in accordance with the good of love" (*HH* §176).  
* **The Source:** The ending follows an American novel that had made Chapman's Swedenborgianism its spine. In Eleanor Atkinson's *Johnny Appleseed: The Romance of the Sower* (1915), Johnny reads "Swedenborg's *Heaven and Hell*," pictures "the Garden of God where every order of creation dwelt in harmony, and each found its own highest happiness and usefulness," and knows that "when his work on earth was done there would be orchards for his planting and nurturing in the Garden of God"; the novel ends: "Johnny was gone … to plant orchards in the Garden of God" (Atkinson 1915). Whether the story team read Atkinson is not documented; the parallel is textual. An animation historian with the studio's production draft in hand saw the same thing: "I am reluctant to comment on the way that his Swedenborgian religious views enter into the story" (Perk 2010).  
* **The Insight:** By rejecting the "eternal rest" trope, the film validates the Swedenborgian doctrine that the afterlife is a continuation of useful labor.

### **6.3 "The Lord is Good to Me": The Hymn That Became Canon**

A critical part of the myth is the song "The Lord is Good to Me."

* **The Myth:** It is often cited as a hymn Chapman actually sung.  
* **The Reality:** It was an original composition for the film, words and music by **Kim Gannon** and **Walter Kent**, © 1946 Walt Disney Music Company (Silverman 2012).  
* **The Correspondence:** Despite being a Hollywood invention, the lyrics ("the sun and rain and an apple seed"; *Johnny Appleseed* 1948) perfectly encapsulate Swedenborgian correspondence.  
  * *Sun* \= Divine Love.  
  * *Rain* \= Divine Truth (Water).  
  * *Seed* \= The Word/Potential Good.  
* The song was so theologically sound that it passed after the film's release into common use as a table grace—Silverman calls it "the familiar table blessing"—and into church songbooks for children (Silverman 2012; *Little Ones Sing Praise* 1989), completing a cycle of life imitating art.

## **VII. Conclusion: Strategic Erasure, Spiritual Fidelity**

The investigation into *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* leads to a nuanced conclusion. The claim that the film lacks "direct influence" because of the generic Bible is factually correct but theologically superficial.  
**1\. Strategic Choice, Not Ignorance:** The studio, advised by Carl Carmer—whose own retelling already set a Bible in Johnny's coffee sack—and defending its angel by Chapman's own belief in spirits, possessed full knowledge of Chapman’s Swedenborgian faith. The decision to replace tracts with a generic Bible was a calculated move to navigate the Hays Code and create a unifying American hero during the early Cold War.  
**2\. The Persistence of Structure:** While the label was erased, the structure of the faith remained. The film depicts a universe governed by Swedenborgian rules:

* **Angels are human in form, as the departed are.**  
* **Heaven is a place of useful work (planting trees).**  
* **Salvation is the union of Love, Faith, and Works ("Blessings Three").**

**3\. The Role of the Creators:** The creative team was uniquely suited to this translation. Carl Carmer’s folklore expertise, a story department that had researched the man, and Mary Blair’s aesthetic sensibility combined to create a work that felt "spiritually true" even if it was "factually generic."  
**Verdict:** The "Generic Bible" was a Trojan Horse. By stripping away the controversial sectarian label, Disney created a vessel that allowed the core Swedenborgian ethos—that religion is a life of useful service and that the natural world corresponds to the spiritual—to be consumed and beloved by millions who would have rejected the explicit doctrine. In erasing the name of the church, Disney inadvertently canonized its theology for the American century.
