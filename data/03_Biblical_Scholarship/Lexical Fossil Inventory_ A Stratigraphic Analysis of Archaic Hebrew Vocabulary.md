# **Lexical Fossil Inventory: A Stratigraphic Analysis of Archaic Hebrew Vocabulary**

## **1\. Introduction: The Philology of the Archaic**

The linguistic landscape of the Hebrew Bible is not a monolith but a stratified deposit of literary traditions spanning nearly two millennia. While the final redaction of the Masoretic Text (MT) reflects the grammatical and orthographic conventions of the first millennium BCE—specifically the late monarchic and Persian periods—embedded within this matrix are "lexical fossils." These are vocabulary items, morphological forms, and syntactic structures that defy the conventions of Standard Biblical Hebrew (SBH) and Late Biblical Hebrew (LBH). They find their only intelligible cognates in the archives of the third and second millennia BCE, specifically from Ebla (Tell Mardikh), Ugarit (Ras Shamra), and the Akkadian-speaking sphere of Mesopotamia.

This report provides an exhaustive inventory and analysis of these archaic forms. It proceeds from the premise that these anomalies are not "errors" or "Aramaisms," as frequently cited in early 20th-century scholarship, but are authentic retentions—fossils—of an earlier stage of the language. By analyzing the geographic distribution, cognate relationships, and extinction horizons of these terms, we can reconstruct the "Uruk-Ebla Horizon" (c. 3200–2400 BCE) and the "Amarna-Ugarit Horizon" (c. 1400–1200 BCE) that underlie the biblical text.

### **1.1 The Concept of Lexical Stratigraphy**

In geological stratigraphy, the age of a rock layer is determined by the fossils contained within it. Similarly, linguistic stratigraphy dates the core traditions of a text by the density of its archaic vocabulary. A "lexical fossil" is defined here as a word that:

1. **Possesses a clear cognate** in a 3rd or 2nd millennium Semitic language (Eblaite, Ugaritic, Old Akkadian).  
2. **Is functionally extinct** or semantically shifted in the standard Hebrew of the First Temple Period (Iron Age II).  
3. **Appears in fixed literary contexts** (poetry, ancient narratives like Genesis 1–11 or Job) that resist scribal modernization.

The identification of these fossils relies heavily on the "Cumulative Evidence Principle." While an individual word might be dismissed as a loanword or a dialectal variant, the convergence of dozens of such terms within specific textual blocks creates a statistical probability that these texts preserve a genuine Bronze Age substratum (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

### **1.2 The Crisis of Historicism and Linguistic Dating**

The dating of biblical texts has long been a battleground between "Maximalists" (who argue for early composition) and "Minimalists" (who argue for Persian/Hellenistic invention). The standard method of "Linguistic Dating" (as championed by Avi Hurvitz) distinguishes between Early Biblical Hebrew (EBH) and Late Biblical Hebrew (LBH). However, recent critiques by scholars such as Ian Young, Robert Rezetko, and Martin Ehrensvärd (Young, Rezetko, and Ehrensvärd 2008) have challenged the linearity of this model, suggesting that many "late" features may simply be dialectal or stylistic.

In contrast, the **Lexical Fossil** approach avoids the pitfalls of style by focusing on *obsolescence*. If a word like *qesitah* (a currency unit) or *'ed* (a technical hydrological term) had passed out of use by 1000 BCE, a scribe in 450 BCE could not have "invented" it to sound archaic because the knowledge of the term had vanished. Its presence implies the curation of a received text, providing a hard terminus ad quem for the tradition (*The Paradigm That Cannot See*).

**2\. The Eblaite Stratum: The Uruk-Ebla Horizon (c. 3200–2400 BCE)**

The deepest linguistic stratum identified in the Hebrew Bible corresponds to the "Uruk-Ebla Horizon." This stratum is characterized by technical vocabulary related to the hydrology, economy, and administration of the Early Bronze Age Mesopotamian basin. The primary control data for this stratum comes from the Royal Archives of Ebla (c. 2400 BCE), discovered in 1974, which revealed a Northwest Semitic language (*Eblaite*, which Pettinato classed as Paleo-Canaanite) that predates the Canaanite/Aramaic split (Pettinato 1981, 7, 56).

### **2.1 *'Ed* (אֵד): The Hydrology of Eden**

**Biblical Location:** Genesis 2:6; Job 36:27.

**Cognate:** Akkadian *edû* (from Sumerian *a.de.a*); Eblaite *i-du*.

The term *'ed* appears in the critical juncture of the Eden narrative: "But there went up a *'ed* from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground" (Gen 2:6). Traditional translations render this as "mist," but this creates a hydrological contradiction: mists do not irrigate entire landscapes sufficient for agriculture.

Two derivations have been proposed, both reaching back into the third millennium. E. A. Speiser traced *'ed* to Akkadian *edû*, "flood, waves, swell," a loanword from Sumerian *a.de.a*, and read it of the rise of subterranean waters, citing the use of *edû* for irrigation (Speiser 1955, as summarized in Harris 1968, 177–178); the *Chicago Assyrian Dictionary* glosses *edû* "onrush of water, high water" and records it as a Sumerian loanword (*CAD* E, s.v. *edû*), and Brown–Driver–Briggs already compared *edû*, "flood, mass of waters," and *editu*, "overflow (for irrigation)" (BDB, s.v. *'ed*). Mitchell Dahood, working from the Ebla calendar, read the obscure month name NI.DU as Semitic *i-du* and took both Eblaite *i-du* and Hebrew *'ed* as "rain cloud" (Dahood 1981b); Mark Futato defends the sense "rain cloud" from the biblical contexts while leaving the Eblaite etymology open (Futato 1998, 6), and David Tsumura criticized Dahood's etymology (Tsumura 1989, 95–97).

The implications are profound on either reading. The agriculture of the Levant (Canaan) is rain-fed (*baal*\-agriculture), dependent on the seasonal rains. The agriculture of Mesopotamia is irrigation-fed, dependent on the rising of the rivers and the water table. On Speiser's derivation, by preserving the term *'ed* the Genesis narrative retains a "memory" of a specific hydro-agricultural system that does not exist in the highlands of Judah: the word is a fossil of the riverine civilization where the tradition originated. On Dahood's, it is a fossil of the Eblaite calendar's vocabulary of rain. Either way the word is a third-millennium retention. Its reappearance in Job 36:27—"For he maketh small the drops of water: they pour down rain according to the *'ed* thereof"—confirms its technical meteorological nuance (Futato 1998, 6–7).

### **2.2 *Tehom* (תְּהוֹם): The Depersonified Deep**

**Biblical Location:** Genesis 1:2; Psalm 104:6; Proverbs 8:24.

**Cognate:** Eblaite *ti-é-ma-tum*; Akkadian *Tiamat*.

For over a century, the standard critical view (*Chaoskampf*) held that the Hebrew *tehom* ("the deep") was a polemical borrowing of the Babylonian *Tiamat*, the chaos-dragon goddess of the *Enuma Elish*. This linguistic connection was often used to date the Priestly (P) source of Genesis 1 to the Babylonian Exile (6th century BCE).

However, the discovery of *ti-é-ma-tum* (*tihamatum*) in the Eblaite texts (c. 2400 BCE) fundamentally shifted this trajectory. At Ebla, *ti-é-ma-tum* appears as a common noun meaning "the ocean waters," lacking the personification or mythological agency of the later Babylonian Tiamat (Pettinato 1981, 60–61).

Genesis 1:2 uses *tehom* without the definite article (*ha-tehom*), treating it as a proper noun or an archaic massive noun ("Deep"), yet strictly depersonifies it. It is an inanimate object over which the Spirit of God hovers. This usage aligns with the archaic Eblaite common noun rather than the late Babylonian proper name. Thus, *tehom* is not a late polemic *against* Babylon, but an archaic retention *from* the common Semitic heritage of the third millennium, preserved in a pre-mythological state (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

### **2.3 *Mabbul* (מַבּוּל): The Technical Term for Catastrophe**

**Biblical Location:** Genesis 6:17; 7:6, 7, 10, 17; 9:11, 15, 28; 10:1, 32; 11:10; Psalm 29:10.

**Cognate:** Akkadian *bubbulu*.

Biblical Hebrew possesses generic terms for flood (*sheṭep*, *zerem*), but the Noahic narrative exclusively employs the term *mabbul*. It appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible (BDB, s.v. *mabbul*), only resurfacing in Psalm 29:10 ("The LORD sitteth upon the flood"), a poem widely recognized for its archaic Canaanite/Ugaritic background.

Its derivation is uncertain; Brown–Driver–Briggs considered it possibly an old Babylonian loanword (BDB, s.v. *mabbul*). Akkadian *bubbulu* means "flood" and also names the day of the moon's disappearance (*CAD* B, s.v. *bubbulu*). The distinctiveness of *mabbul* marks it as a technical term from the Mesopotamian hydro-lexicon.

The "Extinction" of this term is notable. When the Torah was translated into Greek (LXX), *mabbul* was rendered with the generic *kataklysmos* (Gen 6:17 LXX). Late Hebrew and Jewish Aramaic kept *mabbul* / *mabbûlāʾ* only as the name of the Flood (BDB, s.v. *mabbul*). The specific Mesopotamian nuance had been lost, fossilizing the word in the Hebrew text as the proper name for *The* Flood (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

### **2.4 *Min'lam* (מִנְלָם): The Economics of Job**

**Biblical Location:** Job 15:29.

**Cognate:** Eblaite *ma-ni-lum*.

Job 15:29 contains a notorious *hapax legomenon*: "He shall not be rich, neither shall his substance continue, neither shall he prolong the *min'lam* thereof upon the earth." The King James Version guesses "perfection"; others suggest "shadow" or "grain."

Mitchell Dahood identified the solution in the Eblaite bilingual vocabularies. There Sumerian *áb*, "cow," is rendered by *ma-ni-lum*, "cow, possession" (Pettinato 1981, 242), which Dahood derives from a root *nyl*, "to acquire," and equates with biblical *mnlm*, "his property," noting the same semantic path from livestock to wealth in Hebrew *miqneh* and Latin *pecus*/*pecunia* (Dahood 1981a, 303–4).

Reading Job 15:29 with this cognate yields perfect sense: "He shall not be rich... neither shall his *wealth/herds* extend upon the earth." This identifies *min'lam* as a fossil of the commercial vocabulary of the Syrian Eblaite sphere (c. 2400 BCE). Its presence in the dialogue of Eliphaz the Temanite suggests the author of Job had access to a wisdom tradition rooted in the economic realities of the Early Bronze Age, distinct from the agrarian economy of Iron Age Judah (*The Ancient Word: Philological Evidence for the Uruk-Ebla Origins of Genesis 1-11 and the Book of Job*).

### **2.5 *Mw* (מו): The Archaic Orthography of Water**

**Biblical Location:** Job 9:30.

**Cognate:** Eblaite *ma-wu*.

In the Masoretic Text (MT) of Job 9:30, the written text (*kethiv*) presents the phrase *bemo-sheleg* (במו שלג). The Masoretes, puzzled by the morphology, read *beme-sheleg* ("in waters of snow"; *qere* במי). However, *mo* is typically a poetic particle.

Dahood argues that the consonantal text is sound: "the Eblaite word for 'water' is *ma-wu* whose consonants are identical with biblical *mw*." The same *ma-wu* glosses Sumerian "water" in a trilingual cuneiform fragment from Tel Aphek, and *mw* stands beside *sheleg*, "snow," again in Job 6:15–16 (Dahood 1981a, 303 and n. 57; Pettinato 1981, 242). In Standard Biblical Hebrew, the word is the dual-form *mayim* (מים).

The preservation of *mw* in Job signals a dialectal stratum where the older Semitic form was still productive, and suggests the text's orthography was fixed before the standardization of the *mayim* form (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

**3\. The Ugaritic Stratum: The Amarna-Ugarit Horizon (c. 1400–1200 BCE)**

The second major stratum of lexical fossils corresponds to the Late Bronze Age (LBA). This period, illuminated by the archives of Ugarit (c. 1300 BCE) and the Amarna Letters (c. 1350 BCE), provides the context for the earliest Hebrew poetry (*Exodus 15, Judges 5, Deuteronomy 32*). The cognates here are often poetic, mythological, and military, reflecting the "Canaanite Matrix" from which early Yahwism emerged.

### **3.1 *Pelilim* (פְּלִילִים): The Parched Enemies**

**Biblical Location:** Deuteronomy 32:31.

**Cognate:** Ugaritic *pl* (*p-l-l*).

The Hebrew term *pelilim* is standardly translated as "judges" (from the root *p-l-l*). However, this rendering creates a nonsensical reading in Deuteronomy 32:31: "For their Rock is not as our Rock, even our enemies themselves being judges." Why would the enemies be judges of the theological difference?

Comparative evidence from Ugaritic reveals a root *p-l-l* meaning "dry, parched, cracked, or split." In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, this term describes the state of the furrows of the field during a drought: *pl ʿnt šdm*, "parched are the furrows of the fields" (KTU 1.6 IV 1–2; Notarius 2017; Notarius 2025). Applying this cognate to Deuteronomy 32:31 restores the parallelism: "Indeed their Rock is not like our Rock; our enemies are *parched/crumbling*."

The loss of this root's meaning in later Hebrew led the Masoretes (c. 700 CE) to point the vowels for "judges," effectively fossilizing the older consonantal skeleton while the semantic value shifted. This confirms the text's composition in a period when the *p-l-l* ("parched") root was still intelligible—likely the pre-monarchic period.

### **3.2 The *Yqtl* Preterite and Enclitic *\-m***

**Biblical Location:** Exodus 15:5; Deuteronomy 32\.

**Cognate:** Ugaritic Verbal System.

One of the strongest proofs for the antiquity of the "Archaic Poetry" corpus is the verbal syntax. In Standard Biblical Hebrew prose, the past tense is conveyed by the *qatal* (perfect) or the *wayyiqtol* (waw-consecutive). However, Exodus 15 retains the use of the *yqtl* (imperfect) form for the preterite (past tense) *without* the waw-consecutive, a feature of the older Northwest Semitic poetry of Ugarit (Notarius 2025).

* **Exodus 15:5:** *Yekhasyumu* ("The depths *covered* them"). The form is *yqtl*, yet the meaning is clearly past.  
* **Archaic suffix *\-mu*:** The verb carries the archaic third-person plural object suffix *\-mû* ("them"; *\-mô* elsewhere in Hebrew poetry) in place of the standard *\-m*.  
* **Significance:** These "grammatical fossils" place the Song of the Sea in the linguistic world attested at Late Bronze Age Ugarit, before the standard forms of Classical Hebrew (Notarius 2025; Pat-El and Wilson-Wright 2013). A scribe writing in the 6th century BCE would have used the standard *wayyekhasu*.

### **3.3 *Tsur* (צוּר): The Divine Rock**

**Biblical Location:** Deuteronomy 32 (multiple occurrences).

**Cognate:** Ugaritic *ġr*.

The title *Tsur* ("Rock") for God is a dominant metaphor in the Song of Moses (Deut 32): seven of the word's eight occurrences in the poem are divine titles (32:4, 15, 18, 30, 31 [twice], 37; 32:13 is the literal rock). While "rock" is a common word, its usage as a specific divine title parallels the Ugaritic *ġr*, "mountain," the abode of the gods, as in El's mountain *ġr ks* (KTU 1.1 III 12; Sanders 2016, 2).

The high density of *Tsur* as a divine epithet in Deut 32 (and its relative scarcity in LBH: in Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, Esther, Daniel and Qohelet the word occurs once, as a literal rock, 1 Chr 11:15) marks it as a fossil of the LBA theological vocabulary, where deities were frequently identified with their sacred mountains (Zaphon, Sinai) or the permanence of the mountain itself.

### **3.4 *Resheph* (רֶשֶׁף): The Plague God**

**Biblical Location:** Habakkuk 3:5; Deuteronomy 32:24.

**Cognate:** Eblaite *Rasap*; Ugaritic *Ršp* (Resheph).

In Habakkuk 3:5, describing the theophany of Yahweh, the text states: "Before him went the pestilence (*deber*), and burning coals (*resheph*) went forth at his feet." In classical Hebrew, *resheph* means "sparks" or "flame."

However, in the Eblaite archives two of the principal deities were Dabir, the patron god of the city ("Dabir, the god of Ebla"), and *Rasap* or Resheph, to whom one of the four city gates was dedicated (Pettinato 1981, 247; Dahood 1981a, 296; Dahood 1984). The biblical usage in Habakkuk and Deuteronomy preserves the *personification* of the term. Yahweh is depicted as accompanied by a retinue where *Deber* (Plague) and *Resheph* (Pestilence) are demythologized servants or weapons, rather than independent deities: "in the Bible both these divinities are reduced to the common nouns 'pestilence' and 'plague' or to attendants to Yahweh on his journeys" (Dahood 1981a, 296). The retention of the name *Resheph* in this specific context is a fossil of the Canaanite mythos absorbed into Yahwistic imagery.

### **3.5 *Tiraš* (תִּירָשׁ): The Archaic Wine**

**Biblical Location:** Genesis 27:28; Deuteronomy 33:28; Hosea 2:8.

**Cognate:** Ugaritic *trṯ*; Eblaite *te-rí-iš-tù*.

Hebrew has two main words for wine: *yayin* (the standard term) and *tirosh* (often translated "new wine" or "must"). The term *tirosh* appears frequently in archaic blessings (Isaac's blessing of Jacob) and northern prophecies.

Ugaritic has the cognate *trṯ*, and an Ebla vocabulary of divine names matches a Sumerian deity with the feminine *te-rí-iš-tù*, which "can scarcely be dissociated from Heb. *tîrôš*" (Dahood 1981a, 293–94). The preservation of *tirosh* in the formula "corn and wine" (*dagan v'tirosh*) reflects a fossilized hendiadys; Dahood reads the pair in Hos 7:14 as the names of the Canaanite deities Dagan and Tirosh, "from which the common nouns 'grain and wine' originated" (Dahood 1981a, 293). While *yayin* became the dominant prose term, *tirosh* survived in the fossilized liturgical language of blessing, directly cognate to the LBA poetic tradition.

**4\. Geographic Distribution: The "North" and Transjordan**

A spatial analysis of these lexical fossils reveals a distinct geographic clustering. They are not evenly distributed throughout the Bible but are concentrated in three specific corpora: **Job**, the **Primeval History** (Gen 1–11), and **Northern Prophecy/Poetry** (Hosea, Song of Songs, Qohelet). This distribution suggests a "Transjordanian" or "Northern" provenance for the "Ancient Word."

### **4.1 The Joban-Eblaite Axis**

The Book of Job carries a high density of Eblaite and Arabic cognates (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*); Dahood's survey of Eblaite parallels treats fourteen passages of Job (Dahood 1981a, 301–7). The book is set in the land of **Uz** (*‘Ūṣ*), named in Genesis as a brother of Buz among the sons of Nahor (Gen 22:21).

* **Implication:** The dialect of Job is likely not "standard" Jerusalemite Hebrew but a "Wisdom dialect" of the East/North (Edom/Bashan) that retained closer ties to the Aramaic and Eblaite sphere. Words like *min'lam* (wealth) and *qesitah* (coin) survived in this peripheral zone long after they vanished from the central Canaanite dialects (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

### **4.2 Israelian Hebrew (The Northern Kingdom)**

Scholar Gary Rendsburg has proposed the existence of a distinct dialect of "Israelian Hebrew" (as opposed to Judahite Hebrew), preserved in the northern material of Kings and Judges, in Hosea and Amos, and in the Song of Songs (Noegel and Rendsburg 2009, 4).

* **Fossils:** The relative particle *she'*\- (cognate to Phoenician and Ammonite *'š*) is in preexilic texts limited to northern contexts—the Song of Deborah, the Gideon cycle, the Elisha cycle—and is used consistently in the Song of Songs (Noegel and Rendsburg 2009, 14–15).  
* **Phoenician Connection:** Mitchell Dahood argued for Canaanite-Phoenician influence on the language of **Qohelet** (Ecclesiastes), suggesting a Northern provenance (Dahood 1952), and Noegel and Rendsburg count Qohelet a northern composition of the Persian period (Noegel and Rendsburg 2009, 15). Its *pardes* ("park," Qoh 2:5) is a Persian loanword (BDB, s.v. *pardes*), but if the northern reading is correct, other "late" features of Qohelet may actually be "northern" features (*The Stratigraphy of the Hebrew Bible*).

### **4.3 Toponyms as Fossils**

Place names often preserve archaic vocabulary that has disappeared from common speech.

* **Pishon:** The river Pishon (Gen 2:11) is linked by James Sauer to the "Kuwait River," a fossil river system traced in satellite images by Farouk El-Baz, which crossed Arabia from about 10,000 BCE until it dried up between 3500 and 2000 BCE (Sauer 1996). Its preservation in the text serves as a "geographic fossil," anchoring the tradition in a landscape that physically ceased to exist by the 2nd millennium (*The Paradigm That Cannot See*).  
* **Ashteroth-Karnaim:** (Gen 14:5). Preserves the divine name *Astarte* beside the dual "horns" (*karnaim*).

**5\. Extinction and Replacement: The Mechanism of Loss**

To validate a term as a true "fossil," one must demonstrate its extinction or replacement in the living language. If a word appears freely in the Mishna (2nd century CE) or Late Biblical Hebrew (LBH), it is not a fossil of the archaic period.

### **5.1 The *Qesitah* Extinction**

**Term:** *Qesitah* (Currency). **Replacement:** *Shekel*. The *qesitah*, a unit of unknown value, appears only in the Patriarchal narrative of Jacob's purchase at Shechem (Gen 33:19), its echo in Josh 24:32, and Job 42:11 (BDB, s.v. *qesitah*). The LXX translators (c. 250 BCE) had no idea what the word meant, translating it as "lambs" (*amnas*; Josh 24:32 and Job 42:11 LXX). This shows the term was opaque by the Hellenistic period.

### **5.2 The *Mamlakah* to *Malkut* Shift**

**Term:** *Mamlakah* (Kingdom).

**Replacement:** *Malkut*.

* **Pre-Exilic:** *Mamlakah* is the standard term for "kingdom" in Samuel and Kings (29 occurrences, against two of *malkut*, in 1 Sam 20:31 and 1 Kgs 2:12).  
* **Post-Exilic:** *Malkut*, an Aramaic loan/cognate, gains ground in Chronicles (28 occurrences), Esther (26) and Daniel (16).  
* **Fossilization:** In the dynastic oracle, 2 Samuel 7:12 and 7:16 use *mamlakah*; when the Chronicler retells the history of the monarchy in the Persian period, he substitutes *malkut* in the same verses (1 Chr 17:11, 14). The retention of *mamlakah* in the earlier corpus marks it as a fossil of the pre-exilic administration, immune to the Aramaization that affected later scribes.

### **5.3 The Meaning of *Toledot***

**Term:** *Toledot* ("Generations"). **Cognate:** Akkadian *w-l-d* / Colophon usage. P.J. Wiseman proposed (Wiseman 1936) that the phrase "These are the *toledot* of..." functions as a **colophon** (a concluding signature), mirroring the structure of Mesopotamian tablets ("This is the tablet of..."). In the biblical text, this fossilized structure serves as the "binding" of the Genesis narrative. While the word *toledot* continued to be used for "genealogy," its structural function as a literary divider is a fossil of cuneiform scribal practice (*The Paradigm That Cannot See*).

**7\. Implications: What the Lexical Data Show and Do Not Show**

The comprehensive inventory of lexical fossils presented above establishes the following:

**What the data demonstrate:**
- The Hebrew Bible preserves vocabulary (*'ed*, *mw*, *min'lam*, *tehom*, etc.) that matches Bronze Age cognates in Eblaite, Akkadian, and Ugaritic
- These terms were no longer understood by later translators (Septuagint, Targums confuse or paraphrase them)
- The words function as "fossils"—preserved forms whose original meaning was lost to later scribes
- The density of such fossils is concentrated in Genesis 1–11, Job, and archaic poetry (Song of the Sea, Song of Deborah)

**What the data constrain:**
- A wholly late composition (Persian period) would need to explain how scribes accurately deployed technical Bronze Age terminology they did not understand
- The fossilization pattern is consistent with textual transmission from earlier written sources

**What the data do not determine:**
- The mechanism of transmission (oral tradition, written tablets, scribal copying)
- The motivations of later scribes
- Whether the underlying traditions are "historical" in any modern sense

The lexical evidence speaks only to age and transmission. The fossils mark a "Uruk-Ebla Horizon" beneath the surface of the Masoretic Text—a stratum of vocabulary that predates the composition frameworks typically assigned to the Persian or late Iron Age.
