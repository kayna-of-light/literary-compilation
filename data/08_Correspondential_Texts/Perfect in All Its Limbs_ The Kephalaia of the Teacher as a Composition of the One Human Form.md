## 1. Introduction

The library already holds several studies of the *Kephalaia of the Teacher*, the Coptic compendium of 122 chapters of instruction attributed to Mani (216–274 CE). [*The Ancient Word Recovered*](../00_Framework/The%20Ancient%20Word%20Recovered_%20Extracting%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) separated a correspondential teaching substrate from two later frames, Mani's own Pauline–Marcionite theology and the compiling community's hagiographic staging, and read the substrate through the Swedenborgian key. Among its findings it named the Last Statue the culminating concept of the substrate: a completed cosmic-human body built from gathered and purified spiritual substance (§8.3, Finding 19). [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md) dated what that substrate carries. It took its title from the chapter in which the *Kephalaia* calls its own many names a covering over "a single form, and a single name" (K. 178.13–23). [*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) read the substrate's numbers as properties of the perceiving mind rather than as counts in the world. It found in the *Kephalaia* the meaning of five that Scripture leaves open: five as faculty, the radial order of one capacity from its centre to its surface. It traced that order from mind bound in bone to consideration bound in skin (§§5.3–5.4). [*Three Witnesses to One Architecture*](../06_Mythological_Studies/Three%20Witnesses%20to%20One%20Architecture_%20Genesis%201-11%20and%20the%20Correspondential%20Kephalaia.md) and [*Two Registers of One Perception*](../06_Mythological_Studies/Two%20Registers%20of%20One%20Perception_%20The%20Song%20of%20Solomon%20and%20the%20Regenerative%20Substrate%20of%20the%20Kephalaia.md) set the substrate beside Genesis 1–11 and the Song of Songs. Each of these studies assumes an answer to the question this thesis takes up; none of them asks it.

The question is whether the *Kephalaia* is composed as one human form. Every ancient cosmology uses images of the body, so the issue is not whether the text contains them. The issue is whether the human form is the form of everything the text describes, at every scale it describes. That would include the Father, the realm of light, the two trees, the adversary, the universe, the first human pair, the individual soul in its flesh, the church and the end of history.

Swedenborg made exactly this claim about heaven in 1758. "That heaven in its whole complex reflects a single man is an arcanum hitherto unknown in the world," and the angels "call heaven the Greatest Man and the Divine Man" (*Heaven and Hell* §59). Each society is a man in a lesser form and each angel in the least form (§§68–73), and all of it is from the Lord's Divine Human (§78). Hell in its whole complex "reflects a single devil" (§553). And he wrote that "by the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," a truth "which they derived from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319).

The companion theses take the *Kephalaia* substrate to be a surviving body of that ancient knowledge. If they are right, the Greatest Man should not appear in it as one doctrine among others. It should be the shape of the whole.

The question has to be answered from the text, and the text has to be reliable. This thesis reads the *Kephalaia* in Iain Gardner's English translation of the Berlin edition (Leiden: Brill, 1995). It cites the text by page and line of the Coptic as "K.", and Gardner's introduction and chapter commentaries by the page of his book. It rests no argument on the library's reconstructed Coptic readings. It treats Gardner's square brackets as the line between what the papyrus preserves and what an editor has supplied.

The library's typeset edition of the substrate, *The Ancient Word* (version 1.1), prints Gardner's English with the Manichaean names replaced by Persian ones. It is used here only as a concordance (§15.2), because two of the names it replaces are the names in which the body is spoken of: "the Perfect Man" becomes "Hearkening" and "the Last Statue" becomes "the Final Body".

Read in this way, the answer to the question is plain, and it is in Gardner's words. The bodies appear in this order:

- **The Father.** The Father of Greatness "ex[ists and is] established in his five light limbs," which are "mind, thought, insight, counsel and [consideration]" (K. 64.20–23). The five fathers who go out against the darkness came "from the five limbs [of the Father]" (76.15–25). Love and the Father "are a single living body, the Father [a]nd his love" (156.3–4).
- **The two trees.** The good tree of the Gospel parable has five limbs, from consideration, which is the holy church, to mind, which is the Father (20.12–20), and the soul climbs to the Father through them (20.21–31). The bad tree has the same five limbs (21.28–30). Gardner calls it "an exact negative" of the good (p. 23).
- **The adversary.** The King of Darkness has "five shapes on him," on head, hands and feet, shoulders, belly and tail, each from one of his five worlds (30.33–31.2; 77.27–78.3).
- **The universe.** The enemy constructed the universe "after the likeness of a man": "His ribs are all the firmament[s]. His navel [is the] sphere of the stars and the signs of the zodiac," and "His heart is mankind" (90.20–91.1). Mani says outright that "this whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body," so that "the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos" (169.28–170.2; 170.16–19).
- **Adam.** Adam bears "the seal of the entire universe" (157.25–26).
- **The soul in its flesh.** Sin "bound the mind in bone; the thought in sinew; the insight in vein; the counsel in flesh; the consideration in ski[n]" (95.17–19): the highest faculty in the innermost tissue and the lowest in the outermost. The Light Mind releases those members and will "construct a new man of them," with "five great living members" (96.25–30).
- **The Perfect Man.** The Pillar of Glory is "the Perfect Man; the one who bears up under all things" (35.10–12). The First Man is "the head" whose sons are "the limbs [of] his soul," and his head "is placed on the body of the Pill[ar of Gl]ory in the heights of the universe" (84.21–30).
- **The end.** At the end the counsel "will gather to it its own soul, and sc[ul]pt it in the Last Sta[t]ue." It gathers "the life and the li[gh]t that are in all things" and builds them "upon its body. The time when this Last Statue will be perfect in all its limbs, then it can become free and ascend" (54.14–21). The souls that would not be gathered weep because "their limbs have been severed" and they are "separated from the Last Statue" (149.29–150.4).
- **The names.** The many names under which all this is taught "are a single [power] since the beginning." Stripped of their names, they "will gather together [and] make a single form, and a single name, unaltered and unchangeable for ever" (178.13–23).

Gardner reads the text without the correspondential framework, and he describes the same thing in his own vocabulary. "In Manichaeism," he writes, "what is true in the macrocosmos is also true for the microcosmos." "All the divine is ultimately one, so that the diversification represents levels of descent," and relationships between spiritual levels "will be an exact image of that relationship all the way back up the scale through the gods to the Father" (p. xxvi). He identifies the Perfect Man as "the representation of the church as the body of Jesus in its ascent to the Father" (p. xxxii). He describes the Last Statue as the gathered soul rising as "a divinity that in essence recapitulates the descent into the abyss of the First Man" (p. xxxiii). In his commentary on chapter 31 he states the whole schema in a few sentences: the First Man is "the head upon which the soul depends for its life"; the gathered soul is "his trunk"; "This trunk is the Pillar of Glory which reunites with the First Man at its head. At the end of time it is the Last Statue" (p. 86).

**The central finding** is that the human form is the constant of the composition. The same five-limbed form appears as:

- the Father;
- the realm and church of light;
- the cosmos;
- Adam;
- the regenerate individual;
- the body completed at the end of time.

The same form turned inside out appears as the King of Darkness. Every register the text describes is a body, and the bodies are one body at different magnitudes.

This is the Greatest Man as Swedenborg described it: the whole, the society and the angel, all from the Divine Human, with hell as the same form inverted. And it is set out in a text that Swedenborg could not have known, because it was not published in a European language until Polotsky and Böhlig's edition of 1940, nor in English until Gardner's of 1995.

**The central argument** is that the agreement is not a coincidence between two speculative systems. Two distinct features of the *Kephalaia*'s body have no reason to coincide with Swedenborg's description if the form were invented.

The first feature is the order of the members. The soul's five faculties are bound into the body from the inside out, mind in bone and consideration in skin. Swedenborg calls this order simultaneous: in it "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). In the same section he compares the successive order of the degrees to "a column divided into three stories, through which ascent and descent are made." In simultaneous order, he says, the column becomes like "a solid body" whose finest parts are in the centre. The *Kephalaia* applies both of his descriptions to a single figure. It calls the Pillar of Glory a column, through which the gathered light ascends, and it calls the same figure a man, the Perfect Man.

The second feature is the way the body completes itself. Swedenborg watched a society of heaven take shape: it "appeared at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man," and those who were not of it were cast out (*Heaven and Hell* §68). "Those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (§64). The Last Statue is likewise sculpted gradually from the remnant of all things until it "will be perfect in all its limbs," and it "sweeps out and casts from it the polluti[on] that is foreign to it" (K. 54.15–17).

Both features are structural facts about two bodies of text, and anyone can check them.

What the thesis does not find is any arithmetic architecture. The *Kephalaia* counts constantly, and in a few places it tries to build one number out of others. It makes twelve hours of a day out of five sons, five sons and two more (25.21–30). It makes twelve parts out of ten and two (49.14–50.5). It makes twenty-five limbs out of fives (262.25–263.5). These are exactly the places where Gardner, who has no stake in the question, finds the system strained. He calls them "somewhat artificial" (p. 29), "an undoubtedly forced calculation" (p. 54) and "rather convoluted calculations" (p. 268). He notes that the texts "have difficulty reconciling the sets of five and twelve that structure Mani's system" (p. 67).

Read as counts, five and twelve compete. Read as qualities they do not, because they are two qualities of the same body and nothing has to be added to anything. Five is the radial order of one faculty. Twelve is "the things of love and the derivative faith in one complex" (*Arcana Coelestia* §577). The strain the text shows when it sums is the strain of describing a whole as a total. The reading proposed here, and in *The Resolution of the Finite Mind*, removes that strain; it does not need the sums and does not rest on them.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading

**Correspondence**, in the sense developed by Swedenborg and used throughout this library, is the organic relation between a natural thing and the spiritual reality it expresses. It is grounded in what the natural thing does. Light corresponds to truth because light lets the eye distinguish one form from another, which is what the understanding does with ideas. Fire corresponds to love because fire is the active principle that gives light its existence, as love gives wisdom its existence. A garment corresponds to the truth that clothes a good because it covers and presents what it clothes. Because the relation runs through the function, a correspondence can be read: given the thing and what it does, the spiritual sense can be recovered, not assigned.

Four exclusions keep the term from sliding into its usual counterfeits:

- **Not allegory.** Allegory substitutes one term for another by convention, as scales stand for justice, and could substitute any other term as well. A correspondence cannot be reassigned without breaking the function.
- **Not metaphor.** Metaphor says that A is like B. Correspondence says that A is B at the natural degree: one reality seen at two levels, not two things compared.
- **Not symbol in the modern sense.** A symbol is a conventional sign pointing to an abstract concept. A correspondence is the spiritual reality itself present in its outermost form.
- **Not a Jungian archetype.** An archetype reduces a spiritual reality to a projection of the psyche. On this reading the psyche is itself an effect of the spiritual reality, not its source.

Three further properties matter here:

- **Correspondence is directional.** It runs from inside to outside, from cause to effect: the natural is the spiritual "in ultimates," never the other way round.
- **Correspondence has an opposite sense.** The same image expresses a good or an evil reality according to context. Fire can be love or the burning of self-love; water can be living truth or the flood of falsity. The opposite sense is not ambiguity. A system grounded in function inverts its images exactly where the function is turned against its end, and a system assigned by convention has no reason to invert them at all.
- **Correspondence is ordered by discrete degrees.** Love, wisdom and use, or the celestial, the spiritual and the natural, are not three points on one scale. They are three complete levels, each containing the one below it. Swedenborg says they stand one above another in successive order, and one within another in simultaneous order (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §§205–206). That second arrangement turns out to be the one the *Kephalaia* uses for the body (§7).

For this thesis one application of the doctrine governs all the others: the correspondence of the human body with the whole. Swedenborg's claim is not that the universe is *like* a man. It is that heaven *is* a man, and that the things of the human body exist because heaven's uses flow into them. "This inflow of heaven is into the functions and uses of the bodily members; and the uses, since they are from the spiritual world, take on a form by means of such things as are in the natural world, and thus present themselves in effect. From this is the correspondence" (*Heaven and Hell* §96).

On this view a member of the body is a use made visible, and the body as a whole is the form that many uses take when they serve one life. The test for whether a text is written in this correspondence is therefore not whether it mentions limbs. The tests are:

- whether its members are defined by what they do;
- whether the same members recur at every scale;
- whether the parts are ordered as the uses are ordered;
- whether the whole is one life rather than an assembly.

Two cautions follow from the framework's own rules. First, the persons of the *Kephalaia* — the Father, the First Man, the Living Spirit, the Ambassador, the Pillar of Glory, the Light Mind — are read here, as the library reads the persons of the Nag Hammadi texts, as states and functions rather than as characters. Each is identified by its relation and its work, and a shared name does not merge two figures. Second, the correspondential reading does not cancel the historical one. The *Kephalaia* is a Manichaean book with a dualist theology. Gardner's account of that theology is accurate at the natural level, and this thesis does not dispute it (§13.4).

The question asked here is a structural one: whether the human form governs the composition. The answer to that question can be checked by anyone who reads the text, whatever they think of correspondences. The meaning of the answer is where the correspondential reading does its work.

### 2.2 The Text: Gardner's Translation and What the Brackets Allow

The *Kephalaia of the Teacher* survives in a papyrus codex from the cache of Manichaean books found at Medinet Madi, south-west of the Fayum, and announced in 1933. Gardner dates the Coptic texts of that library to about 400 CE and describes them as translations of Syriac originals reaching back to Mani or to the first generations of his followers. They are written in the Lycopolitan (sub-Akhmimic) dialect (p. xviii). The Berlin pages were edited by H. J. Polotsky (K. 1–102) and A. Böhlig (K. 103–291), with the closing pages supplied by W.-P. Funk (K. 292–295) (p. xxi n. 4). Gardner's translation runs to the end of kephalaion 122 (K. 295.8) (p. xxi).

This thesis cites the text throughout as K. followed by the page and line of the Coptic, as Gardner prints them. A chapter is named by Gardner's number when the chapter matters to the argument. Gardner prefaces each chapter with a short commentary; that commentary, and his general introduction, are cited by the page of his book.

The papyrus is damaged, and Gardner's square brackets reproduce the reconstructions of the edited Coptic. Some of those reconstructions are certain from parallels; others are an editor's best guess. This thesis follows one rule: **no inference turns on a word inside a bracket**.

Where a word the argument needs is restored, the thesis says so and looks for a parallel in which the same word is preserved. Two examples show how this works:

- In chapter 6 the King of Darkness's head is "[lion-faced]" only by restoration (K. 30.34). In chapter 27 the same head "is lion-faced, wh[ich] came about from [the] world of fire" in preserved text (77.29–30), so the lion-faced head may be used.
- In chapter 38 the "twelve members" of the new man stand at the edge of a lacuna as "[the tw]elve members" (97.7). That passage is therefore not used as evidence that the new man has twelve members, although the Father's "twelve light limbs" are preserved (64.24).

Gardner's round brackets mark his own explanatory additions, and his angle brackets mark words added to the primary text. Both are treated as his, not as the papyrus's.

The library also holds its own working versions of the text. This thesis does not use them as evidence, for two reasons:

- **The Coptic transcription.** The library's Coptic transcription of the codex is still being corrected against the manuscript, and its readings are not yet reliable enough to quote.
- **The typeset edition.** The typeset *Ancient Word* (version 1.1) is built on Gardner's English, but it restores lacunae "by the same correspondences that organize the text itself" (preface, p. 5). It also rearranges the chapters by a structure discovered in the extracted substrate, and it replaces the Manichaean names with Persian ones. A restoration made from the correspondential architecture cannot be evidence for that architecture, as [*The Garment and What Wears It*](The%20Garment%20and%20What%20Wears%20It_%20Dating%20the%20Correspondential%20Substrate%20Beneath%20the%20Manichaean%20Kephalaia.md) already argued in setting its circularity guard (§2.4).

The edition is therefore used here only to let its readers find their place. Section 15.2 gives a concordance of Gardner's names with the edition's names. Readers of the edition should note in particular that "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" appears there as "Hearkening" and "the Last Statue" as "the Final Body". Neither renaming bears on the argument, which reads the titles Gardner prints.

One further matter belongs to method rather than to the text. The library's stratigraphy divides the *Kephalaia* into three layers:

- a correspondential substrate (Layer 1);
- Mani's Pauline–Marcionite frame (Layer 2);
- the community's hagiographic frame (Layer 3).

Some of the body vocabulary studied here is plainly Pauline in its wording. Gardner derives "the new man" "ultimately... from Paul" (p. xxxi). He glosses "the Perfect Man" with Ephesians 4:12–13, the passage in which the church grows "unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fulness of Christ" (p. xxxii).

This thesis does not run the stratigraphy again. It asks a question that sits beneath the stratigraphy: whether the human form governs the composition as it stands. The answer has two consequences. First, a form that governs passages of every layer cannot be credited to the wording of any one of them. Second, a Pauline name for that form is evidence that Paul also knew it, not evidence that Mani borrowed it from him. Section 13.3 returns to this point.

### 2.3 The Recognition Vocabulary: Swedenborg's Greatest Man

Swedenborg is used in this thesis as a vocabulary of recognition, not as a standard against which the *Kephalaia* is graded. He published his account of the Greatest Man (*Maximus Homo*) between 1749 and 1763, nearly two centuries before the Medinet Madi codices were found. Where his description and the *Kephalaia* agree, the agreement is between two independent derivations of one structure. Where they differ, the difference is reported as such.

The test is for the state, never for the name. Swedenborg has no figure called the Pillar of Glory, and the *Kephalaia* has no phrase "the Greatest Man". What can be compared is what each text says a whole is, how its parts are ordered, and how it comes to completion.

The account is gathered in *Heaven and Hell* §§59–102 and in the series on the Grand Man that runs through the interchapters of *Arcana Coelestia* from §3624. It can be set out in seven propositions, each with its own section reference.

**First, heaven as a whole is one man.** "That heaven in its whole complex reflects a single man is an arcanum hitherto unknown in the world, but fully recognized in the heavens," and the angels therefore "call heaven the Greatest Man and the Divine Man" (*Heaven and Hell* §59). The arcanum is old: "the universal heaven is so formed as to correspond to the Lord, to His Divine Human; and... man is so formed as to correspond to heaven in regard to each and all things in him, and through heaven to the Lord" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3624).

**Second, the form is the same at every magnitude.** "In the most perfect form generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals, with simply such a difference as there is between like things of greater or less magnitude" (*Heaven and Hell* §62). Each society of heaven is therefore a man (§68), and so is each angel: "As heaven is a man in largest form, and a society of heaven in a less form, so is an angel in least" (§73). The likeness holds "because in the heavens the goods of all are from a single love, that is, from a single origin" (§72).

**Third, the members are uses, and a whole is one life.** "So many different things in man act as a one, because there is no least thing in him that does not do something for the general welfare and perform some use" (*Heaven and Hell* §64). The societies of heaven are arranged "as the members, organs, and viscera are in man" (§94), and the inflow of heaven "is into the functions and uses of the bodily members" (§96). *Divine Love and Wisdom* draws the consequence for the felt unity of a person: "man feels them all as one... because all these are in such a form that not one can be lacking" (§22).

**Fourth, the form is from the Divine Human, and the Divine is its head.** "It is from the Lord's Divine Human that heaven as a whole and in part reflects man" (*Heaven and Hell* §78). In all the heavens there is "no other idea of God than that He is a Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §11). "Because God is a Man, the whole angelic heaven in the aggregate resembles a single man, and is divided into regions and provinces according to the members, viscera, and organs of man" (§288). For the church, Swedenborg uses the image of head and body. "The body spiritual is the church; its head is God-Man"; a church that thought of several gods "would appear as one body with several heads; thus not as a man, but as a monster" (§24). And the human being is human only by reception: "the Lord alone is Man, and an angel and a spirit, and also a man on earth, are men in exact proportion to what they have from Him" (*Arcana Coelestia* §4219).

**Fifth, the degrees of the form are ordered by height in the whole and by depth in the body.** In general, "the highest or third heaven forms the head down to the neck; the middle or second heaven forms the breast down to the loins and knees; the lowest or first heaven forms the feet down to the soles, and also the arms down to the fingers" (*Heaven and Hell* §65).

The same degrees also have a second arrangement. In successive order they stand "like a column divided into three stories, through which ascent and descent are made"; in simultaneous order the column subsides, and "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference," as "in a solid body" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205).

Within the body, the parts with least life lie at the frame and at the surface. The societies to which the bones, cartilages and skins correspond "have little spiritual life in them, just as there is little life in the bones as compared with the soft parts which they enclose" (*Arcana Coelestia* §5560; see §5552). Yet the skins of a regenerate man appear "a beautiful weaving of spirals wonderfully intertwined," and those of the deceitful appear "like knottings together of mere serpents" (§5559).

**Sixth, the form completes itself by gathering, and it casts out what is foreign to it.** Swedenborg describes watching a society of heaven separate hypocrites from itself. "The entire society appeared at first like a single indistinct body, then by degrees in a human form, but still indistinctly, and at last clearly as a man." Those "who were not in the man and did not make up the man were hypocrites; these were cast out" (*Heaven and Hell* §68). The rule behind the separation is use: "those who do not perform uses for the common good are cast out of heaven as something heterogeneous" (§64).

The inmost of each person tends toward the same form from the beginning. "The primitive of man, which is from the soul of the parent, endeavors toward the formation of the whole man in the ovum and the womb," and "everyone is as it were the center of all, for he is the center of influxes from all through the heavenly form" (*Arcana Coelestia* §3633).

**Seventh, hell is the same form inverted.** There is no single devil: "by 'the devil' and 'Satan' there hell is meant," and every one of the myriads in hell "is a devil in accord with his opposition to the Divine" (*Heaven and Hell* §544). Swedenborg was not shown the form of hell as a whole, but was told that "as the entire heaven in one complex reflects a single man... so the entire hell in one complex reflects a single devil." At the gates of particular hells "a monster commonly appears that represents in a general way the form of those within" (§553). The forms there "were all forms of the love of self and the love of the world," which "are diametrically opposite" to the loves that constitute heaven (§554).

To these seven propositions belongs one historical claim, which is the framework's prediction for this thesis. "By the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," and "the ancients called man a microcosm, or little universe, from truth which they derived from the knowledge of correspondences, in which the most ancient people were, and from their communication with angels of heaven" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). "All things of the created universe, viewed in reference to uses, represent man in an image," and this "testifies that God is a Man" (§319, heading).

If the *Kephalaia* substrate is a remnant of that ancient knowledge, the propositions above should be recognisable in it. They need not appear in Swedenborg's words. They should appear as features of its composition:

- one life in many members;
- the same form at every magnitude;
- members defined by use;
- a head from which the whole lives;
- the degrees ordered from within outward;
- a completion that gathers and expels;
- an adversary that is the same form inverted.

Sections 3 to 10 look for each of these features in turn, and §12 gathers the result.

### 2.4 Numbers as the Quality of One Whole

A text about a body with limbs invites counting, and the *Kephalaia* counts at every turn: five limbs, five sons, five storehouses, twelve wisdoms, twelve judges, fourteen vehicles, eighteen thrones, twenty-five limbs, fifty days. The reading of these numbers decides what kind of structure the thesis can claim. It therefore has to be fixed before the text is read.

Swedenborg's rule is that numbers in a text written in correspondence signify things, not quantities. "All numbers in the Word signify things," and "multiplied numbers have a like signification as the simple numbers from which they arise by multiplication" (*Heaven and Hell* §73, notes, citing *Arcana Coelestia* §§5291, 5335, 5708, 7973). Of seven he says that, "in the internal sense," it "partakes not at all of the idea of number. For they who are in the internal sense, as angels and angelic spirits are, do not even know what number is, and therefore not what seven is" (*Arcana Coelestia* §716). Twelve "signifies faith, or the things of love and the derivative faith in one complex" (§577), and "wherever it occurs in the Word, signifies all" (§3272). Ten signifies remains (§576). The eighth day, as the first day of a new week, "signifies any beginning whatever" (§2044).

[*The Resolution of the Finite Mind*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Resolution%20of%20the%20Finite%20Mind_%20Celestial%20Perception%2C%20Numerical%20Architecture%2C%20and%20the%20Limit%20of%20Spiritual%20Analysis.md) developed this rule into the framework's account of number (§2.4). On that account a number is not a count of things in the world. It is a property of the perceiving mind: the form in which the spiritual mind receives a whole that is in itself undivided. The celestial mind, which perceives the whole directly, has no use for number. The spiritual mind receives the whole through distinction, and its distinctions have irreducible dimensions, which the primes name:

- **two:** the bilateral distinction of good and truth;
- **three:** the vertical distinction of the degrees;
- **five:** the radial order of a faculty from its centre to its surface;
- **seven:** the arc of regeneration (§§4.4, 7.1).

The study found the meaning of five in the *Kephalaia*, where Scripture leaves it open. Five there is the number of faculty. It is the five intellectual qualities of mind, thought, insight, counsel and consideration, ordered from innermost to outermost and bound into bone, sinew, vein, flesh and skin (§§5.3–5.4).

Two consequences of this account govern the present thesis.

**Composite numbers are read by their prime factors only.** The factors say which dimensions of one whole are in view; a higher power of the same prime deepens the resolution along that dimension without changing its meaning (*Resolution* §7.2). On this reading:

- twelve (2 × 2 × 3) is love and faith across the degrees, taken as one complex, as *Arcana Coelestia* §577 says;
- twenty-five (5 × 5) is the faculty, five, seen at a finer resolution;
- ten (2 × 5) is the faculty in its good and its truth;
- fourteen (2 × 7) is the arc of regeneration in its two sides;
- eighteen (2 × 3 × 3) is the bilateral and the vertical, resolved more finely along the vertical.

None of these numbers is a sum. No composite number is read here as the total of smaller sets that a text has assembled.

**The arc of seven is plainly a sequence, but it is not read as a sum.** Regeneration does pass through stages, and the six days before the seventh show it: "the six days of creation, and... the seventh, which is the celestial man, in whom is peace, rest, and the sabbath" (*Arcana Coelestia* §395). As a sequence, the seven is two triads and a rest, three layers of which the third is the centre. But the regenerated person in whom the sequence is complete is not seven of anything. The person is one body regenerated whole and at rest, each member of which is one part of that body. The successive order of the stages has become the simultaneous order of the person, in which "the highest things of successive order... are in the inmost, the lower things are in the middle, and the lowest in the circumference" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). Seen in time, the seven is an arc. Seen as a person, it is one body, every element of which stands in its place in the whole, as in the Greatest Man.

This settles what the thesis may claim about structure. It does not claim that the *Kephalaia* is built on an arithmetic plan: not five plus five plus two making twelve, not sums that come to seven, eighteen or twenty-five, not any architectural integer whose components can be totalled. The body the thesis finds is not a total of members. It is one life, whose members are its uses, and its numbers are the qualities in which that life is perceived.

The *Kephalaia* sometimes does sum, and §11 shows that those are exactly the places where Gardner finds the system forced. The thesis does not explain those sums away. It reports them, reports Gardner's judgement of them, and shows that the body stands without them.

| Two | 2 | The bilateral: good and truth, will and understanding |

## 3. The Body of the Father: Five Limbs and One Living Body

The *Kephalaia* nowhere describes the Father of Greatness as an abstraction. When Mani is asked how the Father "is established and determined," the answer is anatomical. Chapter 21 opens:

> Hear [this lesson t]hat I will recount to you. Very great is [land of] light in the presence of they who will hear and know it, due to the first Father of Greatness. How he ex[ists and is] established in his five light limbs, which are [ ... the store]houses that have neither limit nor measure to the[m ... ] mind, thought, insight, counsel and [consideration ... ] his twelve light limbs that are his twelve wisdoms. Now, there are five great light lim[bs] in each one [ ... ] (K. 64.17–26)

The papyrus is broken at the edges of the passage, but its structure survives. The Father "exists" and "is established" in limbs: five of them, named as the five intellectual qualities, and twelve of them, named as twelve wisdoms. "Five great light lim[bs]" are found "in each one." The list of five ends in a lacuna at "consideration," but nothing hangs on the restoration. The same five qualities, in the same order and with the same last term, are preserved elsewhere in the codex: in the binding of the soul (95.17–19), in the limbs the First Man sent forth (85.1–2), and in the five virtues of the new man (96.30–97.3).

Gardner's comment on the chapter is exact: "The kingdom of light is a manifestation of the Father himself, which he pervades by his qualities or limbs" (p. 67). In his introduction he puts it more strongly: the realm of light "indeed is an extension of himself," and the Father "resides in five intellectuals or limbs: mind, thought, insight, counsel and consideration" (p. xxvi).

What the chapter goes on to say confirms that a living body is meant and not a list of attributes. From the Father "five grea[t] springs of praise gush for[th]" (64.26–27). Three survive in legible form: "his light that enlig[htens]," which "gushed forth from him and pour[ed]" into the aeons (64.29–30); "the perfume of the [ ... ]," which "pervades all the aeons of greatness" (64.31–32); and "his living voice" (64.33). Light, perfume and voice are what a living body sends out and what another body receives through eye, nostril and ear. The fourth and fifth springs are too broken to read, and the thesis builds nothing on them. Of the fifth only a fragment survives, but it uses the language of the Last Statue: it "was sculpted in them... se[a]led in the[m]" (65.4–5).

A single short chapter then says where the saving powers come from. Chapter 25, "[Concerning the Advent of Five Fathers from the Five Limbs of the Father]," reads in full:

> Once again the apostle speaks concerning [the advent of five fathers fro]m the Father, one after another. [He] speaks t[hu]s: [The Ambas]sador [came] from his mind; the Beloved of the Lights [from his thought]; the Mother of Life from his insight; [Jesus] the Beloved from his counsel; the Virgin of Lig[ht f]rom his consideration. So in this way were summone[d these five] fathers. These five advents came from the five limbs [of the Father]. One came from another and was manifested in its season. (K. 76.17–25)

The powers that go out from the Father into the work of salvation are not separate gods summoned from elsewhere. They are the Father's own limbs going out. Each comes from one faculty of his body, and they come "one after another," "one... from another," each "in its season." The successive order of the outgoing — one season after another — is the unfolding of an order that already exists simultaneously in the Father's body as limbs.

This is the first appearance in the text of the pattern traced in §2.3: what is a sequence in time is a body in the whole.

A third chapter shows that the relation of the Father to his realm is repeated at every level of the system. In Chapter 20, "The Chapter of the Name of the Fathers," Mani explains why several divine figures may be called "father." The reason is the same each time. Each father has a "greatness" in which "he lives" and "is established":

- **the Father of Greatness:** "His greatn[ess is the gr]eat earth where he lives, he being established [i]n it" (63.30–31);
- **the Third Ambassador:** his greatness is "the light ship of living fire" (63.35–64.1);
- **Jesus the Splendour:** his greatness is "the ship of living waters where he lives" (64.2–3);
- **the Pillar of Glory:** its greatness is "the five gods of ho[ly ... ] which [li]ve and are established in them" (64.5–7);
- **the Light Mind:** "His gre[atness] is the [holy] church; becaus[e] he lives [and is established i]n it" (64.8–10).

Several of these clauses are partly restored, but the formula — a father, his greatness, his living and being established in it — survives in each. Gardner's summary is that the name "can also properly be used of the derived gods in their own realms of greatness" (p. 67).

Read structurally, the chapter says that the system is made of one relation repeated at different magnitudes. In each case there is one who dwells and the dwelling in which he lives and is established: a life and its body. The Father and his land, the Ambassador and his ship, the Pillar and its five gods, the Light Mind and the church each stand in that relation. This is Swedenborg's "generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals" (*Heaven and Hell* §62), stated as a rule of naming.

Chapter 63, "Concerning Love," turns the relation into an identity:

> [Lo]ve is the Father of Greatness, who dwells in his glorious land. The entirety of the divine has been revealed [there]in! These two are a single living body, the Father [a]nd his love; because he has given himself for every thing, as he exists in his aeons. Indeed, due to this, they called the Father, the Lord of the totality, "love." For he gave the victory to his aeons and his limbs. (K. 156.1–7)

The chapter then applies the same formula one level down:

> [These] two, the Mind and the church, a single body is also their likeness; because, again, the apostle too shall gi[ve] his [own] self for his church... Likewise, too, the beginning of ri[ght]eousness is made of love for the church, according both to body and to spirit. While in the body, in the church here; so in the spirit, in the heights above. (K. 156.10–19)

Three points in this passage carry the argument:

- **The ground of the unity.** The Father and his love are not two beings in alliance. They are "a single living body," and what makes them one body is the Father's giving of himself: "he has given himself for every thing." The body of the Father is constituted by self-giving, and his limbs are where the giving goes.
- **The repetition at every level.** The relation repeats exactly at the level of the church. "The Mind and the church, a single body is also their likeness," and again the ground is self-giving: the apostle "shall give his own self for his church," as the Gospel saying about laying down one's life for one's friends confirms (156.14–16, Gardner noting John 15:13).
- **Body and spirit as two degrees.** The one body exists "according both to body and to spirit," below "in the church here" and above "in the heights." These are not two bodies. They are the same body in two degrees.

Two further passages complete the picture of the Father as the head and inmost of a body. In Chapter 60 the corporeal body is used to explain the visible and the hidden in the divine: "Just as [the] corporeal body has limbs that are hidden, so it also has others that are visi[b]le... So it is also with this entire greatness, as we resemble it" (151.9–14). The Father is the body's hidden governor: "Now, thus the directive of the bo[dy] that lives within, hidden in the storehouses inside the body and unseen. So it i[s] also how the Father, the God o[f] truth, exists; as he too is hidden in his kingdom" (151.17–21). Gardner's summary calls this "the analogy of the corporeal body" and glosses the Father as "the directive faculty... of the body, hidden in his own kingdom" (p. 158).

Chapter 39 ends the history of salvation in the Father's own measure. On the third great day "the Father will unveil over them his [image]," receives them "in to his hidden treasur[y]," "gives [ ... ] his soul," and will "perfect their stature in sa[m]e measure as his first aeons. And he sets each power [i]n its aeon, in its dwelling-place" (103.10–22). The end of the story is a body brought to the stature of its source, with every member in its own place.

**Spiritual reading.** The correspondential reading of these chapters states plainly what the text shows in pieces: the Father of Greatness is God as Man. Swedenborg's "In all the heavens there is no other idea of God than that He is a Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §11) is the doctrine that Chapter 21 presents as anatomy.

The five limbs are the Divine love and wisdom in the radial order in which a mind receives them, from the inmost (mind) to the outermost (consideration). The twelve limbs that are twelve wisdoms are the same Divine considered as love and faith across the degrees in one complex (*Arcana Coelestia* §577). The two sets are not rival inventories to be reconciled (§11). They are two ways in which one body is perceived.

The land of light, which Gardner calls "an extension of himself," is heaven made by the Divine. "It is from the Lord's Divine Human that heaven as a whole and in part reflects man" (*Heaven and Hell* §78). That is why the Father "exists" and "is established" in his limbs: the limbs are not his possessions but his presence.

The saying that the Father and his love "are a single living body... because he has given himself for every thing" is the correspondential centre of the chapter. Love is the Divine's own being, and love gives itself. The body of heaven exists because the Divine has given itself into receivers, which is influx. Its limbs are the places where that giving is received. The same law holds one degree down: the Mind and the church are one body because the one who leads the church gives himself for it. Swedenborg puts the same relation as head and body: "The body spiritual is the church; its head is God-Man" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §24).

The five fathers who come "from the five limbs" are the Divine proceeding. In the text's order the powers go out one after another; in the Father they are already present at once as members. This is successive order unfolding from simultaneous order. The Divine does not acquire powers in the course of salvation. It sends out what it already is.

The chapter on the names of the fathers makes the pattern a rule. At every magnitude a life dwells in a greatness and is established in it, and at every magnitude that relation is a human one. The end the text anticipates — the Father unveiling his image and perfecting the stature of every power in its own dwelling-place — is the Greatest Man complete.

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|

| The five light limbs (mind to consideration) | The Divine love and wisdom in the radial order of a faculty, inmost to outermost |
| The twelve light limbs, twelve wisdoms | The same Divine as love and faith across the degrees in one complex (*AC* §577) |
| The land of light, "an extension of himself" | Heaven, made by the Divine of the Lord |

## 4. The Two Trees: The Ladder of the Limbs and Its Exact Negative

The first teaching chapter of the *Kephalaia* is an exposition of a Gospel parable: "The good tree shall give [good fr]ui[t]; also [the] bad [tr]ee shall give b[ad] fruit... [One kn]ows [each tree by] its fruits" (K. 17.5–9; Gardner notes Luke 6:43–44). Gardner observes that the parable had already served Marcion as "a proof-text for dualism," and that "from [Marcion] it must be traced to Mani" (p. 22).

Mani begins by rejecting the sects' readings of the two trees, which compare them with plants growing "on this composite earth" (17.16–20; 17.33–35). Then he gives his own reading, and it is anatomical. The trees have limbs. The same text later says, from the other direction, that "the body is like the earth, wh[ile these] limbs als[o ... ] of it are like the trees" (281.6–8). In chapter 2 the trees are bodies.

The good tree is introduced as a body clothed in the soul's own substance. It is "the intellectuals that are cloaked upon its body," and "the light elements that are mingled, being compounded in the totality" (20.1–3). Its fruit is "glorious Jesus the Splendour, the father [of] all [the apo]st[l]es," and "the taste of the fruits of the [good] tree [is the] holy [c]hurch" (20.4–6). Then its limbs are named:

> [ ... ] it has five limbs. [They are consideration, counsel, insight, thought, mind. I]ts consideration is the ho[ly] church. [Its counsel] is [the Pil]lar of Glory, the Perfe[ct] Man. [Its insight is the Fir]st Man who dwells in the ship of [living] wat[ers]. Its thought is the Third Ambassador [who dwells in] the ship of living fire, that shines in [ ... A]lso, the min[d] is the Father who dwells in great[ness, who is perfe]ct in [the] aeons of light. (K. 20.11–20)

The list at the head of the passage is restored, and so are two of the five equations. But the preserved words give the series without doubt: consideration, then "[the Pil]lar of Glory, the Perfe[ct] Man," then the First Man in a ship, then the Third Ambassador in the ship of living fire, then "the min[d]," which "is the Father."

The passage that follows removes any remaining doubt about the order, because it shows the souls climbing the limbs one by one:

> It is consideration that shall [ ... ] they shall be raised up [ ... ] is counsel; [they sh]all be raised up to insight, which is the First Man [who d]wells in the ship of the night; fr[om] insight they shall ris[e u]p to thought, which is the Ambassador who dwells in [the sh]ip of the day. And he too, great glor[ious] thought, [shall bring] them in to mind; which is the Father, the Go[d] of truth, the great Mind of all the [ae]ons of glory. (K. 20.24–31)

The good tree, then, is a ladder of five limbs, and each limb is two things at once. Each is a faculty of the soul: consideration, counsel, insight, thought, mind. Each is also a station of the cosmos through which the purified light passes: the church, the Pillar, the ship of the night, the ship of the day, the Father.

The soul's ascent through its own faculties and its ascent through the heavens are one movement described twice. Gardner sees this and values it highly: "The limbs of the good tree are the five intellectual qualities through which the soul ascends to the Father and the aeons of glory (20.21–31). This equation of the internal mental process of salvation with the eschatological journey through the heavens indicates a living spirituality in Mani's teaching that is often missed by commentators" (p. 22).

In the terms of §2.3 this is the successive order of the degrees: "a column divided into... stories, through which ascent and descent are made" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205). The second rung of that column is the figure the text calls "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man." Section 8 shows that the same figure is both the column of the ascent and a man.

Then Mani names the bad tree:

> [ ... The] bad [tree] has five limbs. Th[ey are consid]eration, counsel, insight, thought, [mind]. [It]s c[onsideration is] the law of de[at]h from which the sect[s] take instruction. I[t]s counsel is [t]ransmigration [ ... ] in typ[e after type]. Also, its insight is the furnaces of [fire ... ge]henna; which is full of [s]moke. Thought [ ... ve]ssel. Its mind is the [ ... ] the lump, the last b[o]nd, the [ ... ], they that Satan ca[st away]. (K. 21.28–36)

The bad tree has the same five limbs as the good, with the same names. It is not a different kind of body; it is the same body with every limb turned the other way:

| Limb | In the good tree | In the bad tree |
|---|---|---|
| Consideration | the holy church | the law of death from which the sects take instruction |
| Counsel | the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man | transmigration in type after type |
| Insight | the First Man in the ship of living waters | the furnaces of fire, gehenna, full of smoke |
| Thought | the Third Ambassador in the ship of living fire | [lost] … vessel |
| Mind | the Father, the great Mind of all the aeons | the lump, the last bond |

The movement through the limbs is also turned round. On the good tree the souls "shall be raised up"; on the bad tree they "are swall]owed i[n] to the lump, and they are bound," and they "will arrive at that place; and they have no repentance therein" (22.15–20). Gardner's summary is again exact: "As an exact negative of this the evil tree is also made up of five intellectual qualities. However, in this case the mental process is directed towards sin and darkness; and the corresponding eschatological journey takes this soul to the ultimate death where it is compounded with the final 'lump' beyond all hope of redemption" (p. 23).

The chapter closes with the sentence Gardner calls "an important semi-creedal assertion of belief in absolute dualism (22.35–23.13)" (p. 23):

> Blessed is [every one ... ] these [t]wo trees, and separates them on[e] from [an]other. He understands that they did not arise out of one another, nor did [th]ey come from one another. They did not come from one! The person who can separate them out may go up to the aeon of light... but whoever will not separate out [ ... ] from [o]ne another, he falls to the land [of darkness]. (K. 22.35–23.9)

This sentence is taken up in full in §13.4. Here only the structural fact matters. The same chapter that denies the two trees a common origin also gives them the same anatomy, limb for limb, and Gardner, without any correspondential interest, calls the result "an exact negative." Whatever the text says about origins, it says about form that there is one form, held in two opposite orientations.

**Spiritual reading.** On the correspondential reading the two trees are the human mind in its two possible orientations. Each tree has the five faculties that every human mind has, from the outermost (consideration) to the inmost (mind), so each is the human form.

The good tree is the mind turned toward the Lord, and in it the faculties are a way up. What is received in the outermost faculty is raised through the inner ones to its source. This is regeneration seen as ascent: the natural mind (consideration, the church in which the catechumens' alms are "purified") opening into the spiritual and then into the celestial. Swedenborg's angels "look to Him as the Only One, the source of all things" (*Heaven and Hell* §64). The good tree's top limb is "the Father, the God of truth, the great Mind of all the aeons."

The bad tree is the same mind turned toward itself. In it each faculty is filled with its opposite:

- **consideration** becomes a law of death, instruction that kills;
- **counsel** becomes the endless round of re-embodiment, the will turning from form to form without rising;
- **insight** becomes the furnace and the smoke, fire in its opposite sense, the burning of self-love that blinds instead of giving light;
- **mind**, the inmost, becomes "the lump, the last bond," the ruling love of self binding everything beneath it into one mass.

These are what Swedenborg describes as the forms of hell: "forms of the love of self and the love of the world," "diametrically opposite" to the loves of heaven (*Heaven and Hell* §554).

The fruit of each tree is its state made visible. The good tree's fruit is the Splendour and its taste is the church; the bad tree's taste is "these evil people, the sects... bound in law after law" (21.19–22). One knows the tree by its fruit because the fruit is the tree in ultimates.

The beatitude on the one who "separates them" names the decisive act of the regenerating mind. It must distinguish what belongs to the Lord from what belongs to self, and refuse to let the second pass for the first. Swedenborg makes the same distinction in his own terms: "none but good uses were created by the Lord," and "evil uses arose out of hell" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §336). Good does not grow into evil, and evil does not grow into good. "They did not arise out of one another." The one who sees this "may go up." The one who confuses them falls.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| A tree with five limbs | The human mind in its five faculties, from consideration (outermost) to mind (inmost) |
| The good tree | The mind turned toward the Lord; regeneration as ascent |
| The limbs as a ladder of ascent | The degrees in successive order (*DLW* §205) |
| Consideration = the holy church | The natural mind receiving and purifying; the church as the outermost receiver |
| Counsel = the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man | The column of ascent; the body of the regenerate (see §8) |
| Insight = the First Man in the ship of the night | Interior understanding in the natural light (the moon) |
| Thought = the Ambassador in the ship of the day | Spiritual understanding in the light of the sun |
| Mind = the Father | The inmost, the Lord as the source of the mind's life |
| The fruit (the Splendour) and its taste (the church) | The state of the tree made visible in ultimates |
| The bad tree with the same five limbs | The same mind turned toward self; the opposite sense of every faculty |
| The law of death | Instruction that kills: truth falsified into a rule against life |
| Transmigration in type after type | The will circling in natural forms without ascent |
| The furnaces of fire, gehenna, smoke | Fire in its opposite sense: the burning of self-love that darkens |
| The lump, the last bond | The ruling love of self binding the whole into one mass |
| Separating the two trees | Discrimination of good from evil; the judgement of the regenerating mind (*DLW* §336) |

## 5. The Anti-Body: The King of Darkness and the Monster at the Gate

If the human form governs the composition, the adversary should be a body as well, and a body of a particular kind: the same form, turned against itself. The *Kephalaia* gives the King of Darkness two full descriptions, in chapters 6 and 27. Gardner judges the chapter 6 account "highly schematic." He says it shows "a certain attention to what can be termed the psychosymbolism of evil," with "a stamp that is unmistakably Mani's" (p. 34).

The opening of chapter 6 sets out the realm from which the King comes, and it is a realm of fives that do not match:

> Five s[to]rehouses have arisen since the beginning in the land of darkne[ss! The] fiv[e] elements poured out of them. Also, from the five e[le]ments were fashioned the five trees! Again, from the five tre[es] were fashioned the five genera of creatures in each world, male and female. And the five worlds thems[el]ves [ha]ve five kings therein, and five spiri[ts, five] bodies, five [tastes]; in each world, they n[ot] resembling [one another]! (K. 30.17–24)

Then the King himself:

> Now, regar[ding the King] of Darkness, there are five shapes on him! His head [is lion-faced; his] hands and feet are demo[n- and devil]-faced; [his] shoulders are eagle-faced; while h[is] belly [is dragon-faced]; his tail is fish-faced. These five shapes, the marks of his [fi]v[e] worlds, exist on the King of the realms of Darkness! (K. 30.33–31.2)

Several of the animal names in chapter 6 are restored. Chapter 27 repeats the description with its reasons, and between the two chapters each shape is preserved at least once:

> Now, f[ive forms] exist in his body, corresponding to (the archetype) [of the five] creations that exist in the five [wo]rlds of darkness. Indeed, his head is lion-faced, wh[ich] came about from [the] world of fire. His wings and his [shoulders] are eagle-faced, after the likeness of the children of the wind. [His hands] and [h]is feet are daemons, after the likeness [of the children] of the [w]orld of smoke. His belly is [dragon]-faced, [after the likeness of the] world of darkness. His tail [is the] form of the fish, which belongs to [the world of the ch]ildren of water. (K. 77.27–78.1)

Where a chapter is broken, the other supplies the word. The kings of the separate worlds confirm each shape in preserved text. The King of Fire "is lion-faced, the fore[mo]st of all the beasts" (33.9–10); the King of Wind "is eagle-face" (33.17); the King of Water "is fish-face" (33.25); the King of Darkness "is a dragon" (33.33). In chapter 27 the King "shall crawl on his [bell]y like the children of dark[ness]" (78.9–10). Gardner's note on "corresponding to (the archetype)" gives the literal sense as "[the appearance] of the seal" (p. 79 n. 32).

The point that matters for the argument is not that the King has a body. It is the kind of body he has. Every member of it comes from a different world. Its head is a lion, its shoulders are an eagle, its hands and feet are demons, its belly is a dragon and its tail is a fish. Chapter 6 states the principle in a single clause: the five worlds and their kings, spirits, bodies and tastes exist "in each world, they not resembling one another" (30.24). The King is a body whose parts do not resemble one another or the whole. He is an assembly of incompatible forms, held together by force.

The rest of the two chapters develops this body as the inverse of a living one:

- **What it is made of.** It is made of the five "other properties": "his darkness... his putridity... his ugliness... his bitterness, his own soul... his burning" (31.3–7).
- **Who shaped it.** It did not grow; it was made from outside. "Matter, who is the though[t] of death," is "the o]ne w[ho] sculpted him from the nature of the land of darkness" (31.9–11), and so he is "h[ar]der than every [ir]on, copper and steel and [lea]d" (31.12–13).
- **What it can see.** It has no access to interiors. The King knows the speech of his worlds, but "their heart is not manifested to him. He k[n]ows not their mind nor their thought... Rather, he only knows and apprehends what is before his eyes" (32.10–13); "he knows not what is far from him" (32.26–27).
- **What its life is.** It has no life of its own: "There is no life [in him] whatsoever, but his 'life' is the bile of [anger] that is app[arent] on his face" (78.31–33).
- **What its members do.** Its members do not hold together as members. "When it pleases him to move, he spreads all his limbs out and walks. When it comes to mind, he with[draws] his limbs and takes them in, and is rolled (?) to his companions; and he falls to the ground like a grape and a great iron ball!" (32.14–19).

The last image is exact. A living body keeps its members in their places. The King's body can draw its members in until it is a single mass, a ball of iron. This is what the bad tree's inmost limb already named: "the lump, the last bond" (21.34–35).

Finally, the anti-body is not located only at the beginning of time. Its members are present now, in human life:

- the spirit of the King of Smoke "reigns today in the principalities and authorities of the earth... humiliating mankind with tyranny" (33.5–8);
- the spirit of the King of Fire reigns "in the greater ones and the leaders," and "is found in these sects that worship [fi]re" (33.13–16);
- the spirit of Wind is "idolat[ry]" in "every temple" (33.21–24);
- the spirit of Water "reigns today in the sects of error; these that [d]ip the baptism of the waters" (33.29–31);
- the spirit of Darkness "speaks till today in the soothsayers" (34.2–3).

Mani ends the chapter by turning to his own: "Concerning this I tell you, my [bret]hren and my limbs, the perfect faithful, the holy elect... Put behind you the service of their five bodies. Live not in them" (34.6–11). In chapter 27 the warning is personal: "do not [lay upon] yourselves... the shapes of this ruler... Hold yourselves secure from his midst and [h]is wicked teachings that dwell in your body" (79.2–6).

Two bodies compete for the same persons as their members. The elect are the apostle's "limbs." They are warned not to live as limbs of the five dark bodies, whose shapes can be "laid upon" a person and whose teachings "dwell in your body."

**Spiritual reading.** Swedenborg never saw the form of hell as a whole. He was told that "as the entire heaven in one complex reflects a single man... so the entire hell in one complex reflects a single devil" (*Heaven and Hell* §553). He was also told that there is no single devil apart from the inhabitants of hell: "by 'the devil' and 'Satan' there hell is meant," and "everyone of them is a devil in accord with his opposition to the Divine while he lived in the world" (§544). The King of Darkness answers to both statements.

As the description of chapters 6 and 27, he is the whole of hell in one form. As the spirits that reign "today" in tyrants, in leaders, in idolatry and in soothsaying, and as shapes that a person may "lay upon" himself, he is made of human beings in their opposition. His members are those who serve "their five bodies." His body exists in so far as people live in it.

The form of that body is the precise inversion of the Greatest Man. In heaven "generals are like the parts, and parts are like the generals" (*Heaven and Hell* §62), because all are "from a single love" (§72). The King is a body whose parts "do not resemble one another," because each comes from its own world and its own appetite. This is the monster of *Divine Love and Wisdom* §24: a body whose parts take their direction from several heads instead of one, "thus not as a man, but as a monster." It is also the monster that appears at the gates of the hells, which "represents in a general way the form of those within" (*Heaven and Hell* §553).

The animals are correspondences, and in this context they are in their opposite sense:

- **The lion** at the head is ruling power as domination, "the fore[mo]st of all the beasts."
- **The eagle** at the shoulders is the soaring of the intellect in its opposite sense, reasoning that flies over truth and seizes it.
- **The demon** at hands and feet is the active and the walking life in the service of falsity.
- **The dragon** at the belly is the appetite that receives and digests, in the sensual mind that crawls.
- **The fish** at the tail is the lowest natural, the merely factual, in its cold and sliding form.

Each part is the use of a human member perverted, and the body as a whole has no use but itself.

The other features of the anti-body are what Swedenborg reports of hell's inhabitants:

- **Their faces.** Faces "hideous, and void of life like those of corpses," and speech "like the speech of anger or of hatred or of revenge" (*Heaven and Hell* §553). The King has no life but "the bile of anger" on his face.
- **Their loves.** These forms are "all forms of the love of self and the love of the world" (§554).
- **Their sight.** A love of self knows only what is before its eyes. It cannot see into another's heart, because it has no interiors open to receive another's life, and so the King "knows not their mind nor their thought."
- **Their making.** The love of self is not a growth from within but a hardening imposed from outside. The King is "sculpted" by "the thought of death," just as, at the other end of the book, the Last Statue sculpts itself from the life it gathers (§9).
- **Their collapse.** In the end the love of self draws every member into itself until nothing remains but a mass. The King "withdraws his limbs" and falls "like a great iron ball," and the bad tree's inmost limb is "the lump, the last bond."

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| The King of Darkness as one body | Hell in its whole complex as "a single devil" (*HH* §553) |
| The spirits of the kings reigning "today" in persons and institutions | Every inhabitant of hell a devil according to his opposition (*HH* §544) |
| Five shapes from five worlds, "not resembling one another" | The parts unlike the whole: the monster, the inverse of the Greatest Man (*HH* §62; *DLW* §24) |
| The lion-faced head | Ruling power as domination (opposite sense) |
| The eagle-faced shoulders and wings | Reasoning that soars over truth to seize it (opposite sense) |
| Demon hands and feet | Action and conduct in the service of falsity |
| The dragon belly | Sensual appetite; the mind that crawls |
| The fish tail | The lowest natural, cold and sliding (opposite sense) |
| Sculpted by Matter, "the thought of death"; hard as iron | The love of self as a hardening imposed from without |
| Knows only what is before his eyes; the heart hidden from him | A mind without open interiors |
| No life but "the bile of anger" on his face | Faces void of life; speech of anger (*HH* §553) |
| Spreading and withdrawing the limbs; falling like an iron ball | The love of self contracting every member into one mass; the lump |
| "My brethren and my limbs" warned not to live in the five bodies | Human beings as members of heaven or hell according to their life |

## 6. The Universe Built After the Likeness of a Man

Three chapters of the *Kephalaia* describe the visible universe as a human body, each from a different direction:

- **Chapter 38** begins from the universe and finds a man in it.
- **Chapter 70** begins from the human body and finds the universe in it.
- **Chapter 64** finds both in Adam, on whom "the seal of the entire universe is stamped."

A short fourth chapter, 60, uses the body to say which parts of the divine are seen and which are hidden. Gardner's chapter notes leave no doubt about the theme. Chapter 38 "demonstrates the correlation between the macrocosmos and the microcosmos of the human body" (p. 93); in it "the universe is constructed in the form of a human" (p. 94). Of chapter 70 he writes that "the basic theme here is the correspondence between the micro- and macro-cosmos" (p. 179).

**Chapter 38: the universe as a man.** A disciple asks how the Light Mind, a great and immeasurable god, can "come and appear in the smallness of the bo[dy]" (89.32–33). Mani answers by describing the great body first:

> All the error, when the en[e]my of the lights constructed it, he constructed after the likeness of a man. The head of the universe is the beginning of the garments. [H]is neck is the nape of the garm[ents]. His stomach [is the] five unfolded ones (?), which a[re ... ] of the garm[e]nts. His ribs are all the firmament[s]. His navel [is the] sphere of the stars and the signs of the zodiac. And, also, [the par]ts [that] come from his navel to his hip are [ ... ] that come from the sphere t[o the corne]rs of the four worlds. His loins are the th[ree eart]hs that are below [ ... u]pon the head of the Porter... His heart is mankind. His liver is the four-footed animals. His lung is the race of birds that [fly in] the air. His spleen is the race of fish that swim in the waters. His kidneys are the world of reptiles that creep [up]on the earth. His outer skin is the wall that [ ... which] surrounds the piercing and the great fire... His veins [ ... ] all [the spri]ngs and wells. (K. 90.20–91.11)

The description runs from head to feet and then from heart to skin. The outer frame of the body is the frame of the cosmos: head, neck, ribs, navel, hips, loins, shins and feet laid along the heavens, the firmaments, the zodiac, the four worlds and the earths. The inner organs are the living things that fill the frame. The heart is mankind, and the liver, lung, spleen and kidneys are the four other kinds of animal life: beasts, birds, fish and reptiles.

Mani then names what keeps this body alive and in order: "F[iv]e g[od]s are fastened in him. [ ... ] they are his soul and his life" (91.15–16). Five "sleepless guardians" stand watch in it (91.18), each identified with one of the five faculties:

- the Keeper of Splendour, "the mind" (91.20; "set firm in the great mind," 92.12–13);
- the King of Honour, "who is the thought" (92.24–25);
- the Adamant of Light, "who is the insight" (91.25);
- the King of Glory, "who is the coun[sel ... ] patience" (91.27–28), "the great counsel" (93.9–10);
- the Porter, "who is the consideration" (91.31).

At the centre of the whole the Ambassador "placed in them the great Mind; who is the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man" (92.5–6). Gardner's summary is that "the five sons of the Living Spirit are the intellectual qualities, who act as sleepless guardians, each firmly established in its place," and that "the Pillar of Glory, the Perfect Man, is the Mind in the universe" (p. 94).

Mani then turns from the great body to the small one:

> So also is this body! A mighty power lives here, even if it is small in its stature. Nevertheless, s[in] dwells within, and the old man who is lodged in it... until the Lig[ht] Mind finds how to humble this body, and drive it [according to] his pleasure. Just so, in the w[atch-]districts of his great outer brothers, they who are masters in the zone. In them, in the great body: the earthquake and treachery happened from time to time. So, also, is the watch-district of the Mind, which is the body of the flesh. (K. 94.17–26)

The comparison holds both ways. The universe is "the great body," the individual is "this body," and the Light Mind keeps watch in the small body as the guardians keep watch in the great one.

The preceding short chapter, 37, has already given the principle of construction. The Living Spirit "suspended the zones on their zones," hanging "the five powers of darkness on the five shining men, the sons of the M[a]n," while "the entire edifice is suspended from" the five sons of the Living Spirit (89.4–11). The universe is a series of layers each hung from the one within it.

**Chapter 70: the body as a universe.** Chapter 70 begins from the other end, and its title states its thesis: "Concerning the Body: It was constructed after the Pattern of the Universe." Mani's opening sentence states the principle in both directions:

> This whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body; [as the formation of this body of flesh ac]cords to the pattern of the universe. Its head is like the first-fruits of the five garments. And, from its neck down to the site of its heart, it resembles the pattern of the ten firmaments. And again, the heart accords to the wheel of the rotating sphere. And, from its heart down to its intestines (?), is like this atmosphere that extends from the sphere down to the earth... And also, from its intestines (?) down to its loins is like the three earths. And also, its shinbones to the pattern of the space in which the Porter stands. Its footsoles to the great earth upon which the Porter is stood... Its liver to the vessel of fire. Its flesh to the vessel of darkness. Its blood to the vessel of water. Now, this is how the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos in its firmaments, in its orderings, in its mountains, its walls and its vessels. (K. 169.28–170.20)

The first clause, "This whole universe, above and below, [re]flects the pattern of the hum[an] body," is preserved apart from two letters. The closing summary, "this is how the small body corresponds to the macro-cosmos," is preserved entire.

The chapter then does what chapter 38 did, but in the regenerate person. The universe has "five great camps," each kept by one of the five sons of the Living Spirit (170.21–171.11). "This is also the case with this body that the e[le]ct wear. There are another five camp[s] there, and the Light Mind is watching over them, and the new man who is with him" (171.29–172.4). Each camp in the person is a member brought under order, and each such mastery "resembles" one guardian:

> The elect person who will control and subdue the configuration of his face, which is above his body, and guide it towards the good; he resembles the mystery of the Keeper of Splendour... Whoever will become master [over] his heart and subdue it; he is the sign of the great King of Honour... Whoever will become master to his genitals and subdue his lust; he is the mystery of the Adamant of Light, the one who subdues Matter. Whoever will subdue the stomach and become master to the fire that is in him, and also purify the nourishment that comes in to him; he resembles the likeness of the King of Glory, the one who turns the wheels, sending the life above. Whoever will become master over the ruling-power that is beneath in his feet, and bind it with the chain of peace; he is like the Porter. (K. 172.4–20)

Then two further correspondences close the series. "The teaching of wisdom, which circulates in that body," is "to the likeness of the Virgin of Light, who goes up and comes down." "Love and joy, faith and truth, in which the person should l[iv]e," are "to the likeness of these two light ships. For, the living s[o]ul should go up in them" (172.20–29).

**The order is by use, not by position.** Taken together, chapters 38 and 70 give the same five at three registers: a guardian of the cosmos, a faculty of the mind and a member of the regenerate body. Each is drawn from the preserved statements of the two chapters:

| Guardian (son of the Living Spirit) | Faculty (ch. 38) | His watch in the universe (ch. 70) | Member mastered in the elect (ch. 70) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keeper of Splendour | mind | the battlement of all the works; the three heavens | the face, "above his body" |
| King of Honour | thought | the seven firmaments | the heart |
| Adamant of Light | insight | from the firmament down to the earth; the sphere and atmosphere | the genitals and lust; "subdues Matter" |
| King of Glory | counsel, patience | the three wheels of wind, water and fire; the three earths | the stomach, its fire and its nourishment; "sending the life above" |
| Porter | consideration, wisdom | the great earth and its four fastenings | the feet, bound "with the chain of peace" |

The order of the members in the last column is not anatomical. In the body the stomach lies above the genitals, but the text sets the genitals third and the stomach fourth. It does so because it assigns each member by what it does. The Adamant "subdues Matter," so he answers to the mastery of lust. The King of Glory "turns the wheels, sending the life above," so he answers to the stomach that purifies "the nourishment that comes in to him." What the five have in common is their use, not their position in space.

**Chapter 64: Adam.** Between the great body and the small stands the first human being. Chapter 64 explains that "three great things that are distinct were reveal[ed] in Ad[am] the first human" (157.3–4):

- "The image of the exalted one was placed upon him," for his makers "sealed him after the shining form of the image that had been displayed to them above" (157.7–9).
- He "was shaped in his structure" by light, so that "the formation of his soul fits over the correct distribution of the elements" (157.12–18).
- "The teachings and counsels and the seal of all the powers above and below" were "gathered" and "sealed" in him. "(Adam) and his consort Eve became a dwelling and home for the signs of the zodiac and the stars; and the months, the days and the years. For the seal of the entire universe is stamped upon Adam" (157.20–26).

The rulers who formed him intended to use the seal against the light. Among their seven purposes, Adam was to be "a seal-ring and a new impression, so [that] all births who will be begotten might be brought forth after his s[eal]" (158.12–14). Adam is the macrocosm in the form of a single man, and the form passes to everyone born of him.

**Chapter 60: the hidden and the visible.** The short chapter 60 uses the same body for a different purpose. "J[u]st as [the] corporeal body has limbs that are hidden, so it also has others that are visi[b]le... So it is also with this entire greatness, as we resemble it" (151.9–14). The members of the divine body are then assigned:

- the Father is "the directive of the bo[dy] that lives within, hidden in the storehouses inside the body and unseen" (151.17–21);
- the sun and moon are its "two eyes that are visible and seen, and are even the body's source of illumination" (151.22–27);
- the living soul is the part of the body that "can be grasped and mastered" and "receive blows and wounds" (151.28–152.1);
- Jesus the Splendour is the tongue, "that speaks, proclaims, reprimands and reveals about everything happening in this body," who "manifests and reveals about everything, both the external and the internal" (152.1–10).

**Spiritual reading.** These chapters state the doctrine Swedenborg attributed to the ancients: "By the ancients man was called a microcosm, from his representing the macrocosm," and they knew it "from the knowledge of correspondences" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §319). The *Kephalaia* does not merely compare the universe to a body. Its claim is that the universe "reflects the pattern of the human body" and that the human body "accords to the pattern of the universe." That is the claim of correspondence, which says that the two are one form at two magnitudes, and it is what Swedenborg means when he writes that "all things of the created universe, viewed in reference to uses, represent man in an image" (§319, heading).

The text even agrees with Swedenborg about the key to the image. Swedenborg says that the image "is not seen when these things are viewed in their forms, it is seen only when they are viewed in respect to uses" (§322). The *Kephalaia* assigns the members of the regenerate body to the guardians by use, against their position, and the image holds because it is read by use.

Chapter 38's claim that the enemy "constructed" the error "after the likeness of a man" needs careful reading. In Mani's mythology the mixed world is made from the bodies of conquered demons, and the light is held in it as in a prison. The structural point is that even the work of the enemy is a man. The text knows no other form in which a world could be built. Swedenborg makes the same observation about hell, which is the inverse of the human form and yet, "among themselves," appears "as men" (*Heaven and Hell* §553).

The form is the Lord's. Opposition can only bend it, not replace it. The universe of the *Kephalaia* is therefore the human form in the state the text calls mixture: the natural degree, in which the life of the spiritual is present but bound. Its "soul and life" are the five sons of the First Man, and it is held in order by five guardians who are the faculties of a mind.

In the language of the framework this is the natural mind in which the spiritual is present but not yet free. Its faculties stand as watchmen at their posts, and in its watch-districts "the earthquake and treachery happened from time to time." Swedenborg's account of the natural is the same in kind. The natural is the outermost of the simultaneous order, the circumference within which the higher degrees are present (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §205), and it is where the work of regeneration is done.

That is why chapter 70 can treat the mastery of each bodily member as the likeness of a cosmic guardian. To govern the face, the heart, desire, appetite and gait is to bring the outermost of the human form into order under its inmost, which is what the guardians do for the universe.

The single correspondences agree with Swedenborg's account of the provinces:

- The head of the universe is "the beginning of the garments," and the head of the body is "the first-fruits of the five garments": the inmost and highest, as the head is the third heaven in the Greatest Man (*Heaven and Hell* §65).
- The heart is mankind in the great body and "the wheel of the rotating sphere" in the small. In both, it is the centre about which the life of the whole turns.
- The stomach, which "purif[ies] the nourishment," answers to Swedenborg's societies of "various purifications of good and truth" (§96).
- The feet, standing on the great earth, are the lowest good on which the whole rests (§96).
- Jesus the Splendour as the tongue "that... reveals about everything... both the external and the internal" is Swedenborg's province of the mouth and tongue, "the ability to converse from understanding and perception" (§96).
- The Father as the hidden "directive" inside the body is the inmost from which the whole lives unseen.

Adam, finally, is the Greatest Man in least form. "The seal of the entire universe is stamped upon Adam" is the *Kephalaia*'s way of saying what Swedenborg says of every angel: "As heaven is a man in largest form, and a society of heaven in a less form, so is an angel in least" (*Heaven and Hell* §73). Adam bears "the image of the exalted one" because the human form is the image of the Divine. Those who formed him wanted to rule "through him" because whoever holds the human form holds the key to the whole. On the correspondential reading, the rulers' seven purposes (158.1–23) are the love of self's attempt to make the human form its own instrument. The good "induce him to life because of their image and shape placed upon him" (157.28–29). The form is theirs before it is anyone else's.
