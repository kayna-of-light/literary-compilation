## Background Context

The Correspondential Hypothesis proposes that the 32 geometric signs cataloged in European Upper Paleolithic caves are not arbitrary cultural conventions but a universal vocabulary arising from shared human perception of ontological realities. If signs map real spiritual correspondences rather than local cultural codes, then:

- **Geographically isolated populations** with no cultural contact should independently develop the **same core lexicon**
- The signs should appear in **similar spiritual/ritual contexts** across cultures
- The correspondence should hold even when cultural "content" differs dramatically

### The Prediction

> If the signs map ontological reality, newly discovered Ice Age art in unconnected regions (e.g., Sulawesi, Amazonia, Australia) should utilize the *same* geometric core lexicon for similar spiritual contexts (e.g., spirals in birth/creation contexts, hand stencils marking presence), even without direct cultural contact.

### Initial Evidence

- **Sulawesi, Indonesia** (40,000+ BP): Hand stencils and scalariforms discovered — same signs as European caves, no possible cultural contact
- **Australian rock art**: Hand stencils ubiquitous across deep time
- **African Middle Stone Age**: Crosshatch patterns at Blombos Cave (75,000 BP) — predates Out-of-Africa migration

---

## Research Question

**Primary Question**: What geometric signs appear in Paleolithic and deep-time rock art traditions outside of Europe, and how do they compare to the European 32-sign inventory?

### Specific Sub-Questions

1. **Sulawesi Inventory**: What geometric signs have been documented in the Sulawesi cave art? Beyond hand stencils and scalariforms, what else appears? What are the dates?

2. **Australian Rock Art**: What geometric signs appear in the oldest Australian rock art traditions? Do spirals, dots, lines, circles, and other forms from the European inventory appear?

3. **African Antecedents**: What geometric signs appear in Middle Stone Age African sites (Blombos, Apollo 11 Cave, etc.)? Given that two-thirds of the European signs were brought FROM Africa, what is the African "source inventory"?

4. **Amazonian Rock Art**: What is known about the oldest rock art traditions in South America? What geometric signs appear in early (pre-Columbian, ideally Pleistocene) contexts?

5. **Contextual Comparison**: When the same sign appears in different regions, does it appear in similar contexts (e.g., spirals in "entrance" or "transformation" locations; hands at boundaries)?

6. **Quantitative Overlap**: What percentage of the European 32-sign inventory appears in each of these independent traditions?

## What Would Confirm the Prediction

- **High overlap** (>50%) between European inventory and signs in Sulawesi, Australia, Africa
- Signs appearing in **functionally similar contexts** across regions (e.g., spirals at cave entrances, hands at thresholds)
- Evidence that the geometric vocabulary is **older than regional differentiation** — carried out of Africa as part of shared cognitive heritage
- Scholars noting the "universality" of certain motifs without cultural contact explanations

## What Would Falsify the Prediction

- **Low overlap** (<25%) between regions — each region developed unique geometric vocabulary
- Same signs appearing in **unrelated contexts** (e.g., spirals as random decoration in one region, meaningful symbols in another)
- Evidence of **convergent cultural evolution** better explaining the overlap than shared perception

## Critical Warnings

1. **DO NOT conflate** "hand stencil" as a single phenomenon — examine whether they appear in similar threshold/boundary contexts
2. **DISTINGUISH** between truly ancient (Pleistocene) traditions and more recent rock art that may reflect cultural diffusion
3. **NOTE** dating uncertainties — some regional chronologies are contested
4. **DO NOT assume** absence of evidence is evidence of absence — many regions are under-surveyed
5. **BEWARE** of assuming "shamanic" interpretation explains cross-cultural similarity — this is the trap we're testing against
