# The Looking-Glass of Theology: A Systematic Reconstruction of Swedenborgian Correspondence in the Narrative Architecture of Lewis Carroll

## Reading the *Alice* Books and *Sylvie and Bruno* in Correspondence, and What the Record Shows of the Route by Which Their Content Reached Carroll

---

> **Abstract.** This study reads Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, *Through the Looking-Glass* and the two volumes of *Sylvie and Bruno* in correspondence, and sets beside that reading what the documentary record shows of how their content reached him. The two questions are kept apart. The reading, which quotes every Swedenborg locus it uses, finds that Carroll's worlds follow the rules Swedenborg gives for the spiritual world: time is the measure of state, so that the Hatter's time stops with his state (*Heaven and Hell* §§163–164); place is state, so that Alice reaches the Red Queen by walking away (*HH* §§192, 195); judgement is by present choice, so that the courts that punish before the act are absurd; and the states of consciousness in *Sylvie and Bruno* are the two Swedenborg reports of himself (*HH* §§440–441). Carroll's essay "Eternal Punishment" holds the same account of judgement in his own words. The record shows no direct route from Swedenborg. Carroll's recorded library holds no work by Swedenborg, he names him nowhere that could be searched, and he named Sinnett and psychical research as the source of his states. The documented routes are his friends George MacDonald, who held the correspondence of inward and outward as his own and named Swedenborg its "European master", and F. D. Maurice, whose teaching that eternity is a state Carroll argued over with him in 1863 and carried through his fiction. Carroll is therefore a convergent witness, not a transmitter: he reached Swedenborg's account through other routes and his own reasoning. On the framework's premise that correspondence is the structure of reality, that convergence is stronger evidence than transmission would have been.

> **Keywords.** Lewis Carroll, Charles Dodgson, Swedenborg, correspondences, *Heaven and Hell*, time and state, space and state, eternal punishment, αἰών, F. D. Maurice, George MacDonald, *Sylvie and Bruno*, convergent witness, transmission

## 1. Introduction

Charles Lutwidge Dodgson (1832–1898), the Oxford mathematician and deacon who wrote as Lewis Carroll, has long been read as a writer of "nonsense": *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865), *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871) and the two volumes of *Sylvie and Bruno* (1889, 1893) as play with logic and language, free of doctrine. That reading has been giving way. Karen Gardiner's doctoral study of Carroll's theology, built on letters F. D. Maurice wrote to him during the Jowett controversy of 1863, concludes that "Lewis Carroll's imaginative works contain theology within the nonsense, and that he was particularly influenced by the eschatology of F. D. Maurice" and the Broad Church circle around him (Gardiner 2023, 297). The theme she finds running through Maurice and Carroll alike is "the understanding of eternity as a state which is unrelated to time or space" (Gardiner 2023, 298–299).

That theme is the meeting point of this study with the library's framework. Swedenborg wrote that angels "have no notion or idea of time and space", because "in heaven instead of years and days there are changes of state" (*HH* §§162–163), and that "change of place is nothing else than change of state" (*HH* §192). The library's companion documents read the stories of the Grimms, Andersen and Disney as worlds in which the environment answers to the state of the soul (*The Mirror of the Invisible*; *The Celestial Botany of the Fairy Tale*; *The Animating Spirit: A Cultural History*). Carroll's worlds do the same thing: the Hatter's time stops at six o'clock after his quarrel with Time; in the looking-glass country one reaches the Red Queen by walking away from her; in *Sylvie and Bruno* space and time bend to the state of the one who perceives them. These are correspondential readings of Carroll's texts, and this study sets them out.

It also asks a second question, which must be kept apart from the first: by what route did this content reach Carroll? The answer the record gives is precise. No document shows that Carroll read Swedenborg. He never names him in any of his writings that could be searched. His library, so far as it is recorded, held three books in which Swedenborg appears through other authors (Emerson, the hostile Asa Mahan, and Swedenborg's English editor Garth Wilkinson) and none of Swedenborg's own; that record is itself about half complete (Lovett 2005). What the record does document are two friendships. George MacDonald, the first reader of *Alice*, knew Swedenborg's writings and stated the correspondence idea as his own: "Such symbols are the true bodies of the true ideas" (MacDonald 1868). F. D. Maurice, whose eschatology Carroll's fiction carries, taught that eternity is not endless duration. And Carroll himself, in the only finished essay of a planned book on religious difficulties, argued that endless punishment for finite sin would be unjust and that the doctrine turns on the meaning of the single word αἰών (Carroll 1899).

The finding, then, is a convergence. The account of time, space and judgement that Carroll built into his fantasies is Swedenborg's account in content, point for point where the texts can be set side by side; it reached him through Maurice's Broad Church theology, through MacDonald, and through his own reasoning, not through Swedenborg's books. On the library's own premise that correspondence is ontological law, open to more than one route of access (*The Transmission of the Ancient Word*), this is what one should expect: a careful mind working honestly on eternity and on the moral order of the world arrives where Swedenborg arrived. Carroll is therefore a convergent witness, not a transmitter, and the evidence for that is in his own words and his friends'.

Section 2 sets out the method and separates the record from the reading. Section 3 gives the record of Carroll's contact with Swedenborg: the library and Carroll's own writings. Section 4 gives the two documented channels, MacDonald and Maurice. Section 5 reads Carroll's essay "Eternal Punishment" against Swedenborg. Sections 6 to 8 read *Wonderland*, *Looking-Glass* and *Sylvie and Bruno* correspondentially, with every Swedenborg locus quoted from the text. Section 9 states what the record supports, what the reading shows, what follows for the framework, and what remains unknown.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading

Correspondence, in Swedenborg's sense and the library's, is the relation between a natural thing and the spiritual reality it is the effect of. It is not allegory, where a sign is assigned to a meaning by an author; it is grounded in function, so that a thing corresponds to what it does. Swedenborg gives the animals as his first example: "The animals of the earth correspond in general to affection, mild and useful animals to good affections, fierce and useless ones to evil affections" (*Heaven and Hell*, hereafter *HH*, §110). The relation runs one way, from within outward: the spiritual is the cause, the natural the effect. In the spiritual world this is visible as appearance. Places, distances and the passage of time are there the outward form of states of mind: "change of place is nothing else than change of state" (*HH* §192), and "instead of years and days there are changes of state" (*HH* §163).

A correspondential reading of a story therefore asks what each natural feature of the story's world is the outward form of. Where a story's world behaves as Swedenborg says the spiritual world behaves (time stopping with a state, distance lengthening with reluctance, the evil holding themselves in their own suffering), the reading names the correspondence and gives the locus in Swedenborg's text. It is a reading of the text. It does not by itself say anything about where the author got the idea.

### 2.2 Record and Reading

This study keeps two kinds of claim apart. A claim of record says what a document shows about Carroll: what he owned, wrote, read, said and to whom. It is true or false by the documents, and only a document can carry it. A claim of reading says what Carroll's text is, read in correspondence. It stands on the text and on Swedenborg's words, and it does not need a channel from Swedenborg to Carroll in order to stand.

The two kinds meet in one question: the route by which the content reached the author. A route is a claim of record. The library's framework allows several routes to the same content: direct transmission of the codified system, mediation through others who hold it, and an access that needs no transmission because the content is in the nature of things (*The Transmission of the Ancient Word*; *The River and the Vessel*). For Carroll the documents establish the second and are consistent with the third; they do not establish the first. This study says so wherever the route is in question, and it makes no claim about Carroll's intentions beyond what he wrote.

### 2.3 Sources

Carroll's books are quoted from their first editions as transcribed by Project Gutenberg. His diaries are quoted from Roger Lancelyn Green's edition (Carroll 1971); the complete edition by Edward Wakeling (Lewis Carroll Society, 1993–2008) could not be searched. His library is given as Charles Lovett catalogues it (Lovett 2005). His essay "Eternal Punishment" is quoted from *The Lewis Carroll Picture Book* (Carroll 1899). MacDonald is quoted from his own books, Maurice through Gardiner's thesis and Carroll's diary. Swedenborg is quoted from the Swedenborg Foundation Standard Edition: *Heaven and Hell* in Ager's translation, *Arcana Coelestia* (hereafter *AC*) in the Clowes–Potts translation, cited by section number.

## 3. The Record: Carroll and Swedenborg

### 3.1 The Library

Dodgson's books were dispersed after his death in January 1898, chiefly through an auction in Oxford that began on 10 May. Charles Lovett's catalogue of his library rests on that sale catalogue, which "describes 1545 titles in 2711 volumes", supplemented by later sales and surviving copies. Lovett states the limit of the record himself: "there are nearly as many items listed in the Brooks Catalogue as merely 'others' or 'bundle of books'", and "because of the practice of the auctioneer of lotting up groups of undescribed books … we can never know for sure that Dodgson did not own a particular book" (Lovett 2005). The catalogue can therefore show what Dodgson owned. It cannot show what he did not own.

What it shows touching Swedenborg is three books, all by other authors. Emerson's *Representative Men* (London, 1850) has an essay on Swedenborg among its six lives. Asa Mahan's *Modern Mysteries, Explained and Exposed* (Boston, 1855) gives its fourth part to the "Clairvoyant Revelations of Emmanuel Swedenborg"; its title states its purpose, which is to expose. James John Garth Wilkinson's *The Human Body and its Connexion with Man* (London, 1851) is by the physician whom Emerson called "the editor of Swedenborg" (Lovett 2005, nos. 669, 1289, 2267). The catalogue lists no work by Swedenborg himself.

The same catalogue shows where Dodgson's interest in the unseen world actually led him. He owned A. P. Sinnett's Theosophical *Esoteric Buddhism* (1885) and *The Occult World*, and the *Journal* and *Proceedings* of the Society for Psychical Research, of which, Lovett notes, "Dodgson was a charter member" (Lovett 2005, nos. 1852–1853, 1903–1904). These are the sources he himself named when he came to explain the states of consciousness in *Sylvie and Bruno* (§8).

### 3.2 Carroll's Own Words

Carroll does not name Swedenborg anywhere the record could be searched: not in Lancelyn Green's edition of the diaries, not in Collingwood's *Life and Letters* or the *Selected Letters*, not in *The Lewis Carroll Picture Book* with its essay on eternal punishment, and not in the *Alice* books or either volume of *Sylvie and Bruno* (Carroll 1971; Collingwood 1898; Carroll 1982; Carroll 1899). Two large bodies of his writing were out of reach: Wakeling's complete edition of the diaries and Cohen and Green's full edition of the letters. A mention there is possible. None is known.

The record of Carroll and Swedenborg is therefore short. He had Swedenborg on his shelves at second hand, and in one case through a hostile author; nothing he wrote that survives in the searched record shows that he read Swedenborg or thought about him. Everything further that can be said about Carroll and Swedenborg's ideas comes through the two friends whose part in his life and work is documented, and through Carroll's own theology.

## 4. The Circle: MacDonald and Maurice

### 4.1 George MacDonald

Carroll met George MacDonald in 1859 at Ore, near Hastings, in the home of James Hunt (1833–1869), the specialist he consulted for his stammer, and the friendship took in the whole MacDonald family (Carroll 1971, introduction). MacDonald's family was the first audience that *Alice* had beyond the Liddell children. MacDonald's son Greville remembered that Dodgson "asked my mother to read his first Alice book to us [the MS. of Alice's Adventures Underground], just to see how we took it and thus to gauge its worth if published. . . . I remember that first reading well, and also my braggart avowal that I wished there were sixty thousand volumes of it!" (Greville MacDonald 1924, quoted in Carroll 1971, introduction). Collingwood adds that MacDonald, "to whom he had shown the story, persuaded him to submit it to a publisher" (Collingwood 1898). The literary traffic between the two men has been traced in detail by John Docherty, who finds mutual symbolic references throughout their fantasies (Docherty 1995, as summarized in Gardiner 2023, 18).

MacDonald's own relation to Swedenborg is in his own words. In *England's Antiphon* (1868), writing of the mystical poets, he states the correspondence idea as his own conviction:

> They use the largest figures for the largest spiritual ideas—*light* for *good*, *darkness* for *evil*. Such symbols are the true bodies of the true ideas. For this service mainly what we term *nature* was called into being, namely, to furnish forms for truths, for without form truth cannot be uttered. (MacDonald 1868)

He goes on to warn that when logic "leaves the poetry behind" the symbol hardens into "a worship of the symbol", and names Swedenborg as the great instance of both the power and the danger: "Witness some of the writings of the European master of the order—Swedenborg: the highest of them are rich in truth; the lowest are poverty-stricken indeed" (MacDonald 1868). In *Malcolm* (1875) a character, "without having read a word of Swedenborg", is "a believer in the absolute correspondence of the inward and outward"; in *Adela Cathcart* (1864) the narrator has "formed the Swedenborgian resolution of never growing old", which is Swedenborg's teaching that those in heaven "are continually advancing toward the spring of life" (MacDonald 1875; MacDonald 1864; *HH* §414). Literary scholarship reads the same inheritance in MacDonald's fantasies: Richard Reis notes that MacDonald "apparently read widely in the classic literature of mysticism, including Swedenborg and Boehme" (Reis 1972), and Adelheid Kegler reads *Phantastes* and *Lilith* through Swedenborg's Divine Human and Grand Man (Kegler 2005).

The closest friend of Carroll's literary life thus held the doctrine of correspondences, knew where it came from, and wrote fantasies built on it. That is a documented channel by which correspondential thinking was present at the making of *Alice*. It is not a record that Carroll took it up; what he took up shows in his texts and must be read there.

### 4.2 F. D. Maurice

Frederick Denison Maurice, dismissed from King's College London in 1853 after the final essay of his *Theological Essays* challenged the received doctrine of eternal punishment (Gardiner 2023, 68), argued that "eternal" in the New Testament does not mean endless duration. Carroll's diary for February 1863, during the prosecution of Benjamin Jowett for heresy, records that Maurice had written a newspaper letter "in which there was so much that I thought he had misunderstood, that I wrote him a long letter on the subject"; two days later he "Got a long, earnestly written, answer from Maurice", and the day after "Wrote a second long letter to Maurice" (Carroll 1971). He assisted at Maurice's church in Vere Street, "went back to lunch with him and Mrs. Maurice, and after the afternoon service went to the MacDonalds", where they talked of MacDonald's "new novel David Elginbrod" (Carroll 1971). Maurice's side of the 1863 exchange survives, and Gardiner publishes it more fully than ever before (Gardiner 2023, appendix 2).

Gardiner's conclusion is that Maurice's eschatology runs through Carroll's fiction from *Wonderland* to *Sylvie and Bruno*: that "everlasting punishment is an inadequate and immoral axiom for an eternally loving God", and that eternity is "a state which is unrelated to time or space" (Gardiner 2023, 298–299). She quotes Maurice's *Theological Essays*: when we live in a state of eternity we "break loose from the fetters of Time" (Maurice 1853, quoted in Gardiner 2023, 271). The content Gardiner traces from Maurice to Carroll is the content Swedenborg states. Neither Gardiner nor the record traces it from Swedenborg to Maurice, and this study does not claim that it went that way. What is documented is that Carroll received it from Maurice, in letters and at Vere Street, in the same years that he wrote *Wonderland*.

## 5. Carroll's Own Theology: "Eternal Punishment"

Carroll planned a book of essays on religious difficulties, which his nephew Stuart Collingwood calls "the one on which his heart was most set". He finished one essay, on eternal punishment, a subject "on which he felt very deeply", and Collingwood printed it after his death (Carroll 1899, 344–345). It is the fullest statement of his theology in his own words, and it is the one place where his thinking can be set directly beside Swedenborg's without the mediation of a story.

The essay is a piece of logic. Carroll states the difficulty as three propositions that cannot all be true: "I. God is perfectly good. II. To inflict Eternal Punishment on certain human beings, and in certain circumstances, would be wrong. III. God is capable of acting thus." He then divides the possible subjects of eternal punishment into three cases: (A) one who has lost free will and can no longer sin or repent; (B) one who keeps free will and has repented and is "choosing good as good"; and (C) "one who retains Free-Will and continues for ever to choose evil". Of the third case he writes:

> In such a case Eternal Punishment would be infinite suffering, inflicted as punishment for infinite sin. I assume that the reader would not feel any difficulty in recognising the justice of inflicting continuous suffering as punishment for continuous sin. Hence we may set aside case (C) altogether. (Carroll 1899, 347)

The difficulty, then, lies only in cases (A) and (B), where suffering would continue after the sin had stopped. There Carroll finds "the irresistible intuition that infinite punishment for finite sin would be unjust, and therefore wrong", and he holds that to override it is "virtually the abandonment of Conscience as a guide in questions of Right and Wrong" (Carroll 1899, 352). He adds an intuition "of which no account has yet been taken": "that there is some eternal necessity, wholly beyond our comprehension, that sin must result in suffering" (Carroll 1899, 351). And when he comes to the scriptural ground of the doctrine, he reduces it to a single word: the interpretation of the passages "depends largely, if not entirely, on the meaning given to one single word (αἰών). This is rendered, in our English Bibles, by the word 'eternal' or 'everlasting': but there are many critics who believe that it does not necessarily mean 'endless'" (Carroll 1899, 354). The essay closes on four logically possible positions, the last of which holds that "eternal" has been mistranslated, and Carroll says that his "object has been, throughout, not to indicate one course rather than another, but to help the Reader to see clearly what the possible courses are" (Carroll 1899, 355).

Set beside Swedenborg, the essay agrees with him on three points, each in Carroll's own terms. The first is the case Carroll found unproblematic. Swedenborg writes that "no one in the other world suffers punishment on account of the evils that he had done in this world, but only on account of the evils that he then does", because "everyone after death returns into his own life and thus into like evils" (*HH* §509), and that "he who wills and loves evil in the world wills and loves the same evil in the other life" (*HH* §547). Hell, on this account, is case (C): continuous suffering for a continuing choice, and the one who suffers it "is led into hell, not by the Lord but by himself" (*HH* §547). The second is Carroll's "eternal necessity … that sin must result in suffering". Swedenborg states it as a law of the spiritual world: "Every evil carries its punishment with it, the two making one; therefore whoever is in evil is also in the punishment of evil" (*HH* §509), and punishment "is from the evil itself, since evil is so joined with its own punishment that the two cannot be separated" (*HH* §550). The third is the word. Carroll leaves open whether αἰών means endless time; Swedenborg says that the angels have "an idea of eternity different from that which men on the earth have. Eternity means to the angels infinite state, not infinite time" (*HH* §167).

These are agreements of content, and the essay does not name Swedenborg. The question of the word is Maurice's: it is the point on which he lost his post at King's College, and the point Carroll took up with him in the letters of 1863 (§4.2). The structure of the argument is Carroll's own, a logician's treatment of an inconsistent triad. What the essay shows is that Carroll, reasoning from conscience and from the Greek, held the account of judgement that Swedenborg gives: no one is punished for a past that is over; the one who goes on choosing evil is held in it by his own choice; and the eternity of the New Testament need not be a length of time. This is the theology his fiction carries, and it is the ground for reading the fiction as this study does.

**Correspondential key:**

| Carroll ("Eternal Punishment") | Swedenborg | Point of agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Case (C): "continuous suffering as punishment for continuous sin" | "only on account of the evils that he then does" (*HH* §509); "led into hell, not by the Lord but by himself" (*HH* §547) | Punishment lasts as the evil choice lasts |
| "some eternal necessity … that sin must result in suffering" | "Every evil carries its punishment with it, the two making one" (*HH* §509; cf. §550) | Punishment is the evil itself, not an added penalty |

## 6. *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*

*Wonderland* is a world in which outward things answer to inward ones. Alice's size changes with what she takes in; the creatures she meets are each a single disposition with a voice; time itself is a person who can be offended. The readings below take five features of the story in turn. Each gives Carroll's words, then Swedenborg's, then the correspondence. None of them claims that Carroll had Swedenborg in mind.

**The garden.** In the first chapter Alice finds a little door, opens it with a golden key, and looks "along the passage into the loveliest garden you ever saw. How she longed to get out of that dark hall, and wander about among those beds of bright flowers and those cool fountains, but she could not even get her head through the doorway" (Carroll 1865, ch. 1). The rest of the first half of the book is her attempt to reach it: she is too large, then too small, then has left the key on the table. She arrives only in the seventh chapter, after she has learned to govern her size: "Now, I'll manage better this time," she said … and began by taking the little golden key", and "then—she found herself at last in the beautiful garden, among the bright flower-beds and the cool fountains" (Carroll 1865, ch. 7). In Swedenborg a garden is the understanding of truth: "a 'garden' signifies intelligence, or the understanding of truth; and 'Eden,' being significative of love, signifies wisdom" (*AC* §305; cf. §100). Read in correspondence, the garden is understanding seen from outside before it can be entered, and Alice's changes of size are changes in her own state that have to be brought into order before the way in is open. The story says nothing about how this is done except that she does it by trial and by learning from her mistakes, and that the second time she does things in the right order.

**Time.** At the tea party the Hatter explains why it is always six o'clock. He sang at the Queen of Hearts' concert, "when the Queen jumped up and bawled out, 'He's murdering the time! Off with his head!'" "And ever since that," the Hatter went on in a mournful tone, "he won't do a thing I ask! It's always six o'clock now." They therefore "keep moving round … as the things get used up", and when Alice asks what happens "when you come to the beginning again", the March Hare changes the subject (Carroll 1865, ch. 7). Time in Wonderland is a person — "It's *him*" — with whom one can be on good or bad terms, and the Hatter's quarrel with him has fixed the party in one state. Swedenborg's account of time is that it is the outward measure of changes of state: in heaven "instead of years and days there are changes of state; and where there are years and days there are times, but where there are changes of state there are states" (*HH* §163); angels "have no idea of time; but in its place they have an idea of state" (*HH* §164). Where a state does not change, on that account, there is no time to pass, and that is what the tea party is: one state, repeated, going round the table without advancing. Gardiner reads the same scene through Maurice, as the "punishments we see in the mad tea party" for a "transactional relationship with Time" (Gardiner 2023, 271). Both readings stand on the text: time as the measure of a state, and a state that has stopped moving.

**The court and its sentence.** At the trial of the Knave the King asks the jury for a verdict, and the Queen overrules him: "No, no! … Sentence first—verdict afterwards." Alice answers, "Stuff and nonsense! … The idea of having the sentence first!" (Carroll 1865, ch. 12). The Queen's justice throughout the book is the same: "Off with their heads!" for white roses, for a late croquet player, for the Hatter's singing, without reference to what was done. This is the judgement that Carroll's essay rejects — punishment set apart from the act and out of proportion to it, which "according to the voice of my conscience" would be "unjust, and therefore wrong" (Carroll 1899, 345). It is also the opposite of the judgement Swedenborg describes, in which "no one in the other world suffers punishment on account of the evils that he had done in this world, but only on account of the evils that he then does" (*HH* §509). The Queen's sentences, moreover, are never carried out: "The Queen never executes nobody, you know" (Carroll 1865, ch. 9). Her court is the doctrine of arbitrary punishment drawn as it would look if it were true, and it does no harm to anyone.

**The painted roses.** At the entrance to the garden "the roses growing on it were white, but there were three gardeners at it, busily painting them red", because they had planted a white tree by mistake and the Queen would have their heads (Carroll 1865, ch. 8). Swedenborg names red and white as the two fundamental colours: "the color 'red' signifies the good which is of love; and the color 'white' signifies the truth which is of faith", red because it comes from fire and white because it comes from light (*AC* §9467). Read in correspondence, the scene is the colour of love laid on the outside of truth by those who fear punishment, where in a living rose it would come from within. Swedenborg's image for the same thing is the vessel "shining and polished on the outside" with something else within (*HH* §505). Carroll's essay names the motive: one who "checks and puts out of his mind a sinful wish merely from fear of punishment" (Carroll 1899, 351). The gardeners are that motive at work in a garden.

**The pack of cards.** The court of Wonderland is a pack of playing cards, and the gardeners lying on their faces cannot be told from soldiers or courtiers or the royal children, because "the pattern on their backs was the same as the rest of the pack" (Carroll 1865, ch. 8). At the end of the trial Alice, who "had grown to her full size by this time", says "Who cares for you? … You're nothing but a pack of cards!", and the whole pack rises into the air and she wakes (Carroll 1865, ch. 12). The cards are flat: figures with a face and a back and nothing behind them, whose authority depends on being taken for more than they are. Swedenborg describes the state in which a spirit's externals are taken away and what was within appears, so that one who "in externals takes on the semblance of a good man" is seen as he is (*HH* §505). The correspondence at the end of *Wonderland* is the same movement seen from the other side: when Alice has come to her full stature, the power that threatened her is seen to be surface, and it falls away.

**The animals.** Most of those Alice meets are animals, and each is a single quality: the anxious, hurrying Rabbit; the Caterpillar, whose first counsel is "Keep your temper"; the sleeping Dormouse; the grinning Cat that can vanish and leave its grin. Swedenborg's first example of correspondence is that "the animals of the earth correspond in general to affection, mild and useful animals to good affections, fierce and useless ones to evil affections" (*HH* §110). Read in correspondence, Wonderland's animals are affections met in their own forms. The reading can go no further than that general statement: Swedenborg gives no specific correspondence for a white rabbit, a caterpillar or a Cheshire cat, and this study does not supply one.

## 7. *Through the Looking-Glass*

The second book turns the first inside out. Where Wonderland was entered by falling, the looking-glass country is entered by passing through a surface; where Wonderland's time had stopped, the looking-glass country's time runs backwards; and where Alice wandered in Wonderland, here she moves across a chessboard towards a fixed end, the eighth square, where she will be crowned. In this book the correspondence of space and state is drawn so plainly that it reads almost as a demonstration.

**The room behind the glass.** Before she goes through, Alice describes Looking-glass House to the kitten: "there's the room you can see through the glass—that's just the same as our drawing room, only the things go the other way." Once through, she finds that "what could be seen from the old room was quite common and uninteresting, but that all the rest was as different as possible. For instance, the pictures on the wall next the fire seemed to be all alive" (Carroll 1871, ch. 1). Swedenborg says of the spiritual world that "all things in heaven appear, just as in the world, to be in place and in space", although the angels there have "no notion or idea of place and space" (*HH* §191). Read in correspondence, the glass divides one world from another that looks the same from outside, and the difference is in what lies out of sight of the first. Only the part visible from this side is ordinary; everything else is alive.

**Walking away to arrive.** Alice sees the Red Queen in the distance and sets out towards her, but "she lost sight of her in a moment, and found herself walking in at the front-door again". The Rose has already advised her "to walk the other way". So "she thought she would try the plan, this time, of walking in the opposite direction. It succeeded beautifully. She had not been walking a minute before she found herself face to face with the Red Queen, and full in sight of the hill she had been so long aiming at" (Carroll 1871, ch. 2). Swedenborg describes movement in the spiritual world in the same terms. "All changes of place in the spiritual world are effected by changes of state of the interiors, which means that change of place is nothing else than change of state" (*HH* §192), and one on the way somewhere "arrives more quickly when he eagerly desires it, and less quickly when he does not, the way itself being lengthened and shortened in accordance with the desire, although it remains the same" (*HH* §195). In a world where place is state, to aim at a thing directly is to keep the state in which one is apart from it; to turn from it is to change state, and the change brings the arrival. The hill Alice reaches is the height from which the whole chessboard is seen, and in Swedenborg "mountains" are what is "elevated above the earth and nearer as it were to heaven" (*AC* §795). Gardiner reads the same scene: "Only a child would choose to walk in the opposite direction and trust that it will get her where she needs to be, though this is exactly what is needed in the topsy-turvy, spatially and temporally distorted worlds she has entered" (Gardiner 2023, 253).

**Running to stay in place.** The Red Queen then takes Alice by the hand and they run, faster and faster, until Alice is breathless; when they stop she finds that "we've been under this tree the whole time! Everything's just as it was!" The Queen explains: "Now, *here*, you see, it takes all the running *you* can do, to keep in the same place. If you want to get somewhere else, you must run at least twice as fast as that!" (Carroll 1871, ch. 2). On Swedenborg's account no amount of motion changes place unless the state changes, because "change of place is nothing else than change of state" (*HH* §192). The race is the converse of the walk. Effort of the body, without change in the one who makes it, leaves her where she was.

**Living backwards.** The White Queen tells Alice that in the looking-glass country "the rule is, jam to-morrow and jam yesterday—but never jam to-day", and that this is "the effect of living backwards", which has "one great advantage in it, that one's memory works both ways". Her example is a judgement: "there's the King's Messenger. He's in prison now, being punished: and the trial doesn't even begin till next Wednesday: and of course the crime comes last of all." Alice objects that it "wouldn't be all the better his being punished" if he never commits the crime, and when the Queen asks whether she has ever been punished, Alice answers, "Only for faults … Yes, but then I *had* done the things I was punished for … that makes all the difference" (Carroll 1871, ch. 5). Gardiner notes that "since it is not only space, but time that runs backwards in Looking Glass, there are ethical and moral concerns regarding the consequences of crime or suffering" (Gardiner 2023, 171). The scene repeats the Queen of Hearts' "Sentence first—verdict afterwards" and states in a child's voice the principle of Carroll's essay, that punishment is just only in proportion to a sin actually chosen (Carroll 1899, 349–350). It is the principle Swedenborg gives for the other life, where punishment is "only on account of the evils that he then does" (*HH* §509). The Queen's finger shows the same rule at work in the body: she screams, then bleeds, and only then pricks her finger on the brooch, after which "I've done all the screaming already … What would be the good of having it all over again?" (Carroll 1871, ch. 5). Where time runs backwards, suffering comes loose from its cause; Alice's common sense, that one is punished for what one has done, is what the book asks the reader to hold on to.

**The White Knight.** The knight who escorts Alice to the last brook is kind, inventive and unable to stay on his horse: he falls off forwards when the horse stops and backwards when it starts, and carries his box upside down "so that the rain can't get in", until Alice points out that "the things can get *out*" (Carroll 1871, ch. 8). Swedenborg reads the horse of Scripture as the understanding: "a 'horse' signifies the faculty of understanding, and a 'rider' one who is intelligent" (*AC* §2761). Read in correspondence, the White Knight is a good will that cannot keep its seat on its own understanding, whose inventions keep out what it fears and let out what it needs. He is the gentlest figure in the book, and he brings Alice to the brook that she crosses to become a queen.

## 8. *Sylvie and Bruno*

*Sylvie and Bruno* (1889) and *Sylvie and Bruno Concluded* (1893) are Carroll's last fiction and his most openly theological. They move between three worlds — an English country town, the court of Outland, and Elfland — and the narrator passes between them in states that he himself cannot always tell apart. Here, unlike the *Alice* books, Carroll explained his design, and the explanation names its source.

**The states of consciousness and their named source.** The preface to the second volume sets out "the *theory* on which this story is constructed":

> It is an attempt to show what might *possibly* happen, supposing that Fairies really existed; and that they were sometimes visible to us, and we to them; and that they were sometimes able to assume human form: and supposing, also, that human beings might sometimes become conscious of what goes on in the Fairy-world—by actual transference of their immaterial essence, such as we meet with in 'Esoteric Buddhism.' I have supposed a Human being to be capable of various psychical states, with varying degrees of consciousness, as follows:— (*a*) the ordinary state, with no consciousness of the presence of Fairies; (*b*) the 'eerie' state, in which, while conscious of actual surroundings, he is *also* conscious of the presence of Fairies; (*c*) a form of trance, in which, while *un*conscious of actual surroundings, and apparently asleep, he (i.e. his immaterial essence) migrates to other scenes, in the actual world, or in Fairyland, and is conscious of the presence of Fairies. (Carroll 1893, preface)

Carroll's named source is A. P. Sinnett's Theosophical *Esoteric Buddhism*, which he owned, and behind his "psychical states" stands the Society for Psychical Research, of which he was a charter member (Lovett 2005, nos. 1852, 1903–1904). The word "eerie" he takes from the Scots, as the first volume says (Carroll 1889).

Swedenborg describes two states of his own that correspond to Carroll's (*b*) and (*c*). Of the first kind he writes: "Walking through the streets of a city and through fields, talking at the same time with spirits, I knew no otherwise than that I was fully awake, and in possession of my usual sight", and only afterwards found that he had been "led away by the spirit into another place" (*HH* §441). This is conscious presence in the actual surroundings together with consciousness of spirits, which is Carroll's state (*b*). Of the second: "Man is brought into a certain state that is midway between sleeping and waking, and when in that state he seems to himself to be wide awake … This is the state that is called being withdrawn from the body, and not knowing whether one is in the body or out of it" (*HH* §440). This is Carroll's state (*c*), the body "apparently asleep" while the person is elsewhere and conscious. The correspondence of the two schemes is exact in what they describe; the difference is that Swedenborg reports experiences and Carroll states a supposition for a story. It is a convergence, and the record gives its route: Carroll's states came to him from Sinnett and psychical research, and he says so.

**The Outlandish Watch.** The Professor of Outland shows the narrator "an Outlandish Watch … which has the peculiar property that, instead of its going with the time, the time goes with it". Moving its hands back moves the world back; a "Reversal Peg" makes "the events of the next hour happen in the reverse order" (Carroll 1889). The narrator borrows it to undo a street accident in which a young man is hurt. He turns the hands back and prevents the injury, but when the watch again reaches the moment at which he turned it, the cart is back at the door and "the wounded youth was once more reclining on the heap of pillows": "The good I fancied I could do is vanished like a dream" (Carroll 1889). Gardiner reads the watch as Carroll's return to the White Rabbit's watch and the Hatter's, with time now "controlled not by Time, but by those who hold it" (Gardiner 2023, 271–272). In Swedenborg's account the measure of time is the state of the one who lives in it: "where there are changes of state there are states" and not times (*HH* §163). Read in correspondence, the watch makes the point by its failure. Time turned back from outside changes no one's state, and so when the outward clock catches up, everything is as it was.

**Spiritual space.** Gardiner shows that in *Sylvie and Bruno* "the author allows physical space to be manipulated and experienced in a multitude of ways depending on the spiritual state of the individual" (Gardiner 2023, 271). Her example is Bruno's report of a visit to a cross and unwelcoming man: "Hadn't room for any questions … The room were so crowded." "Three people *couldn't* crowd a room," said Sylvie. "They *did*, though," Bruno persisted. "*He* crowded it most" (Carroll 1893). The room is crowded by the man's state, not by his body. Swedenborg's statement that "change of place is nothing else than change of state" (*HH* §192) is the principle; Bruno's room is that principle met in a cottage doorway.

**The locket.** At the end of the second volume Sylvie learns that the two jewels her father offered her, between which she chose, were one. Seen one way it is red and reads "SYLVIE WILL LOVE ALL"; held up to the sky it is blue and reads "ALL WILL LOVE SYLVIE". Her father tells her: "it *was* the same Jewel—but you chose quite right", and Bruno says that "when you look *at* it, it's red and fierce like the sun—and, when you look *through* it, it's gentle and blue like the sky!" (Carroll 1893, ch. 24). Gardiner reads the unity of the two lockets as Maurice's "Trinity which is united in love" (Gardiner 2023, 295). Swedenborg gives the two colours their meanings. Red is the colour of the good of love, "because it comes from fire" (*AC* §9467); blue "belongs to the color of the sky" and signifies "truth from a celestial origin", so that in heaven "the good itself" appears "as crimson, and the derivative truth as blue" (*AC* §9466). Read in correspondence, the jewel is love and the truth that comes from it, one thing seen from two sides. The side Sylvie chose is love going out, "Sylvie will love all"; the other side, "all will love Sylvie", is what follows when the jewel is looked through towards the sky. Her choice is the right one because it is the active side, and Swedenborg defines its opposite exactly: "the love of self is wishing well to oneself alone, and to others only for the sake of self" (*HH* §556). A child who chose to be loved by all would have chosen the same jewel from the wrong side.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 What the Record Supports

The record supports five statements, and no more. First, Carroll owned three books in which Swedenborg appears through other writers — Emerson's essay, Mahan's exposure, Garth Wilkinson's physiology — and no book by Swedenborg is recorded among his possessions; the record of his library is incomplete, so this establishes what he had, not what he lacked (§3.1). Second, he does not name Swedenborg in any of his writings that could be searched (§3.2). Third, his closest literary friend, George MacDonald, held the doctrine of correspondences as his own, named Swedenborg as its great modern teacher, and was the first adult reader of *Alice* outside the Liddell family (§4.1). Fourth, Carroll corresponded with F. D. Maurice on the doctrine of eternity in 1863, took part in services at Maurice's church, and carried Maurice's eschatology into his fiction from *Wonderland* to *Sylvie and Bruno*, as Gardiner has shown from Maurice's own letters to him (§4.2). Fifth, when Carroll named a source for the states of consciousness in *Sylvie and Bruno*, he named Sinnett's *Esoteric Buddhism*, and he was a charter member of the Society for Psychical Research (§§3.1, 8).

What the record does not support is equally definite. No document shows that Carroll read Swedenborg, owned his works, or knew his teaching at first hand. No document shows that he meant his stories to carry Swedenborg's doctrine, or any doctrine in coded form; his only recorded statement about a hidden meaning in his books is the 1896 note that the Hatter's riddle, "as originally invented, had no answer at all" (Carroll 1896). Nor does any document connect him with Garth Wilkinson beyond the one book on his shelves. A claim that Carroll was a Swedenborgian, or that he built his fantasies as an exposition of Swedenborg's system, has nothing in the record to stand on, and the record of his own named sources points elsewhere. That is where this study leaves the question of transmission. The channel that is documented is the circle — MacDonald and Maurice — and the content that circle carried is the content Swedenborg gives.

### 9.2 What the Reading Shows

The reading shows that the worlds Carroll built behave as Swedenborg says the spiritual world behaves, on four points that can be checked against the text. Time in them is the measure of state: it stops when the Hatter's state stops, it runs backwards where the order of cause and effect is reversed, and it cannot be moved from outside without the change coming undone (*HH* §§163–164; §§6, 7, 8). Space in them is the form of state: Alice reaches the Red Queen by turning away, runs without moving, and Bruno's cottage is crowded by one cross man (*HH* §§191–192, 195; §§7, 8). Judgement in them is judged by Swedenborg's rule and Carroll's: the sentences of the Queen of Hearts and the punishment of the King's Messenger are absurd precisely because they separate suffering from a present choice, the very separation Carroll's essay calls unjust and Swedenborg's account of the other life excludes (*HH* §§509, 547; §§5, 6, 7). And the visionary states of *Sylvie and Bruno* are the two states Swedenborg reports of himself (*HH* §§440–441; §8).

Beyond these structural points the reading finds particular correspondences where Swedenborg gives a meaning for the natural thing and the story uses the thing in the way the meaning requires: the garden as understanding (*AC* §§100, 305); red and white as love and truth, in the painted roses (*AC* §9467); red and blue as love and the truth that comes from it, in Sylvie's locket (*AC* §§9466–9467); the horse as understanding, in the White Knight (*AC* §2761); the hill as elevation (*AC* §795). These are fewer than the structural points and rest on single scenes. They are offered as readings, with the Swedenborg locus that makes each one checkable, and the key tables list nothing for which no locus was found. Where Swedenborg gives no specific meaning — for the White Rabbit, the Cheshire Cat, the Caterpillar — the reading stops at his general statement that animals are affections (*HH* §110).

The structural points are the stronger result. They do not depend on single images but on the rules by which Carroll's worlds work, and they are the same rules that Maurice taught and that Gardiner traces through the books on independent ground. When two readers working from different premises — Gardiner from Maurice's letters, this study from Swedenborg's text — find the same account of time, space and judgement in the same scenes, the account is in the scenes.

### 9.3 Implications for the Framework

The library's framework distinguishes the content of correspondence from the routes by which it reaches a writer. *The Transmission of the Ancient Word* treats correspondence as ontological law, "not invented by any human tradition", and allows access to it through an intuitive faculty, through transmission of the codified system, or through direct instruction. *The River and the Vessel* argues that content grounded in reality "does not drift because each generation's lived experience re-confirms the structure". On these premises a writer does not need to have read Swedenborg to write in correspondence, and a correspondential reading of his work does not need a Swedenborgian channel in order to stand. The channel matters only to a claim of transmission.

Carroll is the clearest case in the library of why the two must be kept apart. The content is present, and present exactly: the account of time, space and judgement in his fiction and his essay is Swedenborg's account. The route is documented, and it is not Swedenborg's books. It runs through a Broad Church theologian who argued from the Greek of the New Testament that eternity is not duration, through a Scottish novelist who took the correspondence of inward and outward as the ground of his art, through the psychical research and Theosophy of the 1880s, and through Carroll's own reasoning as a logician about what a good God could justly do. Each of these is a separate route, and they arrive at the same place. On the framework's own terms that convergence is evidence of the kind it values most: independent witnesses reaching one structure, which is what the structure's being real would predict. A Carroll who had copied Swedenborg would be evidence only that Swedenborg could be copied.

The implication for the library is therefore a matter of classification. Carroll belongs with the convergent witnesses, not with the transmitters: where the library reads his stories in correspondence, the readings stand as readings, and where it traces the route, the route runs through MacDonald and Maurice. Every other writer the library reads in correspondence can be asked the same two questions separately: what the text shows, and what the record shows of the route. Carroll's case shows that the content can arrive without a Swedenborgian channel, and the library is stronger for keeping the two kinds of case distinct.

### 9.4 Limitations and Lacunae

The negative findings of the record are limited by what could be searched. Carroll's name for Swedenborg was looked for in Lancelyn Green's two-volume edition of the diaries, in Collingwood's *Life and Letters*, in the *Selected Letters*, in *The Lewis Carroll Picture Book*, and in the four books of fiction. Edward Wakeling's complete ten-volume edition of the diaries (1993–2008) and Morton Cohen and Roger Lancelyn Green's two-volume *Letters* (1979) could not be searched; a mention of Swedenborg in either is possible and would change §3.2. Lovett's catalogue is itself, by his own account, a partial record of a library sold largely in undescribed lots (§3.1). A University of Reading special-collections document cited elsewhere in the library for Carroll's books could not be retrieved and has not been read; this study does not rely on it in either direction.

Several studies were known but not read. John Docherty's *The Literary Products of the Lewis Carroll–George MacDonald Friendship* (1995) is cited here only as Gardiner summarizes it. Adelheid Kegler's work on MacDonald and Swedenborg is cited from her essay in English translation (Kegler 2005); her longer German study of *Lilith* (2002) was not read. Gardiner's thesis is the principal secondary source for Maurice, and Maurice's own *Theological Essays* and letters are quoted through her; where she quotes them, this study has not checked her transcription against the originals.

The readings have their own limits. Swedenborg gives fixed meanings for some natural things and none for others, and the readings use only those he gives; a richer reading could be built, but it would rest on this study's inference rather than his text. Some of the particular correspondences rest on single scenes, and other readings of the same scenes are available, Gardiner's through Maurice among them; the readings here do not exclude hers, and in the structural points they agree with it. Finally, nothing in this study bears on what Carroll intended beyond what he wrote. He said that the Hatter's riddle had no answer when he invented it, and that *Sylvie and Bruno* was built on a supposition about fairies and psychical states. The readings are readings of the texts he left, and the convergence is a fact about those texts, not about his mind.

## 10. Conclusion: The Looking-Glass and the Window

A looking-glass shows what is on this side of it. A window shows what is on the other side. This study has tried to keep the two apart: to say what Carroll's texts show when they are read in correspondence, and separately what the record shows about how their content reached him, without letting the one pass for the other.

What the texts show is an account of time, space and judgement that is Swedenborg's. Wonderland's time stops with the Hatter's state, and Swedenborg says that time in the spiritual world is change of state (*HH* §§163–164). The looking-glass country is crossed by turning away and not crossed by running, and Swedenborg says that "change of place is nothing else than change of state" and that the way is "lengthened and shortened in accordance with the desire" (*HH* §§192, 195). The courts of both books punish before the act and are absurd for it, and Carroll's own essay calls such punishment unjust while accepting "continuous suffering as punishment for continuous sin" (Carroll 1899, 347), which is Swedenborg's account of hell, where no one is punished except "on account of the evils that he then does" (*HH* §509). The states of consciousness in *Sylvie and Bruno* are the two Swedenborg reports of himself (*HH* §§440–441), and Sylvie's locket, red on one side and blue on the other and one jewel, is love and the truth that comes from it in Swedenborg's own colours (*AC* §§9466–9467).

What the record shows is that none of this came to Carroll, so far as can be known, from Swedenborg's books. He owned none that are recorded, named Swedenborg nowhere that could be searched, and named other sources — Sinnett and the psychical researchers — for the one part of his design he explained. The content came through his friends: George MacDonald, who held the correspondence of inward and outward as the ground of his own fantasies, and F. D. Maurice, whose teaching that eternity is a state and not a length of time Carroll argued over with him in 1863 and carried through all his fiction. It came also through Carroll's own reasoning, careful and his own, about what a good God could justly do.

It would be easy to make Carroll a systematic Swedenborgian who encoded a doctrine he had read. The record does not allow that claim, and this study does not make it. What the record and the reading together allow is a stronger claim. A logician and clergyman, working from his friends' theology and his own conscience, built worlds whose rules are the rules Swedenborg gives for the world of spirits. On the framework's premise that correspondence is the structure of reality and not a tradition one must inherit, this is what one would expect to find. Carroll did not transmit the doctrine. He confirmed it, and he did so without having it from its source. He held up the glass, and it showed what was on the other side.

The looking-glass shows, the window opens, and the same world is on the other side of both.
