# **The Conflagration of Persepolis and the Echoes of the Avesta: A Historiographical and Archaeological Inquiry**

## **1\. Introduction: The Intersection of Myth, Memory, and Materiality**

The destruction of Persepolis in the spring of 330 BCE by the forces of Alexander III of Macedon constitutes one of the most defining ruptures in the history of the ancient Near East. It was an event of singular violence that signaled the irrevocable collapse of the Achaemenid Empire, the first "world empire" that had spanned from the Indus to the Aegean. Yet, beyond the immediate geopolitical transfer of power from the House of Achaemenes to the Macedonian conqueror, the sack of Persepolis has engendered a profound and enduring historiographical controversy regarding the preservation of knowledge.  
Central to this controversy is the persistence of a Zoroastrian hagiographic tradition which asserts that Alexander—vilified in Middle Persian texts as *Guzastag*, or "The Accursed"—did not merely burn a palace, but deliberately destroyed the sacred scriptures of the Zoroastrian faith, the *Avesta*. According to these accounts, the whole Avesta and Zand, "written upon prepared cow-skins, and with gold ink," lay in the archives at Stakhr and was burned by Alexander (AWN 1.7–8); later Arabic and Persian tellings put the hides at twelve thousand (al-Ṭabarī 1987, 72). This narrative posits Alexander not merely as a military adversary, but as a theological cataclysm, an agent of the Evil Spirit (*Angra Mainyu*) sent to obliterate the divine word of Ahura Mazda (AWN 1.3–4).  
However, this tradition stands in stark tension with the silence of contemporary Hellenistic historiography and the findings of modern philology. Greek and Roman historians, while detailing the looting of the treasury and the drunken revelry that allegedly precipitated the fire, make no mention of a sacred library. Furthermore, the prevailing consensus among Iranologists and philologists suggests that the *Avesta* was, during the Achaemenid period, a fundamentally oral corpus, transmitted through the mnemonic rigors of the Magian priesthood rather than through written codices (Boyce 1979, 49; Kellens 1987).  
This report seeks to adjudicate this dispute through a rigorous synthesis of Classical historiography, Sasanian religious literature, and the archaeological data derived from the ruins of Persepolis itself. By triangulating the literary silence of the West, the lamentations of the East, and the physical evidence of clay and ash, we aim to construct a neutral historiographical assessment of what was truly lost in 330 BCE. The inquiry is guided by the need to distinguish between the legendary expansions of a traumatized community and the historical realities of ancient record-keeping.  
To understand the fate of the Achaemenid archives, one must navigate three distinct epistemological landscapes: the political narratives of the Greek conquerors, who sought to frame the destruction as either policy or accident; the theological narratives of the Zoroastrian priesthood, who sought to explain the fragmentation of their canon; and the material reality of the Persepolis Fortification Archive, which survived because its medium was clay, buried in the fortification wall, while what was written on hide perished.

## **2\. The Classical Historiography of the Sack: Vengeance, Policy, and Silence**

The primary contemporary or near-contemporary accounts of Alexander’s campaigns derive from Greek and Roman authors writing centuries after the events, yet basing their narratives on the lost journals of Alexander’s companions (Ptolemy, Aristobulus, Nearchus, and Callisthenes). These sources—Arrian, Plutarch, Diodorus Siculus, and Quintus Curtius Rufus—provide the foundational Western understanding of the events at Persepolis. Their accounts are rich in military and political detail but notably deficient in references to Iranian religious literature.

### **2.1 The Narrative of Retribution**

The dominant theme in the Classical sources regarding the destruction of Persepolis is retribution. The war against Persia was ideologically framed by the League of Corinth as a pan-Hellenic crusade to avenge the invasion of Greece by Xerxes I in 480 BCE, particularly the burning of the Acropolis and the destruction of the Temple of Athena.  
**Arrian’s Deliberate Vengeance:** Flavius Arrianus (Arrian), writing in the 2nd century CE but relying heavily on the eyewitness accounts of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, presents the destruction of the palace as a deliberate act. In his *Anabasis of Alexander*, Arrian reports that Alexander burned the Persian palace contrary to the advice of Parmenion, who argued that it was unwise to destroy what was now Alexander's own property and that the men of Asia would conclude that he intended merely to conquer and depart rather than to rule. Alexander answered that he wished to take vengeance on the Persians for the invasion of Greece, "when they razed Athens to the ground and burnt down the temples"; Arrian's own judgment is that Alexander "did not act sensibly in doing this" (Arrian, *Anab.* 3.18.11–12).  
For Arrian, the fire was a policy of retribution, stated as such and carried out against counsel. Crucially, Arrian’s account is devoid of the romantic or chaotic elements found in other sources. There is no mention of a drunken party, no mention of Thaïs, and absolutely no mention of a library or religious archives. The silence of Arrian is significant because his sources were military men (Ptolemy later became King of Egypt) who would have been concerned with the inventory of the palace. Arrian records that Alexander reached Persepolis before the guards could plunder the treasury, and that he also took the money at Pasargadae (3.18.10), but he notes no books.

### **2.2 The Narrative of Drunken Revelry: Thaïs and the Torches**

In contrast to Arrian’s sober assessment, the "Vulgate" tradition—represented by Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch, and Quintus Curtius Rufus—ascribes the conflagration to a spontaneous, alcohol-fueled impulse instigated by an Athenian courtesan.  
**Diodorus Siculus (1st Century BCE):** Diodorus describes a scene of debauchery following the seizure of the city’s immense wealth. Alexander, he writes, "described it to the Macedonians as their worst enemy among the cities of Asia," gave the city to his soldiers to plunder, and "the great royal palace, famed throughout the inhabited world, had been condemned to the indignity of total destruction" (Diod. Sic. 17.70); the treasury, reckoning gold in terms of silver, held one hundred and twenty thousand talents (17.71.1). At a victory banquet Thaïs, an Athenian and the mistress of Ptolemy, declared "that it would be Alexander's greatest achievement in Asia to join in their procession and set fire to the royal palace, allowing women's hands to destroy in an instant what had been the pride of the Persians" (17.72.2). Diodorus explicitly links this to the destruction of Athens. The narrative describes a chaotic procession of drunken revelers, with Alexander throwing the first torch, followed by Thaïs, until "the whole area of the royal palace was quickly engulfed in flames" (17.72.6).  
**Plutarch (1st–2nd Century CE):** Plutarch’s *Life of Alexander* echoes Diodorus, while noting that "others say it was premeditated," and adds that "it is agreed that Alexander speedily repented and gave orders to put out the fire" (Plut. *Alex.* 38.8). Plutarch emphasizes the immense loot taken from the city: "as much coined money was found there as at Susa," and "it took ten thousand pairs of mules and five thousand camels to carry away the other furniture and wealth there" (37.3–4).  
**Quintus Curtius Rufus (1st Century CE):** Curtius Rufus provides the most vivid description of the physical destruction. He notes that the palace’s extensive use of cedar wood made it a tinderbox. He writes:  
"Large sections of the palace had been made of cedar, so they quickly took flame and spread the conflagration over a large area... Such was the end of the palace that had ruled all the East" (Curt. 5.7.5, 8).

### **2.3 The Historiographical Anomaly: The Missing Library**

A critical analysis of these texts reveals a glaring omission relevant to the Zoroastrian claim. The Greeks were not unfamiliar with the concept of libraries or archives. They knew of the libraries of Egypt and Mesopotamia. If Alexander had encountered a massive repository of hides written in gold ink—an object of immense material value and curiosity—it is highly probable that it would have been recorded, if only as an item of plunder.

* **Valuation of Plunder:** The Classical sources are meticulous in their accounting of the plunder: gold, silver, dresses of purple and gold embroidery, furniture, and the beasts of burden needed to carry it are all listed (Diod. Sic. 17.70.3, 17.71; Plut. *Alex.* 37.3–4). The absence of "books" or "scrolls" in these inventories suggests that either they were not present in a form recognizable to the Greeks as a valuable library, or they were considered administrative detritus unworthy of specific mention.  
* **The "Barbarian" Religion:** While Herodotus had earlier described the customs of the Persians (noting they had no statues, temples, or altars; Hdt. 1.131), the Alexander historians show little interest in Persian theology or liturgy. Their focus is on the *political* entity of the Persian Empire. The destruction is framed as the end of a *regime*, not a *religion*.

The consensus of the "Western" historiography is clear: The destruction was physical and political. There is no record in the Greco-Roman tradition of a deliberate campaign to erase Zoroastrian scriptures during the sack of Persepolis.

## **3\. The Zoroastrian Hagiographic Tradition: The Myth of the "Two Copies"**

When we cross the cultural divide to the Iranian sources, the narrative shifts dramatically. For the Zoroastrians, the burning of Persepolis was not merely the end of a dynasty but the catastrophic rupture of their spiritual heritage. The sources for this tradition are primarily Middle Persian (Pahlavi) texts composed or redacted in the Sasanian (224–651 CE) and early Islamic periods (9th–10th centuries CE). These texts, written by the priesthood, view history through a theological lens.

### **3.1 The Narrative of the *Denkard* and *Arda Viraf Namag***

The core of the Zoroastrian claim is preserved in the *Denkard* (Acts of the Religion) and the *Arda Viraf Namag* (Book of the Righteous Viraf). These texts present a coherent, albeit legendary, history of the transmission of the *Avesta*.  
**The *Denkard* Account:** The *Denkard*, an encyclopedic compendium of Zoroastrian knowledge, asserts that the Achaemenid King Darius (identified as *Dara son of Dara* in the conflated Kayanian/Achaemenid chronology, in which he is Alexander's immediate predecessor; West 1892, 413 n. 3) ordered the preservation of the entire religious corpus.  
"Dârâî, son of Dârâî, ordered the preservation of two written copies of the whole Avesta and Zand, according to the receiving of it by Zaratûst from Aûharmazd; one in the treasury of Shapîgân, and one in the fortress of written documents" (Dk 4.23; West 1892, 413).  
This passage is critical because it explicitly claims the existence of a *written* archetype (*nipišt*) during the Achaemenid era. The text continues to describe the fate of these copies: the Arsacid king Valakhsh (probably Vologeses I; West 1892, 413 n. 5) ordered the careful preservation of "the Avesta and Zand as it had purely come unto them, and also of whatever instruction, due to it, had remained written about, as well as deliverable by the tongue through a high-priest, in a scattered state in the country of Iran, owing to the ravages and devastation of Alexander and the cavalry and infantry of the Arumans" (Dk 4.24; West 1892, 413–414). A further chapter of the *Denkard* adds that "that which was in the fortress of documents came to be burnt, and that in the treasury of Shapigan into the hands of the Arumans, and was translated by him even into the Greek language" (Dk 3; West 1892, xxxi).  
**The *Arda Viraf Namag* Account:** This text, which frames a visionary journey to the afterlife, opens with a historical prologue explaining why the journey was necessary: the religion was in doubt because of the destruction wrought by Alexander.  
"[The evil spirit] instigated the accursed Alexander, the Roman, who was dwelling in Egypt, so that he came to the country of Iran with severe cruelty and war and devastation ... And this religion, namely, all the Avesta and Zand, written upon prepared cow-skins, and with gold ink, was deposited in the archives, in Stakhar Papakan, and the hostility of the evil-destined, wicked Ashemok, the evil-doer, brought onward Alexander, the Roman, who was dwelling in Egypt, and he burned them up" (AWN 1.3–8).  
Here, the text adds the specific detail of the medium: "prepared cow-skins, and with gold ink." This description evokes a document of immense value and durability, fitting for a royal archive.

### **3.2 The Demonization of Alexander (*Guzastag*)**

In these texts, Alexander is stripped of his "Greatness" and given the epithet *Guzastag* ("The Accursed"), an epithet he shares with Ahriman alone (Boyce 1979, 78). He is portrayed not just as a conqueror but as an instrument of *Ahriman* (the Evil Spirit). The *Greater Bundahishn* elaborates that Alexander "destroyed all the families of rulers, magi, and public men of Iranshahr, extinguished an immense number of sacred fires, seized the commentary {zand} of the Revelation of Mazda-worship, and sent it to Arum, burned the Avesta" (GBd 33.14), and the *Arda Viraf Namag* that "he killed several Dasturs and judges and Herbads and Mobads and upholders of the religion, and the competent and wise of the country of Iran" (AWN 1.9).  
This demonization serves a theological purpose. In Zoroastrian dualism, history is a battle between Order (*Asha*) and Chaos (*Drug*). The age before Alexander is remembered as one in which "the religion was in purity, and men were without doubts" (AWN 1.2), and Alexander represents the intrusion of Chaos that shattered this perfection.

### **3.3 The "Fortress of Archives" (*Diz-i Nipisht*)**

The location of the *Diz-i Nipisht* has been a subject of intense speculation. The *Arda Viraf Namag* places the archives "in Stakhar Papakan" (AWN 1.7), and al-Ṭabarī has Bishtasb send the scripture "to a place called Diz Nibisht in Istakhr" (al-Ṭabarī 1987, 72). Istakhr was a major city near Persepolis that served as the religious center of the Sasanian dynasty: Sāsān, grandfather of Ardashir, "was the custodian of the fire temple of Istakhr called that of Anāhīdh" (al-Ṭabarī 1999, 4).

* **Historical Geography:** The term means "the fortress of the writings"; its translator notes an attempt, "not entirely convincing," to distinguish two traditions, one connected with Istakhr and the other with Shiz in Media (al-Ṭabarī 1987, 72 n. 191). No building on the Persepolis terrace or at Naqsh-e Rustam has been shown to be the fortress the texts name.  
* **Sasanian Projection:** It is crucial to note that the term *Diz-i Nipisht* appears in Sasanian texts. It is likely that the Sasanians, looking at the impressive ruins of Persepolis and Naqsh-e Rustam, projected their own archival practices backward onto the Achaemenids. They assumed that a great empire *must* have had a great central library for its holy books.

### **3.4 The "Twelve Thousand Hides": Myth or Memory?**

The recurring detail of "twelve thousand hides" is particularly contested.

* **The Number:** The Pahlavi books give no number: the *Arda Viraf Namag* has cow-skins and gold ink (AWN 1.7), the *Denkard* two written copies (Dk 4.23). The number belongs to the later Arabic and Persian tellings. Al-Ṭabarī writes that the scripture Zoroaster brought to Bishtasb "was written on twelve thousand cow skins, that is, carved on skins and painted in gold" (al-Ṭabarī 1987, 72); in the Letter of Tansar, preserved in a thirteenth-century Persian version, Boyce reads "Alexander burnt the book of our religion - 1200 ox-hides - at Istaxr," adding that "one third of it was known by heart and survived" (Boyce 1968, 37). Boyce judged that Tansar "plainly exaggerates grossly" the scale of the loss, to magnify Ardashir's task of restoration (Boyce 1968, 37 n. 2).  
* **The Medium:** However, the mention of hides (parchment/leather) is significant because it correctly identifies the medium used for Achaemenid royal records (Aramaic on leather), as opposed to the clay tablets used for regional administration or the oral transmission used for liturgy. This suggests the legend contains a "kernel of truth"—a memory of a large leather archive that *did* exist and *was* burned (discussed in Section 4).

## **4\. Archaeological Evidence: The Fire and the Fortification Archive**

To ground the literary debate in material reality, we must turn to the excavations of Persepolis. Conducted primarily by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago under Ernst Herzfeld (1931–1934) and later Erich Schmidt (1934–1939), these excavations provided definitive evidence regarding the nature of the fire and the archives.

### **4.1 The Discovery of the Persepolis Fortification Archive (PFA)**

In 1933, Herzfeld discovered a massive cache of clay tablets in two rooms of the fortification wall at the northeast corner of the Persepolis terrace. This find, known as the **Persepolis Fortification Archive (PFA)**, survives as "about 20,000-25,000 tablets and fragments representing about 15,000-18,000 original documents" (Jones and Stolper 2008, quoted in Garrison 2017, 30), dating from the 13th to the 28th year of Darius I (509–493 BCE). A second, smaller archive, the **Persepolis Treasury Archive (PTA)**, was found in the Treasury building.  
**How the Tablets Survived:** The tablets are unbaked, sun-dried clay, and Alexander's fire did not fire them: they "were not found in burned layers and thus were not baked" (Garrison 2017, 106 n. 150, after Henkelman 2008, 72). They survived buried within the fortification. In the Treasury, by contrast, the Elamite documents lay within a destruction horizon, scattered beyond their room by the looting of Alexander's troops (Garrison 2017, 71, after Schmidt 1957, 5–7).

### **4.2 The "Lost" Archive: Evidence of Parchment**

While the clay tablets survived, they provide undeniable evidence that they were merely one part of a larger bureaucratic system that included documents on perishable materials (leather or parchment).

1. **Textual References:** The Elamite texts themselves refer to "Babylonian scribes (writing) on parchment" (PF 1947: 23, 25, 29), and record hides of sheep, goats, cattle and camels delivered to the treasury, "perhaps for use as parchment" (Hallock 1969, 14).  
2. **The Sealings:** The Treasury yielded some 199 uninscribed but sealed clay labels. Many are bullae, lumps of clay affixed to cords bundling objects—"in some cases perhaps folded papyrus or parchment documents"—and many preserve traces of the cords on their reverses (Garrison 2017, 75). One seal in the Fortification archive, that of the prince Aršāma, is the same seal impressed on the bullae of his Aramaic letters on parchment, now in Oxford (Garrison 2017, 100).  
   * **Implication:** These sealings are the "ghosts" of the lost archive. The parchment itself is gone; what remains is the clay that closed it and the records that speak of it.  
3. **Destruction Horizon:** In the Treasury the documents lay in a destruction horizon reaching some 75 cm above the floor (Garrison 2017, 71). Whatever stood there on hide or papyrus did not survive it.

### **4.3 Reconciling Archaeology with the "Ox-Hide" Legend**

The archaeological evidence creates a compelling convergence with the legend of the gold-inked hides.

* **Material Convergence:** The Zoroastrian tradition claims the texts were on hides. The tablets confirm that the Achaemenid administration employed scribes writing on parchment, and the Aršāma letters show high-level records written in Aramaic on skins.  
* **Location Convergence:** The tradition places the texts in the "Fortress of Archives" and the "Treasury." Archaeology found the clay tablets (and the evidence of lost parchments) in the *Fortification* walls and the *Treasury* building.

**The Crucial Distinction:** However, the content of the surviving tablets is exclusively **administrative**. They record rations of wine, grain, and sheep; travel authorizations for satraps; and payments to workers. There is **no archaeological evidence** that these archives contained religious literature, hymns, or the *Avesta*. The "lost" parchment archive was almost certainly the Aramaic translation of these administrative records, or royal chronicles and diplomatic correspondence, rather than a theological library. The Sasanian priests likely conflated the memory of the *burnt royal archives* with the loss of their *religious tradition*.

## **5\. The Philological Debate: Orality vs. Literacy and the Existence of the Avesta**

Having established that an archive was burned, we must determine if the *Avesta* was inside it. On this the leading Iranists largely agree, and Mary Boyce, who held the orality position as firmly as anyone, drew from it the sharpest account of what Alexander did destroy (§ 5.2).

### **5.1 The Consensus: The Paradigm of Orality**

The majority of scholars (including Mary Boyce, Jean Kellens, Prods Oktor Skjærvø, and Karl Hoffmann) argue that the *Avesta* was strictly an oral corpus during the Achaemenid period and was not committed to writing until the Sasanian era (4th–6th centuries CE). Boyce: "the Avesta continued in oral transmission throughout the Achaemenian period, and indeed long afterwards" (1979, 49); the known Avestan vulgate "originates from a canon which was arranged and written down under the Sasanians" (Kellens 1987).  
**Arguments for Orality:**

1. **Absence of Script:** The Achaemenids used Cuneiform (for monumental inscriptions) and the Aramaic alphabet (for administration). Neither script is capable of capturing the rich phonetic complexity of the Avestan language (e.g., its diverse range of vowels and sibilants). The "Avestan script" found in surviving manuscripts was invented in the Sasanian period specifically for the purpose of recording the oral liturgy (Boyce 1979, 134–135).  
2. **Religious Orthopraxy:** In the Indo-Iranian religious tradition (shared with Vedic India), the *manthra* (holy word) is efficacious only when spoken aloud with precise intonation. The Medes and Persians "plainly regarded the alien art with suspicion (in the Persian epic its discovery is attributed to the devil)," and the priests "rejected it as unfit for recording holy words" (Boyce 1979, 49). The priests, in Boyce's phrase, "were the living books of the faith" (1979, 79).  
3. **Mnemonic Structure:** The Avestan texts, particularly the *Gathas* and *Yashts*, display the structural hallmarks of oral composition: metrical patterns, formulaic repetitions, and ring composition, all designed to aid memorization.

**Conclusion of the Consensus:** Alexander could not have burned the *Avesta* because it did not exist as a physical object. The gold-inked hides are a myth projected backward by a literate Sasanian society that could not conceive of a purely oral empire.

### **5.2 Mary Boyce and the "Living Books"**

Mary Boyce, a titan of Zoroastrian studies, held the orality position and drew from it an account of a very real destruction. Alexander's invasion, she wrote, "was made in pursuit of gain and glory, and had no religious impulse," and the community "evidently suffered more during the actual fighting than in the period of foreign domination which followed" (1979, 78).  
**The Slaughter of the Magi:** Boyce argued that what Alexander destroyed was carried by the **Magi** themselves. The tradition remembers him for exactly this: in a Sogdian fragment his wickedness is that he "killed magi" (Boyce 1979, 78), and the *Arda Viraf Namag* says:  
"And he killed several Dasturs and judges and Herbads and Mobads and upholders of the religion, and the competent and wise of the country of Iran" (AWN 1.9).  
"The Zoroastrians sustained irreparable loss through the death of so many of their priests. In those days, when all religious works were handed down orally, the priests were the living books of the faith, and with mass slaughters many ancient works (the tradition holds) were lost, or only haltingly preserved" (Boyce 1979, 78–79). When Alexander's forces sacked the cities and temples, the priests who guarded these oral texts died with them. If all the priests who knew a specific Nask were killed, that "book" was effectively "burned" and lost forever. Thus, the destruction was real, but the medium was flesh, not parchment.  
**The Possibility of a Written Aid:** Boyce's own account leaves no room for an Achaemenid written Avesta (1979, 49). The tradition nevertheless insists on one, on hides and in gold ink, and such a copy cannot be excluded: a prestige copy kept in the Royal Treasury, written in Aramaic script (however imperfectly), as a symbol of royal piety. If such a singular object existed, its burning would have been a potent symbol of the religion's degradation.

### **5.3 The Hermippus Problem: "2 Million Verses"**

A frequent counter-argument against the "Orality" theory cites Pliny the Elder (1st Century CE), who mentions Hermippus of Smyrna (3rd Century BCE). Pliny writes:  
"Hermippus, again, an author who has written with the greatest exactness on all particulars connected with this art, and has commented upon the two millions of verses left by Zoroaster, besides completing indexes to his several works..." (Pliny, *NH* 30.2).  
Does this prove a massive written Zoroastrian canon existed in the Hellenistic period?

* **Analysis:** Most scholars identify these "two million verses" not as the liturgical *Avesta*, but as **Zoroastrian Pseudepigrapha**. In the Hellenistic world, "Zoroaster" became a magnet for works on magic, astrology, botany, and alchemy. Greek authors wrote treatises on these subjects and attributed them to the Persian sage to give them ancient authority.  
* **Conclusion:** Hermippus's commentary proves that the *name* of Zoroaster was associated with books, but it does not prove the existence of the genuine Avestan canon in written form. In fact, the bizarre content of these pseudepigrapha (often contradicting Zoroastrian dualism) suggests they were forgeries.

## **6\. Alternative Explanations: How Was the Text Actually Lost?**

If Alexander did not burn a comprehensive library of Avestan texts, how do we explain the fragmentary state of the surviving corpus (which is estimated to be only a quarter of the original Sasanian canon, since only about a fourth of the Avestan quotations in the Pahlavi commentary can be found in the surviving texts; Kellens 1987)?

### **6.1 The Disruption of State Patronage**

The most immediate effect of Alexander’s conquest was the decapitation of the state support system. The Zoroastrian priesthood relied on royal patronage for their livelihood and for the maintenance of the fire temples. The Achaemenid kings provided vast resources for sacrifices and rituals.

* **Impact:** When the Macedonians took over, this funding dried up. Persis was no longer pre-eminent, and the priests of each province had to rally on their own from "the carnage and destruction of the conquest" (Boyce 1979, 79). Without the leisure and resources to train new generations of priests in the rigorous memorization of the *entire* canon, the more obscure or "scientific" portions of the oral tradition (law, medicine, geography) were gradually forgotten, leaving only the essential liturgy (*Yasna*): the Gathas "were transmitted intact, being known by heart, with the rest of the Staota Yesnya, by every working priest" (Boyce 1979, 79).

### **6.2 The Neglect of the Parthians**

The Parthian (Arsacid) era (247 BCE – 224 CE) is often viewed by Sasanian sources as a period of religious decay. While the Parthians were Zoroastrians, they were also highly Hellenized. The *Denkard* claims that an Arsacid king (likely Vologeses/Valakhsh) attempted to collect what had remained "written about, as well as deliverable by the tongue," in a scattered state (Dk 4.24), suggesting that by the 1st century CE, the canon was already in disarray.

### **6.3 The Islamic Conquest and the "Real" Destruction**

It is essential to recognize that the surviving Avestan manuscripts all derive from a single Sasanian archetype (Kellens 1987), and that every surviving copy was made long *after* the Islamic conquest.

* **The Second Loss:** The Sasanians *did* write down the Avesta in the 4th–6th centuries. This written Sasanian Avesta was massive (21 Nasks). The vast majority of *this* written canon was destroyed or lost during the Islamic conquest of Persia (7th century) and the subsequent centuries of marginalization. The library of the Sasanians was indeed dispersed, and the texts that survive today are almost entirely the liturgical manuals in constant daily use by the priests of Iran and of the Parsis in India (Boyce 1979, 165).

**Historiographical Insight:** The Zoroastrian tradition likely *telescoped* these two traumas. The narrative of Alexander's burning is older than the Arab conquest—the Letter of Tansar, set in Ardashir's reign, already has it (Boyce 1968, 37)—but the Pahlavi books that tell it most fully were redacted in the 9th and 10th centuries, after the Arabs had destroyed the Sasanian state and its written archives. The later loss was read back into the first "Alien Conqueror," creating a unified narrative of persecution.

## **7\. Dating the Avesta: What Existed in 330 BCE?**

To understand the magnitude of the loss, we must delineate the stratification of the Avestan texts. What parts of the canon definitely existed (in oral form) when Alexander stood before Persepolis?

### **7.1 The Old Avesta (Second Millennium BCE)**

These are the most archaic texts, composed in a dialect close to Rigvedic Sanskrit; Boyce placed Zoroaster himself "some time between 1700 and 1500 B.C." (1979, 18). They were undoubtedly fixed and memorized centuries before Alexander.

* **The Gathas:** The 17 hymns attributed to Zoroaster himself (Boyce 1979, 17).  
* **Yasna Haptanghaiti:** The "Liturgy of the Seven Chapters."  
* **Status in 330 BCE:** These were the core of the liturgy. It is virtually certain that the Magi at Persepolis recited these texts exactly as they are preserved today.

### **7.2 The Young Avesta (c. 900–400 BCE)**

These texts show a later stage of the language and often contain grammatical errors suggesting they were composed when Avestan was no longer a living mother tongue.

* **The Yashts:** Hymns to deities (Mithra, Anahita, Tishtrya). These contain pre-Zoroastrian mythological elements re-integrated into the faith.  
* **The Videvdad:** The "Law Against Demons," a code of purity and ritual law.  
* **Status in 330 BCE:** These texts were likely fully formed or "crystallized" by the Achaemenid period. The *Yashts* in particular reflect the imperial theology of the Achaemenids (e.g., the elevation of Mithra and Anahita under Artaxerxes II).

**Conclusion on Dating:** The "Great Avesta" (the 21 Nasks) likely existed as a stable oral corpus in 330 BCE. Alexander’s conquest did not destroy the *Gathas* or the core *Yashts* (which survive), but it likely led to the loss of the "encyclopedic" commentaries and legal texts that were not reinforced by daily ritual use.

## **8\. Historiographical Synthesis: The Function of the Myth**

Why did the Sasanians construct the specific narrative of the "Burned Book"? It belongs to the "Sasanian Restoration" ideology: Albert de Jong observes "the general tendency of Sasanian sources ... of presenting the rise of the Sasanians in terms of the establishment of order out of chaos," with Ardashir I as the king who "restored" a centralized kingdom with a centralized church (de Jong 2004, 346).

1. **Legitimacy:** The Sasanians needed to present themselves as the restorers of a broken heritage to legitimize their overthrow of the Parthians. By claiming Alexander destroyed the text, they explained why the Parthians didn't have it, and why the Sasanians had to "re-assemble" it. The Letter of Tansar draws exactly this conclusion from the burning at Istakhr: "Therefore the faith must needs be restored by a man of true and upright judgment" (Boyce 1968, 37), and the *Denkard* has Ardashir bring "the same scripture ... from a scattered state to one place" (Dk 3; West 1892, xxxi).  
2. **Competition with "Peoples of the Book":** In the 3rd and 4th centuries, Zoroastrianism faced competition from Christianity and Manichaeism—religions centered on written scriptures. Boyce judged it likely that "proselytizing, first by Christians and then by the Manichaeans (who laid immense stress on the value of the written word for preserving truth) spurred the Zoroastrian priests to serious efforts to commit their own holy texts to writing" (1979, 113). By claiming that the *Avesta* was *originally* a book (on hides) that was lost, they could justify writing it down without appearing to innovate.

## **9\. Conclusion**

The burning of Persepolis in 330 BCE was a historical fact, confirmed by the charred ruins and the destruction horizon of the Treasury. However, the specific claim that Alexander the Great destroyed the *Avesta* is a complex amalgam of history, memory, and myth.  
**Key Findings:**

1. **No Written Avesta:** The scholarly consensus maintains that the *Avesta* was an oral corpus in the Achaemenid period. Therefore, Alexander did not burn a "Zoroastrian Bible": the canon lived in the priests, and at most a prestige copy (§ 5.2) could have perished with the archives.  
2. **Destruction of Archives:** Alexander *did* burn the royal administrative archives of Persepolis. The tablets name scribes writing on parchment and hides sent to the treasury, and the Treasury's sealings once closed bundled goods and documents; of the parchment nothing survives. The Zoroastrian legend of the gold-inked hides likely preserves a historical memory of this administrative loss, conflated over time with religious loss.  
3. **The Slaughter of the Living Books:** As Mary Boyce wrote, "the priests were the living books of the faith" (1979, 79), and the conquest killed many of them. The disruption of the oral transmission chain caused by the conquest was the true mechanism of the text's fragmentation.  
4. **Historiographical Construction:** The narrative of the "Burned Book" was crystallized by the Sasanian priesthood to explain the gaps in their tradition and to legitimize the creation of a written canon in the face of rival scriptural religions.

In the final analysis, Alexander the Great was indeed the destroyer of the Achaemenid textual heritage—not by burning a single library of gold-inked skins, but by shattering the imperial and priestly infrastructure that sustained the oral memory of Iran.
