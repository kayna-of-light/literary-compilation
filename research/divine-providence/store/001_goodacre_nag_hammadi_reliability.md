## Detailed Findings

### The "Growing Jar" Problem (pp. 305-306)
The jar itself no longer exists — Muhammad Ali smashed it. Robinson estimates its size from 1970s interviews as "60 cm or more" (just under 2 ft). But Pagels inflates this to "almost a meter high" in *Gnostic Gospels* (1979), then to "six feet high" in *The Gnostics* (1987) and *Beyond Belief* (2003). The jar literally triples in size across retellings. Goodacre: "A six-foot jar would be enormous. This is unfeasibly large, three times the size of the two-foot jar of the earliest accounts."

### Three Conflicting Versions (pp. 307-308)
Robinson published the story three times with escalating detail and contradictions:
- **1977** (Nag Hammadi Library in English): Two brothers — Muhammad and Khalifah Ali
- **1979** (Biblical Archaeologist): Seven people — three sons of 'Umm-Ahmad plus four camel drivers. **Abu al-Majd actually unearthed the jar**, not Muhammad Ali.
- **1981**: Eight people — five other camel drivers. Again Abu al-Majd made the find.

In ALL later versions, it is Abu al-Majd — not Muhammad Ali — who actually found the jar. Muhammad Ali, Robinson's informant and the man whose name is universally attached to the discovery, may not have been the discoverer at all.

### Kasser and Krause's Disclaimer (pp. 308-309)
Two scholars who worked WITH Robinson on the Nag Hammadi Library — Rodolphe Kasser and Martin Krause — published a remarkable disclaimer in the 1984 *Facsimile Edition*:

> "Rodolphe Kasser and Martin Krause wish to make it known here that they have serious reasons to put in doubt the objective value of a number of important points of the Introduction that follows. They contest especially the detailed history of the discovery... Kasser and Krause and others who were involved do not consider as assured anything more than the core of the story (the general location and approximate date of the discovery), the rest not having for them more than the value of stories and fables that one can collect in popular Egyptian circles thirty years after an event whose exceptional significance the protagonists could not at the time understand."

This disclaimer was buried in tiny font in square brackets in a two-page footnote in an expensive technical volume. Most scholars have never seen it.

### The Channel 4 Documentary (pp. 310-312)
In 1987, *The Gnostics* documentary featured Muhammad Ali on camera — his only known filmed appearance. Key divergences from Robinson's written accounts:
- Ali says he was ALONE when he found and broke the pot ("I smashed the pot on my own")
- He says "We were seven" only AFTER finding the books — brought others to see
- He explicitly says "We don't want anything to do with these books... It's nothing to do with us... It was all just rubbish to us"
- When asked if he had regrets: "No, I don't care. I don't give a damn about it. It doesn't even enter my head to think about it."
- The blood feud is confirmed but presented as a SEPARATE event, not temporally linked to the discovery the way Robinson narrates it

### Robinson's Methods (pp. 313-315)
- Robinson did NOT tape-record interviews or publish his notes
- Ethnographic field methods were "in their infancy" in the 1970s
- Robinson acknowledged Muhammad Ali showed "instances of not impeccable veracity"
- Financial incentives were routine: "Whenever I went down, I would bring the villagers a bottle of whiskey and a ten-pound note. That was big money at the time."
- Kasser scornfully noted Robinson arrived "in full force, accompanied by Egyptian notables, promising compensation to his informants. For them, the windfall was beautiful."
- Muhammad Ali may have been "playing back what he had learned was the 'correct' number" of codices (thirteen) rather than remembering

### Jean Doresse's 1958 Account (pp. 315-316)
Doresse investigated in the late 1940s — within YEARS of the discovery, not decades later. His account is leaner, less detailed, and probably more reliable:
- He did not ask leading questions or offer incentives
- He found the discovery site: "the south-east flank of the Gebel et-Tarif, where there are entrances to the tombs of the princes of the sixth dynasty"
- His informants told him: "some peasants of Hamra-Dûm and of Dabba, in search of manure, found here a great zîr — which means jar — filled with leaves of papyrus; and these were bound like books"
- "The vase was broken and nothing remains of it; the manuscripts were taken to Cairo and no one knows what then became of them"
- Crucially: the site was near TOMBS — ancient cemetery, not random cliff

### The "Orientalizing" Problem (pp. 317-318)
Goodacre notes the Robinson narrative has an implicit colonial structure: "bloodthirsty, illiterate peasants happen upon an amazing find while out looking for fertilizer. They worry about genies but lust for gold, they have no inkling of the magnitude of their find, and their mother is as stupid as she is callous, burning valuable documents and then encouraging her sons to use the very mattocks that had broken open the earthenware jar now to murder a man."

The story "enables academics to celebrate their literacy, civilization and wealth. It is a story of the rescue of valuable documents from the hands of those who cannot hope to understand them, and placed in the hands of responsible, Western academics."

### Goodacre's Conclusion (pp. 318-319)
- "Were the discoverers really just farmers out looking for sabakh or were they grave-robbers who found the jar in an ancient cemetery?"
- The grave-robbing theory explains: Ali's mention of a corpse (denied by his brother), the cemetery location noted by Doresse, and the cover stories
- Robinson's narrative is like the Synoptic Gospels: "James Robinson in the 1970s, like Mark in the 70s, provides a compelling new written account that then forms the basis for several subsequent accounts. Elaine Pagels produces so strong a new version, embellished with legendary details, that it becomes as famous as Robinson's, just as Matthew writes a new version of Mark, likewise embellished with legendary details."
- "Sometimes, admitting our ignorance is a virtue."

## What We Can Confirm
1. Codices were found near the Jabal al-Tarif cliff, Upper Egypt
2. Discovery was approximately December 1945
3. The site was near ancient tombs (Doresse confirms)
4. The texts were in a jar (multiple sources confirm)
5. Some texts were damaged or destroyed after discovery
6. The blood feud is a real historical event (confirmed independently)

## What We Cannot Confirm
1. Who specifically found the jar (Muhammad Ali? Abu al-Majd? someone else?)
2. How many people were present (2? 7? 8? 1?)
3. Whether the discovery was while digging for fertilizer or while grave-robbing
4. Whether the texts were deliberately buried for preservation or deposited as grave goods
5. The mother burning texts in the oven (may be a trope borrowed from other discovery narratives)
6. The precise connection between the blood feud and the discovery timing

## Significance for the Research

This is devastating for the romanticized narrative but NOT for the providential timing argument. What changes:

1. The "seed bank" reading — someone deliberately preserving texts from Athanasius's destruction — is ONE of several possible explanations. The grave goods theory is equally plausible on current evidence.
2. The discovery circumstances are much less certain than the library documents assume.
3. Robinson's narrative functions like a foundation myth — compelling, pedagogically useful, but not reliably historical.

What does NOT change:
1. The texts exist and their content is established.
2. They were buried in the 4th century (cartonnage dating 341-348 CE).
3. They were found in 1945.
4. The timing convergence with the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947) and Talpiot (1980) is real.
5. The correspondential content of the texts is present regardless of burial circumstances.

**The honest position:** We know the texts were buried near ancient tombs in the 4th century and found in 1945. We do not know whether the burial was protective (Athanasius response) or funerary (grave goods). Both theories have scholarly support. The thesis must acknowledge this uncertainty.
