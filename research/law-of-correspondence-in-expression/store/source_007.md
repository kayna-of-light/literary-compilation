# Source 007 — Northrop, *The Delsarte Method of Expression and Oratory* (1896); Monroe

**Citation**: Northrop, Henry Davenport, comp. and ed. *The Delsarte Method of Expression and Oratory.* Chicago: Monroe Book Company, 1896.

**Accessed**: 2026-10-24, archive.org `delsartemethodof0000henr` (djvu full text; Hamilton Public Library copy).

## What it is

A popular compilation: an exposition of the Delsarte method followed by recitations for "schools, lodges, public entertainments". It shows what the general American public was taught Delsarte meant.

## The passage (section "Delsarte's Philosophy", p. 10)

"Delsarte was a man of religious feeling and knew religious books better than other books. He was acquainted with the lore of the priests, to which he was indebted for his philosophy. Tradition affirms that he was much interested in, and studied Swedenborg. This theory is confirmed by the fact that certain fundamental ideas in his philosophy and the expression of them are intrinsically Swedenborgian. One of the fundamental principles of Delsarte's philosophy is the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg, who held that the material world corresponds to the spiritual world and is the manifestation of man's mental being. In other words, that the spiritual world is symbolized in the physical world. Applied to expression, the interpretation of this law is: 'Every expression of the face, every posture of the body, corresponds to, or is but an outward expression of, an inner emotion or condition of the mind.'"

Next heading, "Law of Correspondence": "the head in its poise, etc., represents intellect better than any other part of the body; the trunk, affection; the limbs, power."

## Monroe

Lewis B. Monroe (Boston) called Delsarte "Swedenborg geometrized" and the ninefold chart "the key of the universe" (Stebbins 1901, 113–114; store 006). Hovey learned Delsarte from him (store 008).

## Why it matters

- By the 1890s the popular American handbooks taught the Law of Correspondence *as Swedenborg's discovery*, and they reported a "tradition" that Delsarte had studied Swedenborg.
- The manuscript scholarship rejects that tradition for Delsarte himself (Waille 2015, store 004). The tradition is evidence of how the Americans received and taught the law, not of Delsarte's own reading.
- Northrop's form of the law, "an outward expression of an inner emotion or condition of the mind", is the one the 1930s studio would state as "the mind is the pilot".
