# What the Apparatus Cannot See: Translation, Selection, and the Concretising Drift Beneath the Christological Text
## A Three-Question Audit of the Contested Passages Across the Hebrew, Greek, and Samaritan Critical Editions

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis reports a systematic audit of the christological text conducted across four primary critical editions — *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, *Novum Testamentum Graece* 27 and 28, the Septuagint, and the Samaritan Pentateuch — covering the passages on which the standard arguments about Jesus are built, the passages commonly alleged to be later additions, and the Old Testament texts conventionally cited as prophecy. Of each passage three questions were asked separately: what the text says in its source script, whether the text is secure in the manuscript tradition, and whether the passage is integral to its literary context. Two results follow. First, the text is far more secure than either side of the public argument assumes: there is one clean forgery (the Comma Johanneum, present in the running Greek of four late Latin-influenced manuscripts, one of which omits its own closing clause), one decisive alteration (ΟΣ → ΘΣ at 1 Timothy 3:16, where the three oldest witnesses read "who" in their first hand and were all later corrected to "God"), and fifteen Textus Receptus verses that resolve into harmonisation, duplication, gloss and liturgical formula, none doctrinal — while the sayings with the strongest motive for intervention were largely left alone. Second, and unanticipated, most of the distance between what the sources say and what is claimed for them was produced by three processes that leave no variants at all: **translation** (nine loci where the argument lives in a Septuagint reading unavailable in the Hebrew, produced by Alexandrian Jewish translators centuries before the dispute existed), **selection** (New Testament authors moving between Hebrew and Greek according to which yielded the needed reading), and **later reading** (Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9:5–6, staples of the modern argument, are cited by no New Testament author). Assembled, the drift is uniform in direction and is not from a low christology to a high one but from figurative to literal — corporate to individual, instrumental to physical, relation to identity — across three sets of hands with nothing in common. The thesis argues that this removes the strongest objection to a correspondential reading of the material without confirming it, and states explicitly that the ordinary account of how figurative language concretises is sufficient for every case examined and is not distinguished from the correspondential account by any evidence gathered here.

> **Keywords.** textual criticism, Nestle-Aland, *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, Comma Johanneum, 1 Timothy 3:16, Western Non-Interpolations, *nomina sacra*, *qere perpetuum*, Psalm 110, Granville Sharp, μονογενής, correspondences, literalization, Marcionite witness, editorial fatigue, christology

## 1. Introduction: The Argument Is Not Where the Argument Is Looking

The public argument about what the New Testament says concerning Jesus is conducted almost entirely in the register of textual criticism. One side holds that the high christological material is late — that a Galilean teacher was progressively elevated by scribes who added, altered, and interpolated until a man became God. The other holds that the manuscripts are secure and the high material original, and that the sceptic's case is an ideological construction laid over an unbroken transmission. Both sides appeal to the apparatus. Both sides can quote it accurately. Neither side is looking where the evidence is.

This thesis reports the results of a systematic audit conducted across the primary critical editions — *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, *Novum Testamentum Graece* in its twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth editions, the Septuagint, and the Samaritan Pentateuch — covering every passage on which the standard christological arguments are built, together with every passage commonly alleged to be a later addition and every Old Testament text conventionally cited as prophecy. Of each passage three questions were asked and answered separately: what does the text say in its source script; is the text secure in the manuscript tradition; and is the passage integral to its literary context. The third question was not part of the original design. It was forced by the evidence, and it turned out to do more work than the second.

The audit produces one central finding, and it is not the finding either side of the popular argument expects. **Most of the distance between what the sources say and what is claimed for them was created by three processes that leave no manuscript variants at all.** The first is *translation*: the Septuagint says things the Hebrew does not say, at nine of the load-bearing loci, and it says them because Jewish translators working in Alexandria two centuries and more before any of this was in dispute rendered figurative Hebrew into concrete Greek. The second is *selection*: the New Testament authors moved between the Hebrew and the Greek according to which yielded the reading their argument required — to the Greek at Isaiah 7:14, to the Hebrew at Hosea 11:1, to the Hebrew at Zechariah 12:10 where the Greek says something unusable. The third is *later reading*: the modern argument from prophecy leans hardest on Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9:5–6, which no New Testament author cites at all. The critical apparatus cannot see any of this, because none of it is a variant. It is all in the secure text.

The secondary findings are equally unexpected, and they cut against the sceptical reconstruction as sharply as against the apologetic one. **The text is far more secure than either side assumes.** There is exactly one clean forgery in the entire corpus — the Comma Johanneum, which stands in the running Greek of four late manuscripts, one of which omits its own final clause. There is one decisive one-letter change, ΟΣ to ΘΣ at 1 Timothy 3:16. The fifteen verses present in the Textus Receptus and absent from the critical text are synoptic harmonisations, an internal duplication, explanatory glosses and a liturgical formula — not one of them doctrinal. And the class of sayings with the strongest conceivable motive for intervention, the imminence sayings at Mark 9:1, Mark 13:30 and Matthew 10:23, together with "nor the Son" at Mark 13:32 and "why do you call me good" at Mark 10:18, was very largely left alone. Where scribes did intervene, the intervention is usually visible, usually small, and — at Jude 5 in NA28, where the editors moved the text *toward* the more christologically loaded reading — sometimes runs in the direction nobody predicts.

**The central argument follows from the direction in which the drift runs.** Assembled across all three silent mechanisms and across the manuscript variants as well, the movement is not from a low christology to a high one. It is from *figurative, corporate, instrumental and spatial* language toward *concrete, singular, individual and physical* language. Ears dug out become a body prepared. A young woman becomes a virgin. A measuring line becomes a voice. A pit becomes decay. The opening of a prison becomes sight for the blind. A collective seed acquires a masculine pronoun and becomes a single male descendant. A relative pronoun with no stated antecedent becomes the word *God*. A functional designation — ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, the Chosen One — becomes a creedal title, ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ. This is one direction, and it is visible in three sets of hands that had nothing in common: Alexandrian translators with no christology at all, evangelists with one, and later scribes with a different one again.

That convergence is the point at which the correspondential framework becomes relevant, and the thesis is deliberately careful about how far it presses. The companion thesis [*Reversing the Arrow*](../08_Correspondential_Texts/Reversing%20the%20Arrow_%20'Gnosticism'%20as%20the%20Downstream%20Literalization%20of%20Correspondential%20Composition.md) established that literalization — the conversion of dynamic states into static entities, interior qualities into exterior persons — is not a property of any particular community but the universal operation that occurs whenever a correspondential composition is received by readers who do not hold its key. If that is right, the concretising drift documented here is exactly what should be observed in the transmission of a correspondentially composed corpus. But an ordinary explanation is also available and is fully sufficient on its own: figurative language concretises whenever it crosses a language boundary or a generation, because translators and readers render what a phrase *means to them*, and what it means to them is the nearer and more graspable sense. **This audit cannot decide between those two descriptions.** They predict the same evidence at every point examined. What the audit does establish is that the drift is real, uniform in direction, and produced by mechanisms the apparatus cannot record — and that removes the strongest objection to the correspondential reading, which was that the elevation of a human figure by scribes had already explained the phenomenon without remainder. It has not, because that is not what the evidence shows happening.

The thesis proceeds as follows. **§2** sets out the method: the correspondential reading as this library defines it, the three questions, the primary editions and the source-gating protocol under which they were consulted, and the distinction between receiving and claiming that identity-christology structurally cannot represent. **§3** presents the narrow class of passages where the manuscripts convict the text — the Comma, 1 Timothy 3:16, the fifteen absent verses, the Western Non-Interpolations and the reversal forced by P75, and the correctors of Codex Sinaiticus who worked in opposite directions within a single century. **§4** presents the opposite class: passages transmitted without variation that are nonetheless compositionally secondary, where the manuscripts acquit and the composition indicts. **§5**, **§6** and **§7** present the three silent mechanisms in turn. **§8** presents the Samaritan Pentateuch as a control specimen — a complete, surviving, independently transmitted second edition of the Torah whose editing is documented rather than inferred. **§9** presents the finding that organises the rest: the drift from function to title, the titles that are shared with Adam, Israel, Cyrus and Isaac, and the argument John places in Jesus's own mouth at 10:34, where the ground given for the word θεός being used of anyone is that the word of God came to them. **§10** discusses what each layer contributes, what follows for the framework hypothesis, what the Marcionite witness can and cannot decide, and the limitations of the whole enterprise. **§11** concludes.

One boundary should be stated at the outset, because it governs everything that follows. **Nothing in this audit settles whether Jesus was divine, and the audit was not shaped to.** What it settles is narrower and more useful: that the popular arguments on both sides are running on features of the text that are not there. The sceptic's late-invention story understates how early and how secure the high material is. The apologist's argument from prophecy overstates what the sources say in their own languages. Both are arguing about a text that neither has read in the form in which it survives.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading: Expression, Not Allegory

Correspondence, as this library uses the term, is not a literary device. It is a claim about how reality is layered. Emanuel Swedenborg's position, argued across *Arcana Coelestia*, *Heaven and Hell* and *Divine Love and Wisdom*, is that every natural object, act and relation exists because an interior reality flows into it and takes form there. The natural thing does not *stand for* the spiritual thing; it *is* the spiritual thing at the outermost degree, in the only shape that degree can hold. The relation is therefore one of expression, not of resemblance, and it runs in one direction only: from inside outward. A correspondence can be read upward, from the form to what expresses itself in it, but the form is never the cause of what it carries.

Three distinctions follow, and they are load-bearing for everything in this thesis.

**Correspondence is not allegory.** An allegory is a story constructed to carry a meaning assigned to it by its author; the assignment is arbitrary and could have been made otherwise. A correspondence is not assigned. It is a function-relation that obtains whether or not anyone notices it. Water cleanses; truth cleanses; the correspondence of water to truth is not a decision but an observation about what each does at its own degree.

**Correspondence is not symbolism.** A symbol is a token agreed upon within a culture and legible only inside it. Correspondences, on this account, are stable across cultures precisely because they are grounded in function rather than convention, which is why the same relations surface in Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Israelite and Iranian material without contact sufficient to explain borrowing.

**Correspondence is not metaphor.** A metaphor asserts a likeness between two things that remain two. A correspondence asserts that one reality is being perceived at two degrees. When Paul writes ἡ πέτρα ἦν ὁ Χριστός — "the rock *was* Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4) — he does not write that the rock resembled Christ or prefigured him. He uses the copula. That is the grammar of correspondence rather than the grammar of simile, and it appears in the undisputed Pauline corpus in a textually secure verse.

A worked example, from a passage that will recur in §9:

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural form | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Rock in the wilderness (1 Cor 10:4) | The stable ground of truth on which a people in transit subsists |
| Water from the rock | Truth received as sustenance in a state of privation |
| The rock "following" them | The same source present at every stage of a passage through desolation |
| Light (John 1:4–9) | Divine wisdom in its illuminating aspect, received according to the state of the receiver |
| The bosom of the Father (John 1:18, εἰς τὸν κόλπον) | Interior conjunction — the state of being within what one expresses, not adjacent to it |

The relevance of this to a textual-critical audit is precise and limited. If a corpus is correspondentially composed, then its statements about a central figure will tend to be built as **through-language**: expression, image, manifestation, indwelling, exegesis of the invisible. They will point *through* the figure to what is expressed in him. If a corpus is not correspondentially composed but shares the same cultural background, its statements will tend to be built as **function-language** or, further downstream, as **title-predication**: X is Y, with appellations stacked. The prediction that follows is testable in principle, and §9.4 reports what the audit found. What the audit cannot do — and this is stated here so that it need not be re-stated at every turn — is establish that the correspondential account of the layering is true. It can establish only whether the textual evidence behaves as that account predicts.

### 2.2 The Three Questions

Every passage in the audit was put to three questions, and the discipline of the method consists in refusing to let them collapse into one another. In ordinary discussion they routinely do, with the result that a claim established at one level is defended with evidence drawn from another.

**Question one: what does the text say?** This means the wording, the grammar and the lexical range in the source script — Hebrew, Greek, or Samaritan Hebrew — before any inherited translation choice is permitted to settle the matter. It is asked first because the other two cannot be asked coherently until it is answered. A great deal of the argument about the New Testament is conducted over English renderings whose decisions were made centuries ago and are no longer visible as decisions: *only-begotten* for μονογενής, *hell* for three unrelated Greek words, *virgin* for עַלְמָה.

**Question two: is the text secure?** This means the manuscript support, the significant variants, the earliest attestation, and whether the reading is a known or suspected interpolation. It is the question the popular argument treats as decisive, and the audit's most consistent result is that it decides far less than either side supposes.

**Question three: is the passage integral to its context?** This question was added during the audit, under pressure from the evidence, and it changed the shape of the results. A passage can stand in every surviving manuscript without variation and still be a later insertion, detectable only by the seam it leaves: an argument broken and then resumed, vocabulary the author uses nowhere else, an editorial voice in the first person plural, a conclusion given twice. **Manuscript unanimity proves that a passage was in place before our oldest surviving copy. It does not prove that it stood in the author's text**, and the gap between composition and earliest extant witness is, for every New Testament book, at least a century. Questions two and three are therefore evidentially independent, and a passage can be secure on one and secondary on the other. §4 presents four such cases.

The separation matters in both directions. When 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1 is defended on the ground that no manuscript omits it, an answer to question two is being offered to an objection raised at question three, and it is not responsive. When Matthew 28:19 is alleged to be an interpolation on the ground that Eusebius cites a shorter form, an argument proper to question three is being dressed as an answer to question two, for which it has no manuscript support whatever. The rule has to run both ways or it is not a rule.

### 2.3 The Primary Editions and the Source-Gating Protocol

The audit was conducted against four bodies of primary text, and the first methodological point to register is that two of them are made on opposite principles.

*Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* is a **diplomatic** edition. It reproduces a single manuscript — the Leningrad Codex, B19A, sigel **L**, copied in the eleventh century — and confines every judgement to the apparatus beneath. What is printed is what one scribe wrote. *Novum Testamentum Graece* is an **eclectic** edition. What is printed is a reconstruction that exists in no manuscript at all, assembled reading by reading from the whole tradition. The Hebrew edition prints an artefact; the Greek edition prints an inference. This asymmetry is invisible in translation and is quietly responsible for a good deal of confusion, because a reader who compares "the Hebrew" with "the Greek" is comparing one eleventh-century manuscript with a twentieth-century committee's best judgement about a first-century original.

The BHS apparatus operates on its own conventions, and two of them bear directly on the findings below. The **Ketiv/Qere** system preserves the received consonants (כְּתִיב, *what is written*) while placing on them the vowels of the word the Masoretes intended to be read (קְרֵי, *what is read*), with the replacement consonants in the margin; the form standing in the text is therefore a hybrid never meant to be pronounced as it appears. The *qere perpetuum* does this silently, without marginal note, for high-frequency words — and the most consequential instance in the whole audit is that **יהוה is pointed with the vowels of אֲדֹנָי**. §5.3 traces what follows from that.

On the Greek side, the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth editions of Nestle-Aland were consulted together, with explicit confirmation demanded at each locus of whether they agree. They almost always do: the text of NA27 was identical to NA26 and stood unchanged for over thirty years, and NA28 altered it in **exactly thirty-four places, all in the Catholic Epistles**, under the Coherence-Based Genealogical Method developed for the *Editio Critica Maior*. CBGM abandons the family-tree model of classical stemmatics — which fails because scribes contaminated, copying from several exemplars at once — in favour of comparing whole texts across thousands of variant units, measuring how consistently the direction of development runs between any two witnesses, and reconstructing an *Ausgangstext* that is then entered into the database as a further witness. NA28 also abolished the square brackets in the Catholic Epistles, replacing them with split guiding lines where the editors judge two readings equally possible: an admission that the older brackets were recording editorial doubt rather than evidence.

For the Marcionite material the audit used Dieter T. Roth's *The Text of Marcion's Gospel*, and adopted his standard of certainty as the standard for the audit as a whole. Roth works from three hostile witnesses. Tertullian, *Adversus Marcionem* IV, attests roughly ninety per cent of the 486 verses known to have stood in the gospel and is the **sole** witness for about two-thirds of them; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 42, supplies numbered *scholia*; the *Dialogue of Adamantius* is third and least trusted, its author working from anti-Marcionite secondary literature and its Latin transmission conforming readings toward the Vulgate. Roth's discipline lies in analysing the citation habits themselves — Tertullian quoted loosely, from memory, drifted into Matthean and Markan wording, and paraphrased for rhetorical effect, so a Latin synonym is not evidence of a Greek variant — and above all in his typographic grading, introduced to correct the speculative confidence of Harnack's reconstruction:

| Marking | Evidential status |
|---|---|
| **Bold** | Secure: citation habit and attestation agree, or multiple independent sources |
| ***Bold italic*** | Very likely: citation habit clear but attestation lacking, or the reverse |
| Regular | Probable: some evidence, not definitive |
| *Italic* | Possible: attested, carrying no strong historical confidence |
| *(Italic in parentheses)* | The verse element is attested; the wording is unrecoverable |
| {Curly brackets} | Words attested, word order uncertain |
| … | Unattested |

The value of this scale is that it separates three claims that ordinary discussion fuses: *this text existed*, *this wording existed*, and *we cannot know*. Every judgement in §§3–9 below is intended to be readable against it.

Finally, a protocol point that is procedural but was decisive for the reliability of the results. The primary editions were consulted in an environment that also holds this library's own framework theses, and the two were **never enabled together for a primary-text question**. The single weakest result produced early in the audit — a New Testament reading cited to a framework reconstruction rather than to a critical edition — was caused by leaving everything switched on at once. Primary-text questions receive primary sources only. This is what keeps `[P]` evidence and `[T]` reconstruction from being cited in the same register, and it is the reason the findings below can be checked independently of anything this library argues.

### 2.4 Receiving and Claiming: The Distinction Identity-Christology Cannot Represent

The popular argument runs on a shared and unexamined premise: that a christological statement is an **identity statement** — a claim about which entity a name denotes. Once that premise is in place the whole dispute becomes an argument about how many entities there are and which one Jesus is, and both sides can quote the same secure text indefinitely without moving. It is worth noticing that the premise is not in the sources. It is a conceptual apparatus that arrives later, with the fourth-century controversies, and is then read back into first-century documents that were not built to answer it. The Greek New Testament contains no word for a threefold godhead: τριάς, or any abstract noun for such a thing, occurs nowhere in it.

The position from which this audit reads is a third thing, and it is not a compromise between the two poles. It holds that the figure in question is a soul of the celestial degree who **descended**; that what is expressed through him is the Father, without remainder, because he is transparent to what flows through him; and that the relation between them is **correspondential** — the Father as divine love, the Son as that love expressed in the form of divine wisdom, so that mention of the Son points *through* him to what he expresses. This is not a second entity standing alongside the first, and it is not a man later promoted. The library's treatments of discrete degrees and of the operation of the divine through a vessel set out the underlying architecture: see [*Asking the Skin to Be the Heart*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/Asking%20the%20Skin%20to%20Be%20the%20Heart_%20The%20Category%20Error%20of%20Physical%20Miracle%20Theology%20and%20the%20Architecture%20of%20Discrete%20Degrees.md) and [*The Bridge That Became the Path*](../02_Swedenborgian_Theology/The%20Bridge%20That%20Became%20the%20Path_%20The%20Lord's%20Operation%20Through%20the%20Vessel,%20and%20the%20Error%20of%20Making%20One%20Soul%20the%20Exception.md).

The operative distinction is between **receiving** and **claiming**. A vessel perfectly transparent to what fills it can truthfully say "the Father in me" and would never say "I am the Father" — not because the second is forbidden but because continuous receiving knows itself as receiving. Only the self, taking what flows through it to be its own possession, produces the second sentence. Identity-statement christology has no way to represent this at all: it must resolve the case into either two Gods or one man, because its only available operator is the equals sign.

This matters methodologically, and not only theologically, for a reason that becomes visible in the data. **If the sources are composed in through-language and are read by an apparatus that can only register identity-predication, then every genuinely correspondential statement will be mis-scored** — assigned to one pole or the other, and cited by whichever side finds it convenient. John 10:30 is the clearest instance. The Greek reads ἐγὼ καὶ ὁ πατὴρ ἕν ἐσμεν: **ἕν is neuter**, "one thing," not the masculine εἷς, "one person," and the same neuter is applied to the disciples at John 17:21–22. The verse is textually secure in P45, P66, P75, ℵ, B, A, W and the Byzantine tradition; nothing in the apparatus is in dispute. Read as an identity statement it is either a proof of deity or an embarrassment to be explained away. Read as it stands, it says something the identity frame has no slot for.

The framework claim is therefore being used here in the way this library uses Swedenborg generally — as a hypothesis under test rather than a doctrine under defence. Its prediction is specific and is stated in advance in §2.1: correspondentially composed material should carry christological weight through expression-language, and material that shares the same cultural background without being correspondentially composed should carry it through function-language or title-predication. §9.4 reports whether the corpus behaves that way, and §10.5 states plainly why the result there is suggestive rather than conclusive.

## 3. What the Manuscripts Convict: The Narrow Class of Real Corruption

The audit began with the expectation that question two — is the text secure — would carry most of the argumentative load. It does not. Of the passages on which the standard christological case is built, the overwhelming majority are transmitted without any variant that changes what is being claimed. John 1:1 is uniform in P66, P75, ℵ, B, C, W and the Byzantine tradition. John 8:58, John 10:30, John 10:33, John 20:28 and John 5:18 are stable across every early papyrus and uncial. Philippians 2:6–11, Colossians 1:15–20, Colossians 2:9, Hebrews 1:6, Hebrews 1:10 and Revelation 3:14 have no variant of substance among them: an article present or absent, a word transposed, a tense form. Whatever is argued from the christological hymns is argued from a stable text, and it follows that the dispute at those passages is lexical and grammatical rather than text-critical and should be conducted as such.

What this section presents is therefore the residue: the small, well-defined class of places where the manuscripts genuinely convict the printed tradition. The class is smaller than the sceptical account requires, and its contents are more banal. But it is not empty, and where it does bite, it bites hard — and the two places it bites hardest are both places where a statement about relation or manifestation has been converted into a statement of identity.

### 3.1 The Comma Johanneum: One Clean Forgery

The words at 1 John 5:7–8 — "there are three that bear witness in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit, and these three are one" — constitute the only passage in the corpus that is a bare identity statement about three named parties. They are also the only passage in the corpus with no ancient Greek existence whatsoever.

The reading stands in the running Greek text of **exactly four manuscripts**, and all four are late and all four are under Latin influence:

| Manuscript | Date | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 629, Codex Ottobonianus | 14th c. | Greek–Latin bilingual; the Greek column reworked to match the facing Latin |
| 61, Codex Montfortianus | c. 1520 | The manuscript produced for Erasmus; **omits the closing clause "and these three are one"** |
| 918 | 16th c. | Escorial, paper |
| 2318 | 18th c. | Bucharest, written under printed Vulgate influence |

Every papyrus, every uncial and every early minuscule omits it. Where the words appear elsewhere in Greek — in 221, 88, 429, 636 — they are marginal additions entered by hands centuries later than the manuscripts they annotate. The versional evidence is equally decisive: the Comma is absent from the Coptic in both dialects and from the Syriac tradition. Even the Latin evidence is split, the reading being absent from the critical editions of the Vulgate and appearing only in late Vulgate manuscripts, the Clementine edition, the *Speculum*, and the late Latin fathers Priscillian and Fulgentius.

Two features of this deserve to be held together. The first is that the earliest Greek witness with the words *in its text* is fourteenth-century, and it is a bilingual harmonised to its own Latin column: which is to say the Greek tradition acquired the verse by translation from Latin, after the doctrine it supports had already been settled for a thousand years. The second is Montfortianus. The manuscript produced to satisfy Erasmus's undertaking **drops the final clause** — whoever made it was working from a Latin exemplar and did not reproduce the sentence whole. The strongest trinitarian proof-text in the printed Bible thus has a Latin ancestry, a sixteenth-century debut in the printed Greek, and a defective first witness.

The Erasmus episode itself — the undertaking, the appearance of 61, the third edition of 1522 — lies outside the primary editions and is recorded here as an external matter requiring independent trace.

### 3.2 1 Timothy 3:16: One Crossbar

The verse reads either "**who** was manifested in flesh" or "**God** was manifested in flesh." The difference is between a relative clause with no stated antecedent and a flat assertion of incarnation, and it is produced by a single stroke of ink.

**ὅς, "who", is the earlier reading**, supported by the *original hands* of the great uncials — Sinaiticus (ℵ*, 4th c.), Alexandrinus (A*, 5th c.), Ephraemi Rescriptus (C*, 5th c.) — together with Augiensis (F) and Boernerianus (G), both 9th c., minuscules 33 and 365, and the fourth-century fathers Didymus and Epiphanius.

**θεός, "God", is the later reading**, and in the oldest manuscripts it appears *only as correction*, and late: the Ephraemi corrector (C²) and the Claromontanus corrector (D²), both working in the ninth century, four hundred years after the pages they were altering were first written. Otherwise it is Ψ (9th–10th c.), 088, 1739, 1881, and the Byzantine majority from the ninth century onward. A third reading, the neuter ὅ, stands in the original hand of Claromontanus (D*, 6th c.) and behind the Latin *quod*, agreeing grammatically with "mystery" and almost certainly a scribal tidying.

The mechanism is visible on the page. In uncial script "who" is **ΟΣ**; "God", written as a *nomen sacrum*, is **ΘΣ** with an overline. Omicron is a plain circle and theta is the same circle with one horizontal stroke through it. The entire difference between "who was manifested" and "God was manifested" is a short bar inside a circle plus a line above two letters — an amount of ink that faded exemplars, show-through from the other side of a leaf, or a scribe's settled conviction about what the verse ought to say could each supply.

This is the cleanest case in the audit of a doctrinal reading entering the text by a combination of physical accident and scribal preference rather than by transmission. And the direction of change is not inferred but observed: the three oldest witnesses all read "who" in their first hand, and all three were later corrected to "God." **Nobody corrected "God" into "who."**

### 3.3 The Fifteen Absent Verses: The Banality of Textual Growth

Fifteen verses stand in the Textus Receptus and are absent from the modern critical text. They are the most frequently cited evidence that the Bible has been tampered with, deployed with equal confidence by those who think modern editors removed them and by those who think medieval scribes added them. Sorted by mechanism, they turn out to be neither a conspiracy nor an outrage but a catalogue of ordinary scribal habit.

| Mechanism | Verses |
|---|---|
| Harmonisation to a synoptic parallel | Matthew 17:21 (from Mark 9:29), Matthew 18:11 (from Luke 19:10), Matthew 23:14 (from Mark 12:40 / Luke 20:47), Mark 11:26 (from Matthew 6:15), Mark 15:28 (from Luke 22:37), Luke 17:36 (from Matthew 24:40), Luke 23:17 (from Mark 15:6) |
| Internal duplication within the same passage | Mark 9:44 and 9:46, both repeating the Isaiah 66:24 line already standing at 9:48 |
| Explanatory gloss absorbed from the margin | John 5:4 (the legend of the troubled pool), Acts 15:34, 24:7, 28:29 — all Western, all smoothing a narrative seam |
| Liturgical formula | Mark 7:16 ("let him who has ears"), Romans 16:24 (a benediction duplicating v. 20) |

The omission is supported by ℵ and B throughout, and by P45 at Matthew 17:21, P75 at Luke 17:36 and 23:17, P66 and P75 at John 5:4, and **P46 at Romans 16:24**. The additions are Byzantine and Western. Two further passages belong to the same class and are likewise absent from the printed text without even a bracket: **Acts 8:37**, the Ethiopian's confession of faith, missing from P45, P74, ℵ, A, B and C; and **Matthew 6:13b**, the doxology of the Lord's Prayer, missing from ℵ, B, D, Z, and from Origen and Cyprian. Both are still recited liturgically by millions of people every week.

**Not one of the fifteen changes a christological claim.** This is the datum, and it cuts in both directions. Any argument that the tradition was systematically doctored has to explain why the overwhelming majority of its additions are of this harmless kind — a copyist matching a parallel, completing a familiar formula, or absorbing a reader's explanatory note. Any argument that the tradition was pristine has to explain why fifteen verses accumulated at all. What the class actually demonstrates is that **the ordinary machinery of textual growth is banal**, and that the burden on anyone claiming a doctrinally motivated corruption is to show that a given case is not an instance of this machinery.

The two most famous disputed passages belong here too, and are the two about which the editions are most confident. Mark 16:9–20, the Longer Ending, is omitted by ℵ and B, by the Sinaitic Syriac and the early Coptic, and by Eusebius and Jerome; it is present in A, C, W, D, the Byzantine tradition and Irenaeus in the second century. John 7:53–8:11, the woman taken in adultery, is omitted by P66, P75, ℵ, B, C, L and W, and appears first in D in the fifth century. Both are printed in double brackets: the editors regard them as certainly not original and print them for their history in the church. The decisive datum on the second is not its absence from early witnesses but its **mobility**. It appears after John 7:52, after 7:36, after 21:25, and in some manuscripts in Luke. A passage that floats is a passage that was inserted, because a passage that belongs to a text does not travel through it.

### 3.4 The Western Non-Interpolations and the Reversal at P75

Eight short passages in Luke 22–24 are present in the great Alexandrian manuscripts and absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin. Westcott and Hort, in 1881, named them **Western Non-Interpolations** and judged the shorter text original — the one place in their edition where they departed from their own Alexandrian preference.

| Passage | Words at issue | Omitted by | NA27/28 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luke 22:19b–20 | "which is given for you … poured out for you" | D, Old Latin a d ff2 i l | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:3 | "of the Lord Jesus" | D, Old Latin a b d e ff2 l r1 | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:6 | "He is not here, but has risen" | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:12 | the whole verse: Peter runs to the tomb | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:36 | "and said to them, Peace be with you" | D, Old Latin a b d e f l r1 | printed, unbracketed |
| Luke 24:40 | the whole verse: he showed them his hands and feet | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic and Curetonian Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:51 | "and was carried up into heaven" | ℵ* (original hand), D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |
| Luke 24:52 | "worshipped him" | D, Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac | bracketed |

**The pattern is completely consistent.** Every one of the eight is omitted by Bezae and the Old Latin and by nothing else of weight, which is precisely why Westcott and Hort treated them as a class rather than as eight independent accidents. And the content of the class is not random either: the words at issue are, with one exception, the words that make the resurrection tangible and the ascension physical — a body given, a name attached to a corpse, hands and feet displayed, a bodily assumption, an act of worship.

**But the decisive datum runs the other way.** Papyrus 75, of the third century, contains all eight, and it is two to three centuries older than Bezae. This is why the editions from NA26 onward reversed Westcott and Hort and restored the words to the text, bracketing only half of them. The choice is now between a single early manuscript and a coherent early tradition, and the editors went with the manuscript.

Two observations follow, and they should be kept apart. The first is that this episode is the standing demonstration of reasoned eclecticism working as intended: editors reversed a judgement inherited from their own founding tradition when new physical evidence appeared. It is the strongest available answer to the claim that the critical text is an ideological artefact. The second is that the reversal **does not settle the direction of change**. A scribe adding "and was carried up into heaven" and a scribe removing it are both explicable, and six of the eight are printed unbracketed on the authority of one papyrus. What is worth carrying forward is a small detail at Luke 24:51: the words are absent from the *original hand* of Sinaiticus and supplied by a corrector — the same corrector-pattern seen at 1 Timothy 3:16, running in the same direction.

The physicalising material in Luke 24 is discussed further in §4 and §9, and its relation to the reconstructed Proto-Lukan tradition is treated at length in [*The Forensic Stratigraphy of the Resurrection*](../04_Early_Christian_History/The%20Forensic%20Stratigraphy%20of%20the%20Resurrection_%20A%20Critical%20Reconstruction%20of%20the%20Tomb%20Narrative%20in%20Proto-Luke%20and%20the%20Marcionite%20Witness.md).

### 3.5 The Corrections That Run Both Ways

A tidy theory of unidirectional scribal expansion is available, attractive, and refuted by the manuscripts. Three sets of data refuse it.

**The correctors of Sinaiticus disagree with each other.** At Luke 22:43–44 the *original hand* of Sinaiticus contains the angel and the sweat like blood, and a corrector removed them; the passage is also absent from P69 and P75 and from Vaticanus and the Sinaitic Syriac, and present in D, L and the Byzantine tradition, and cited by Justin in the second century. At Luke 23:34a the *original hand* lacks "Father, forgive them," and a corrector added it; here P75, Vaticanus, D* and W omit, while A, C, L and the Byzantine tradition include. The same codex, the same century, corrections running in opposite directions at two passages a page apart. Whatever was moving the scribes, it was not a single programme.

**The variant at John 1:18 runs against the expected drift.** The reading μονογενὴς θεός is carried by the *earliest* witnesses — P66 (c. 200), P75, ℵ*, B, C*, L — while ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός is carried by the later ones: A, C³, K, W, Θ, Ψ, f¹, f¹³, the Byzantine majority, and the Latin and Syriac versions. Between them stands a third reading, the articular ὁ μονογενὴς θεός in the Sinaiticus corrector and in 33. That is two distinct movements at one verse: a scribe first making the anarthrous reading more definite, and the tradition subsequently swinging to υἱός instead. Any account claiming a single uniform elevation of christology by copyists has to deal with a first-rank variant where the elevation is in the oldest witnesses and the deflation is later.

**The modern editors moved a reading upward.** Of the thirty-four changes NA28 introduced, the most consequential is at **Jude 5**, where NA27's [ὁ] κύριος becomes **Ἰησοῦς** — so that the current critical edition prints the statement that *Jesus* saved a people out of Egypt. The editors moved the text toward the more christologically loaded reading, on the evidence and against the expectation. Anyone arguing that modern critical scholarship systematically deflates christology has to account for this, and the account cannot be that the editors were following a programme, because the programme would have forbidden it.

Two further data belong here because they are so often mis-filed. **1 Corinthians 14:34–35 is not omitted by any manuscript**; but D, F and G — the Western group — place it after verse 40. Dislocation without omission is the signature of a marginal note that entered the text at different points in different copies, which is a *stronger* argument for secondary origin than omission would be, not a weaker one. And **Matthew 28:19 has no textual problem at all**: no Greek manuscript of any century lacks the triadic baptismal formula, and the claim that it is an interpolation rests entirely on Eusebius citing a shorter form in some pre-Nicene works. A patristic citation habit is not a variant reading. Treating it as one is exactly the move that would be refused if an apologist made it in the other direction, and it is refused here.

## 4. What the Manuscripts Acquit: Compositional Seams in Unanimous Text

The third question — is the passage integral to its context — was not in the original design of this audit. It was forced by a recurring result: passage after passage that every manuscript transmits without variation, and that nonetheless carries visible marks of having been inserted. Because the two questions are evidentially independent, this class exists and has to be handled on its own terms. **The manuscripts acquit; the composition indicts.**

The logic is straightforward once stated. Our earliest continuous witnesses to any New Testament book postdate its composition by a century or more. Manuscript unanimity therefore establishes only that a passage was in place before the earliest surviving copy — a period that comfortably accommodates the entire formative history of the tradition. Everything that happened in that window is invisible to the apparatus by construction.

**John 21.** No manuscript ends the gospel at 20:31. But 20:30–31 is a formal conclusion — "many other signs … these are written so that you may believe" — and it is followed by a further chapter that ends with a second conclusion at 21:25. Within that chapter the narrative voice changes twice: at 21:24 it becomes an editorial first person plural, "we know that his testimony is true," and at 21:25 an authorial first person singular, "I suppose." A text with two endings, in which someone who is not the narrator vouches for the narrator, is a text that has been extended. The apparatus records none of this because the extension happened before the copying tradition we can see.

**2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1.** Remove the passage and 6:13 runs directly into 7:2 without a seam — "widen your hearts also … make room for us" — restoring an argument the passage interrupts. The vocabulary is the strongest datum: the paragraph contains at least six words Paul uses nowhere else, among them **Βελιάρ** and **παντοκράτωρ**, and its sharp dualism of light against darkness, righteousness against lawlessness, believer against unbeliever has close analogues in the Qumran material and none in Paul's own treatment of association with outsiders. No manuscript omits it. The case rests wholly on question three, and it is a strong case there.

**Matthew 27:52–53.** At the death, tombs open and "many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised"; then, in the same sentence, they enter the holy city **"after his resurrection."** The events are separated by three days inside a single clause, and the raised bodies are left in an unnarrated interval in between. The verses are uniform in every witness; they read as an insertion into a passage that otherwise moves directly from the death to the centurion's confession.

**Romans 16:25–27.** Here the manuscripts do speak, and what they say is that the doxology **will not stay put**. It stands at the end of chapter 16 in ℵ, B, C, D and 1739; at the end of chapter 14 in the Byzantine tradition; in *both* places in A and 33; and, in the earliest witness of all, **at the end of chapter 15 in P46**. Three positions and a doubling. As with the woman taken in adultery, mobility is the evidence: a paragraph that occupies four different locations in the tradition was not composed in any of them.

**Ephesians 1:1.** The words ἐν Ἐφέσῳ are absent from P46, from the original hand of Sinaiticus, and from the original hand of Vaticanus, and Basil states that the oldest copies known to him lacked them. Without the place-name the letter has no addressee, which for a Pauline letter is an anomaly of the first order. This is a case where questions two and three converge rather than conflict, and §10.4 returns to it, because the same letter is attested in an independent line of transmission under a different name altogether.

**Mark 1:1.** The words υἱοῦ θεοῦ are bracketed in the critical text. They are omitted by the original hand of Sinaiticus, by Θ, by 28, and repeatedly by Origen; they are present in B, D, W, L and the Byzantine tradition. The mechanism is mechanical rather than doctrinal: in a manuscript using *nomina sacra*, the sequence runs ΙΥ ΧΥ ΥΥ ΘΥ — four contracted words in the genitive, every one of them ending in upsilon. Loss by eye-skip in copying, or addition to fill out a title, are equally easy. This one is genuinely undecided and is recorded as undecided.

Two cases are recorded here specifically as *not* established, because the temptation to bank them is strong and they will not bear it. **1 Thessalonians 2:14–16** — the passage blaming "the Jews" for killing Jesus and the prophets, with the wrath that has come upon them "εἰς τέλος" — is often called an interpolation on the strength of its tone, its collective indictment and its apparent reference to the events of 70 CE. But no manuscript omits it, and the balance of scholarly judgement retains it as Pauline. It is recorded as contested, and nothing in this thesis leans on it. **Hebrews** is not an interpolation problem at all, but it is an authorship problem that the manuscripts cannot touch: the work is anonymous, opens without a salutation, and at 2:3 places its author in the second generation — the message was confirmed to us **ὑπὸ τῶν ἀκουσάντων**, *by those who heard*. That sentence cannot be held together with Galatians 1:1 and 1:11–12, where Paul insists his gospel came from no human being. The manuscripts transmit Hebrews faithfully and say nothing whatever about who wrote it.

The class as a whole delivers the single most important methodological result of the audit. **The most consequential editorial work in the New Testament happened before the manuscripts we possess, and is therefore recoverable only by composition-internal argument.** That is a weaker instrument than an apparatus, and it must be used with correspondingly more caution — but declining to use it does not make the evidence go away. It only guarantees that the period in which most of the shaping occurred will be reported as a period in which nothing occurred.

## 5. The First Silent Mechanism: Translation

The audit was designed to test the security of the Greek New Testament. It found instead that a large fraction of the load-bearing christological argument does not depend on the Greek New Testament's security at all, because the argument was already carried by the time the New Testament authors picked up their sources. They read the Hebrew Bible in Greek, and the Greek says things the Hebrew does not say.

Three properties of this mechanism should be fixed before the evidence is presented, because each of them is routinely mishandled in the popular argument.

**It is not corruption.** The Septuagint translators were not producing a defective text. They were doing what translators do: rendering figurative, elliptical and idiomatic Hebrew into a language with different resources, and choosing, at each ambiguity, the sense that was available to them. Several of the divergences are demonstrably attempts to make sense of consonantal sequences that are genuinely difficult, and at least one — Psalm 22:17 — is now known to reflect a real Hebrew variant rather than a translator's guess.

**It has no christological motive, because it predates the dispute by centuries.** The Pentateuch was rendered into Greek in Alexandria in the third century BCE and the rest over the following two centuries, by Jewish translators for Jewish readers, in a setting where the questions that would later be argued about Jesus did not exist. Whatever one concludes about the direction of the divergences, no one can allege intent. This is the single most important property of the mechanism, and it is what makes the result in §5.2 worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as motivated reasoning at the source.

**It leaves no trace in the New Testament apparatus.** A New Testament author quoting the Septuagint accurately produces a quotation that is textually secure in every Greek manuscript. The divergence from the Hebrew is invisible unless one opens *Biblia Hebraica* alongside, and the argument about whether the New Testament has been tampered with never opens it. Every one of the nine loci below is stable in the Greek tradition. Not one of them is a variant.

### 5.1 Nine Places Where the Argument Lives in the Greek

**Psalm 40:7 at Hebrews 10:5.** The Hebrew reads אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי, *ears you have dug for me* — an idiom of opened hearing, of a servant made able to obey. The Septuagint reads σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι, *but a body you have prepared for me*. Hebrews quotes the Greek and then builds on the substituted word: the whole argument of 10:5–10 turns on the offering of the **body** of Jesus Christ once for all. The Hebrew supports an argument about obedience. The Greek supports an argument about incarnation. Whether ὠτία became σῶμα by inner-Greek corruption or by paraphrase is unresolved; what is not in doubt is that the epistle's conclusion is available only in the Greek.

**Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23.** The Hebrew is הָעַלְמָה — *the young woman*, with the definite article, a term for a girl of marriageable age carrying no statement about sexual status. Biblical Hebrew has a word that does carry that statement, בְּתוּלָה, and Isaiah does not use it here. The Septuagint renders ἡ παρθένος, which in Greek does. Matthew quotes the Greek. And the surrounding verses are as important as the word: Isaiah 7:15–16 attaches a deadline to the sign — before the child knows to refuse evil and choose good, the land of the two kings Ahaz fears will be abandoned — which places the fulfilment inside Ahaz's own lifetime, against two named contemporary monarchs.

**Psalm 19:5 at Romans 10:18.** The Hebrew has קַוָּם, *their measuring line*, going out through all the earth — the heavens as a surveyor's cord, silently laying out order. The Septuagint has ὁ φθόγγος αὐτῶν, *their sound*, most likely reading the consonants as קוֹלָם. Paul quotes the Greek to establish that Israel has heard and therefore has no excuse. A measuring line cannot be heard. The argument requires the noise.

**Psalm 16:10 at Acts 2:27 and 13:35.** The Hebrew has שַׁחַת, *the pit*, in parallel with שְׁאוֹל: the poem is a prayer not to be handed over to death. The Septuagint has διαφθορά, *decay*, most plausibly deriving the word from שׁחת, *to ruin*. Acts builds its central resurrection proof on the Greek noun — David died and saw corruption, therefore he was not speaking of himself, therefore he spoke of another whose flesh did not decay. **The argument cannot be made from the Hebrew**, where the psalm says only that the speaker will not die, which David plainly did.

**Isaiah 61:1 at Luke 4:18.** The Hebrew reads פְּקַח־קוֹחַ לַאֲסוּרִים, *the opening of the prison to those who are bound*. The Septuagint reads τυφλοῖς ἀνάβλεψιν, *recovery of sight to the blind*, having taken the doubled root of opening as opening of eyes. Luke quotes the Greek, and the restoration of sight enters the programmatic statement of the ministry from a phrase that in Hebrew is about release from confinement.

**Genesis 3:15.** The Hebrew זֶרַע is a masculine singular noun with collective sense, and the pronouns that follow agree with it grammatically — הוּא … תְּשׁוּפֶנּוּ — without implying an individual. The Septuagint's σπέρμα is **neuter**, so the grammatically expected pronoun is αὐτό. The Greek has **αὐτός**, masculine. A pronoun that does not agree with its antecedent in gender has stopped tracking the noun and started tracking a person, and the effect is to produce a single male descendant where the Hebrew has a line of offspring. (The later Latin *ipsa*, *she*, has no support in the Hebrew, the Greek or the Samaritan.)

**Amos 9:12 at Acts 15:17.** The Hebrew reads לְמַעַן יִירְשׁוּ אֶת־שְׁאֵרִית אֱדוֹם, *that they may possess the remnant of Edom* — a statement of territorial restoration. The Septuagint reads ὅπως ἐκζητήσωσιν οἱ κατάλοιποι τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *that the remnant of men may seek*, having read אדם as *adam*, humanity, rather than *Edom*, and יירשו, *possess*, as ידרשו, *seek*. Two consonantal readings, and a promise of conquest becomes a promise of Gentile inclusion. **James cites the Greek at the Jerusalem council, as the scriptural warrant for admitting Gentiles without circumcision.** The decision that made the movement a world religion rests on a verse that in Hebrew says the opposite kind of thing.

**Daniel 9:25–26.** The Hebrew distinguishes two figures: at v. 25 a מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד, an *anointed leader*, and at v. 26 a מָשִׁיחַ standing alone. It also divides the period, by the accent placed immediately after שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעָה: the text as pointed reads **seven weeks, and then sixty-two weeks**, not sixty-nine. The Old Greek of Daniel was judged so poor that the church replaced it with Theodotion; Theodotion has ἕως χριστοῦ ἡγουμένου at v. 25 but renders v. 26 **impersonally** — ἐξολοθρευθήσεται χρῖσμα, *the anointing shall be destroyed*, not *an anointed one shall be cut off*. Here the Greek moves away from an individual rather than toward one.

**Isaiah 9:5–6.** The Hebrew gives a four-part throne-name: פֶּלֶא יוֹעֵץ, אֵל גִּבּוֹר, אֲבִיעַד, שַׂר־שָׁלוֹם. The Septuagint **does not translate it at all**, substituting Μεγάλης βουλῆς ἄγγελος, *messenger of great counsel*. This too runs away from the concrete. Two further points belong to question one rather than question two: the verb וַיִּקְרָא is third masculine singular, so the naming is done *to* the child rather than by him about himself; and Israelite theophoric names routinely predicate something of God rather than of their bearer, as Elijah does not claim to be Yahweh.

### 5.2 The Uniform Direction

Set out together, the nine divergences show a direction:

| Hebrew | Greek | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| ears dug out | a **body** prepared | instrumental → physical |
| the young woman | the **virgin** | unmarked → marked |
| their measuring line | their **voice** | spatial → audible |
| the pit | **decay** | event → bodily process |
| the opening of the prison | **sight** to the blind | release → sensory restoration |
| seed, masculine collective | seed, neuter, with a **masculine** pronoun | corporate → individual |
| may possess Edom | may **seek** the Lord | territorial → religious |
| the anointed *leader*, then an anointed one | ἕως χριστοῦ; the anointing destroyed | personal → impersonal |
| Wonderful-Counsellor / Mighty-God / Father-of-Eternity / Prince-of-Peace | messenger of great counsel | concrete → abstract |

**Seven of the nine move the same way: from figurative, corporate, instrumental or spatial language toward concrete, singular, individual or physical language.** Two run the other way, and they are recorded as running the other way rather than filed under an exception: Daniel 9:26 in Theodotion becomes less personal, and Isaiah 9:5–6 in the Septuagint becomes less concrete.

It is worth noticing at once — and holding against the finding rather than in its favour — that **the two divergences running against the trend are precisely the two that no New Testament author cites.** Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9:5–6 are staples of the modern argument from prophecy and are absent from the New Testament altogether (§7). This is not a small caveat. If the sample of loci is defined as *the places where the christological argument lives*, and the New Testament authors selected the loci where the Greek gave them what they needed, then a uniform direction among the selected loci is partly an artefact of the selection. The correct statement of the result is therefore narrower than it first appears: **among the passages the argument actually uses, the Greek is more concrete than the Hebrew almost without exception — and the two counter-examples are unused.** Whether the trend holds across the Septuagint as a whole is a separate question that this audit did not ask and cannot answer.

With that stated, the finding still has force, because of the property established in §5: these renderings were produced by people with no stake in the outcome, several centuries before the outcome existed. Two readings of the direction are available, and the audit does not choose between them.

**Reading one — the ordinary explanation.** Figurative language concretises whenever it crosses a language boundary or a generation. A translator confronted with an idiom renders what the phrase *means to them*, and what it means to them is the nearer, the more graspable, the more picturable sense. Ears become a body because a body is what having ears is for; a measuring line becomes a voice because the psalm is about proclamation; a pit becomes decay because what one fears in a pit is what happens to the body in it. This requires no theory beyond ordinary translation practice, it is documented across every literary tradition that has been translated, and **it is fully sufficient to account for all nine cases**.

**Reading two — the stronger claim.** The same movement is the movement that the correspondential account describes as literalization: the conversion of a state into a place, a relation into a substance, a function into a thing. On that account, what the Hebrew carries is a correspondence, and what the Greek delivers is the correspondence resolved downward into the form the natural mind can hold. This reading is not ruled out by anything in the data. It is also not supported by anything the data can supply that reading one cannot, and it is subject to the same selection problem. It is recorded as untested.

The honest position is that the audit cannot decide between them, because they predict the same evidence at every point examined. What it can decide is stated in §5.3, and it is a structural fact rather than an interpretive one.

### 5.3 The Psalm 110 Chain: Reverence, Not Editing

One finding in this audit outweighs the rest, and it is not a variant, not an interpolation, and not attributable to anyone. It is a chain of five steps, each of them innocent, whose combined effect shaped the vocabulary in which the entire New Testament speaks of Jesus.

**Step one.** The Masoretes would not alter the consonants of the divine name and would not pronounce it. Their solution was the *qere perpetuum*: they left יהוה standing and placed on it the vowel points of אֲדֹנָי, producing the hybrid form יְהוָה — a spelling nobody ever said aloud, whose purpose is to instruct the reader to say a different word. This is reverence, exactly and only. No editorial intention is involved, and the same mechanism operates elsewhere in the text on words of no theological interest at all, such as הִוא and נַעֲרָה and יְרוּשָׁלַיִם.

**Step two.** The Greek translators therefore rendered the divine name as **κύριος** — the same word they already used for אֲדֹנָי and for its ordinary human counterpart אֲדֹנִי, "my lord," a term of address for any superior. Hebrew distinguishes these lexically. Greek does not distinguish them at all.

**Step three.** At Psalm 110:1 the Hebrew reads נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי — an oracle of *Yahweh* to *my lord*, two lexically distinct words denoting two plainly distinct parties, the second of them a human superior. The Septuagint renders both with the same word: **εἶπεν ὁ κύριος τῷ κυρίῳ μου**. One Greek word, twice, for two different Hebrew words with two different referents.

**Step four.** Matthew 22:44 and Mark 12:36 quote the Greek, and quote it accurately, and build the argument on it. In Mark 12:36 the two occurrences of κύριος stand side by side in a single sentence with two different referents, and the puzzle Jesus poses — how can the Messiah be David's son if David calls him lord — is a puzzle that lives in that sentence.

**Step five.** κύριος is the standing New Testament address for Jesus, in every stratum of the corpus, from the Aramaic acclamation preserved at 1 Corinthians 16:22 to the latest of the Pastorals.

**The result: a distinction the Hebrew makes lexically is invisible in Greek, and the entire New Testament is in Greek.** This is not a scribal alteration, not a doctrinal insertion, and not a variant that any apparatus can display. It is a translation effect whose first cause is a Jewish scribal practice adopted out of reverence for the divine name, and it removes at a stroke the ability of a Greek reader to see, at any given occurrence of κύριος, which Hebrew word stands behind it. Whatever else is true, no participant in the later christological argument had access to the distinction that the Hebrew text preserves. The collapse begins in Jewish reverence, and it happens before there is anything to be reverent or irreverent about.

## 6. The Second Silent Mechanism: Selection

The translation layer supplies a bilingual environment in which two authoritative forms of the same book say different things. The second mechanism is what the New Testament authors did with that environment. They moved between the Hebrew and the Greek — not at random, and not consistently, but **according to which form yielded the reading the argument required**. Like translation, this leaves no variants: the resulting quotation is transmitted faithfully by every manuscript. Unlike translation, it is a decision made by the New Testament author himself, and it is visible only when both source forms are placed beside the citation.

The clearest demonstration is a single author moving in both directions within two chapters. **At Matthew 1:23 the Hebrew of Isaiah 7:14 would have blocked the application, so the citation follows the Greek** — הָעַלְמָה would have given a young woman where the argument needs a virgin. **At Matthew 2:15 the Greek of Hosea 11:1 would have blocked the application, so the citation follows the Hebrew.** Hosea's Hebrew reads מִמִּצְרַיִם קָרָאתִי לִבְנִי, *out of Egypt I called my son*, and the son is unambiguously the nation, as Exodus 4:22 states outright; it is a statement about the past, not a prediction. The Septuagint pluralised it to τὰ τέκνα αὐτοῦ, *his children*, which removes the singular the argument needs. Matthew bypasses the Greek and renders the Hebrew to recover τὸν υἱόν μου. The same hand, twenty verses apart, going to whichever text supplies a singular individual.

**John does the same at 19:37.** The Hebrew of Zechariah 12:10 is secure and reads דָּקָרוּ, *they pierced*. The Septuagint has ἀνθ' ὧν κατωρχήσαντο — *because they danced mockingly* — a rendering produced by reading the consonants as רקדו, a straightforward confusion of ד and ר, which in most Hebrew scripts differ by the length of one stroke. The Greek is unusable for a crucifixion citation. John renders the Hebrew, ἐξεκέντησαν, and makes one further change: the Hebrew has אֵלַי, *they shall look upon **me***, and John writes εἰς ὅν, *upon **him** whom they pierced*.

Beyond the choice of source form, the same operation appears as choice *within* a text, and four instances are worth setting out because they are the cases where a citation most visibly shapes the narrative it supports.

**Micah 5:1–2 at Matthew 2:6, reversed.** The Hebrew calls Bethlehem צָעִיר, *little*, among the clans of Judah; the Septuagint intensifies to ὀλιγοστός, *very small*. Matthew writes **οὐδαμῶς ἐλαχίστη — "by no means least."** The citation states the opposite of both source forms. Two further operations are stacked on it: אַלְפֵי, *clans*, is read as though pointed אַלּוּפֵי, *chieftains*, giving ἡγεμόσιν, *rulers*; and the closing clause, "who will shepherd my people Israel," is not from Micah at all but spliced in from 2 Samuel 5:2, where it is said of David.

**Jeremiah 31:15 at Matthew 2:18.** Rachel weeps in **Ramah**, a town in Benjamin, north of Jerusalem; the massacre is at Bethlehem, in Judah, to the south. The two places are not the same place and the verse is not about either event. The link runs through Genesis 35:19, which locates Rachel's tomb near Bethlehem — that is, through an association with the *figure* of Rachel, not through anything the cited verse says.

**Matthew 2:23 cites a text that does not exist.** "He shall be called a Nazarene" corresponds to no sentence in any book of the Hebrew Bible, in any known Greek form, or in the Samaritan text. Matthew's citation formula is itself unusual: **διὰ τῶν προφητῶν**, plural and unnamed, where his other formulae name a prophet. The plausible candidates are consonantal: נֵצֶר, *branch*, at Isaiah 11:1, or נְזִיר, *nazirite*, at Judges 13:5 and 13:7, which the Greek renders ναζιραῖος. Either would be a play on sound rather than a quotation.

**Zechariah 9:9 at Matthew 21:2–7.** The Hebrew is synonymous parallelism, one animal named twice: *on a donkey, and on a colt, the foal of a she-ass*. The Septuagint preserves two nouns, ὑποζύγιον καὶ πῶλον νέον, because that is what the Hebrew has on the surface. Mark, Luke and John each have one animal. Matthew sends for two, has cloaks placed on both, and has him sit **ἐπάνω αὐτῶν** — *on them*, plural. This is the clearest single instance in the New Testament of a narrative detail generated by the reading of a text rather than the other way round.

To these should be added the operation Luke performs at 4:18–19, which combines all three of the mechanisms in this thesis in three moves within two verses. He quotes Isaiah 61 in the Septuagint form, which had already converted the opening of the prison into sight for the blind (§5.1). He **splices in** a clause from a different chapter — ἀποστεῖλαι τεθραυσμένους ἐν ἀφέσει, from Isaiah 58:6. And he **stops mid-verse**, ending at "the acceptable year of the Lord" and omitting the words that follow in both Hebrew and Greek: *and the day of vengeance of our God*. The programmatic statement of the ministry is a composite, and what it leaves out is as deliberate as what it includes.

One further case belongs in this section because it is habitually mis-filed as a scribal error. **Matthew 27:9 attributes a quotation of Zechariah 11:12–13 to Jeremiah**, and ℵ, B, A, C and W all read Jeremiah. The attribution is the author's, not a copyist's; the late minuscules 33 and 1579 and some Syriac and Persian witnesses that drop the name are performing the correction. The Hebrew of the passage has הַיּוֹצֵר, *the potter*, sitting one consonant away from הָאוֹצָר, *the treasury*, and the Septuagint read a third thing again, χωνευτήριον, *the foundry*. An evangelist composing from a memory of prophetic material, not from an open scroll, is the explanation the evidence supports.

## 7. The Third Silent Mechanism: Later Reading

The third mechanism is the simplest and the easiest to overlook: **passages that carry heavy weight in the modern argument and are not used by the New Testament at all.** No corruption is involved, and no author is responsible. The distance is created entirely downstream, by readers assembling a case their sources did not assemble.

**Daniel 9:24–27** is the standard chronological proof of the modern apologetic — the seventy weeks calculated forward to a dated crucifixion. **No New Testament author cites it.** Question one is decisive against the calculation in any case. The Masoretic accentuation places an *atnach*, the major verse-dividing accent, immediately after שָׁבֻעִים שִׁבְעָה, dividing the period into **seven weeks and then sixty-two weeks**; the figure of sixty-nine, on which every version of the calculation depends, requires disregarding that accent. The Hebrew also has **two anointed figures**, not one: a מָשִׁיחַ נָגִיד at v. 25 and a מָשִׁיחַ at v. 26. And the Greek tradition, as noted in §5.1, renders the second impersonally.

**Isaiah 9:5–6** is the standard christological proof-text of the modern argument, and it too is **cited by no New Testament author**. The Septuagint does not translate the throne-name at all. Whatever the verse is doing, the first Christian writers did not do it with this verse.

**Psalm 22:17** is a third case, of a different kind. The modern argument treats "they pierced my hands and my feet" as a prophecy of crucifixion and often alleges that the Masoretes altered the text; the Hebrew reads כָּאֲרִי, *like a lion*, with **no verb at all**, and the Septuagint (Psalm 21:17) reads ὤρυξαν, *they dug*. The discovery at Nahal Hever of a psalms scroll from the late first century CE reading כארו, with a final *waw*, establishes that a Hebrew verbal reading existed in the first century and that the Greek translator was rendering a real variant rather than inventing one. It does **not** establish that the Masoretic reading is a later alteration; it establishes that both readings are ancient. And the point that closes the case is that **the New Testament never cites this clause**, in either form, at any of the four crucifixion narratives.

A fourth case shows the mechanism operating within the transmission of a translation rather than within an argument. At **Genesis 3:15** the Latin tradition produced *ipsa conteret*, *she shall crush* — a reading with no support in Hebrew, Greek or Samaritan, and one that carried a substantial weight of later devotional and doctrinal reflection. It is a later reading in the most literal sense: not a variant in the original language, not a choice by a biblical author, but a form that arose in a daughter version and was then read as though it were the text.

The class matters out of proportion to its size, because it locates a large part of the modern dispute outside the sources entirely. When the argument from prophecy is conducted over Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9, it is not being conducted over material the New Testament used. It is being conducted over material assembled later, from a translation, by readers whose case required texts their predecessors had not needed.

## 8. The Samaritan Pentateuch as Control Specimen

Everything in §§4–7 depends on inference. The compositional seams, the direction of translation, the authors' movement between source forms — none of it can be watched happening. This is the standing weakness of the whole enterprise, and it is the reason the Samaritan Pentateuch belongs in this thesis even though it says nothing about Jesus. **It is a control specimen: a complete, physically surviving, independently transmitted second edition of the Torah, whose editing can be observed rather than reconstructed.**

The scale of divergence is often overstated. Emanuel Tov's count of roughly 7,000 variants against the Masoretic Text is the figure usually quoted, but the great majority are orthographic. Abraham Tal and Moshe Florentin's analysis puts roughly **4,200 as substantial** and about 1,300 as matters of spelling and grammar. Agreements with the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text number fewer than a thousand, of which perhaps five hundred look like a genuinely shared Hebrew *Vorlage* rather than independent convergence — which is itself a significant datum, since it means a Hebrew textual tradition standing behind the Greek existed alongside the one the Masoretes transmitted.

Sorted by type, the substantial variants fall into four classes, and it is the classes rather than the count that make the specimen valuable.

**Ideological.** These are few and they are exactly where one would predict. At **Deuteronomy 27:4** the Masoretic Text has the altar built on Mount Ebal and the Samaritan has Gerizim. Throughout the centralisation formula the Masoretic imperfect יִבְחַר, *the place the Lord **will** choose*, appears in the Samaritan as the perfect בָּחַר, *has chosen* — converting an open future into an accomplished past that can only refer to Gerizim. And the Samaritan Decalogue carries a tenth commandment naming Gerizim, compiled out of Deuteronomy 11:29–30 and 27:2–7. *The direction of the Ebal/Gerizim variant is disputed and is recorded here as disputed: the Samaritan reading has ancient support, and the claim that either side altered the other requires evidence this audit did not gather.*

**Modernisation.** The Samaritan is a *Vulgärtext*: archaic and difficult forms are replaced with contemporary ones, unusual grammar is regularised, and awkward expressions are smoothed. Genesis 13:6 and Genesis 24:55 are standard examples. Nothing doctrinal is at stake; a scribal tradition is simply making its text readable.

**Small harmonisations.** At **Genesis 4:8** the Masoretic Text has Cain say something to Abel and then breaks off without reporting it; the Samaritan supplies the words, נלכה השדה, *let us go to the field*. At **Genesis 2:2** the Masoretic Text has God finish his work on the seventh day, which sits awkwardly with resting on it; the Samaritan moves the completion to the sixth.

**Large block expansions.** There are **exactly thirty-five** of these, and they are the most instructive class of all. A passage from Deuteronomy is imported bodily into Exodus so that the narrative reports the command being given as well as recalled — Deuteronomy 18:18–22, the promise of a prophet like Moses, is inserted into Exodus 20:21; Deuteronomy 9:20 is inserted into Exodus 32.

What the specimen settles is a question about priors, and it settles it in a direction that lowers the temperature of the entire discussion. Gap-filling, contradiction-resolution, ambiguity-closing and the relocation of blocks of text are simply **what scribal traditions do when they have room to do it**. The Samaritan tradition did all four, demonstrably, to a text it regarded as maximally sacred, without any of it constituting deception. This raises the prior considerably on the New Testament seams identified in §4 — a text with two endings and an editorial "we" is behaving exactly as a text in an active tradition behaves — while removing most of the drama from the finding. **Filling a gap is not a conspiracy. It is a habit.** The same conclusion is reached from an entirely different direction in [*No Original Exists*](No%20Original%20Exists_%20The%20Universal%20Reconstruction%20of%20Biblical%20Texts%20and%20the%20Forensic%20Recovery%20of%20Proto-Luke.md), which argues that reconstruction from later witnesses is the universal condition of biblical scholarship rather than a special embarrassment of any one corpus.

## 9. The Direction of Drift: From Function to Title

The preceding sections have reported four bodies of evidence — manuscript variants, compositional seams, translation divergences, and citation practice. This section reports what they have in common, which is a direction.

The direction is not the one the popular argument predicts. On the sceptical account the movement should be from a low christology to a high one, and the evidence should consist of scribes progressively elevating a human teacher. That is not what the material shows. The one place where a genuine elevation is caught in the act — 1 Timothy 3:16 — has a companion at John 1:18 where the *earliest* witnesses carry the higher reading and the later tradition lowers it; and both movements have the same shape when described properly. In both cases the tradition moved **away from language that points through a figure and toward language that lands on him.** ὅς, "who was manifested," is a relative clause about manifestation with no stated antecedent — hard to hold, and easy to stabilise by supplying a subject. μονογενὴς θεός is a phrase whose second word will not sit still in any available category — hard to hold, and easy to stabilise by exchanging it for the familiar υἱός. The direction of each change is toward whichever settled category the scribe already possessed. **The drift is not from low to high. It is from correspondential to literal.**

### 9.1 The Titles Are Shared

The premise underneath the identity-statement reading is that the christological titles are proper to one figure. In the biblical text they are not. Every one of them is held by someone else, in the same books, often in the same idiom.

| Title | Others who hold it, in the biblical text |
|---|---|
| Son of God | **Adam** (Luke 3:38); Israel corporately (Romans 9:4, υἱοθεσία); peacemakers (Matthew 5:9); all led by the Spirit (Romans 8:14, Galatians 3:26); the heavenly host in the Septuagint; the emperor, as *divi filius*, on coinage in circulation while the Gospels were written |
| Son of Man | Daniel 7:13 has "one **like** a son of man" — a comparison, not a designation — and Daniel 7:27 glosses the figure corporately as "the people of the holy ones of the Most High" |
| κύριος | "Sir" from the Samaritan woman (John 4:11); Philip, addressed by Greeks (John 12:21); a father, by his son (Matthew 21:30); any estate owner (Matthew 20:8, Colossians 4:1) |
| χριστός | Anointed priests (Leviticus 4:5); Saul, David and Solomon; and **Cyrus the Persian**, at Isaiah 45:1, τῷ χριστῷ μου Κύρῳ — a Gentile king, called the Lord's anointed |
| παῖς | David (Acts 4:25) and Israel (Luke 1:54) — the same word, in the same two books, as Acts 3:13 and 3:26 |
| μονογενής | **Isaac** (Hebrews 11:17) |
| The prophet like Moses | The prophetic succession generally; John the Baptist is asked whether he is the figure and says no (John 1:21) |
| θεός | **Satan** (2 Corinthians 4:4); **the belly** (Philippians 3:19); human judges (John 10:34, citing Psalm 82:6); pagan gods (Acts 14:11, 19:26); **Paul, by the islanders at Malta** (Acts 28:6); Herod Agrippa (Acts 12:22); and "many gods and many lords" acknowledged as a fact of the world (1 Corinthians 8:5) |

Two of these deserve to be drawn out, because they are cases where an English rendering has closed a question the Greek leaves open.

**μονογενής does not mean "only-begotten."** The word is formed from **γένος**, *kind* or *class*, not from γεννάω, *to beget*; it means *one of a kind*, *unique in its relation*. The corpus proves this against itself at **Hebrews 11:17**, where Isaac is called Abraham's μονογενής although Abraham had Ishmael before him and further sons by Keturah after. "Only-begotten" enters through the Latin *unigenitus* and from there into the creeds and the English versions. This bears directly on John 1:18: μονογενὴς θεός is not a statement about generation but about **uniqueness of relation** — which is precisely what the remainder of the verse says, ὁ ὢν εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρὸς ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο, *the one who is in the bosom of the Father, that one has made him known*.

**παῖς is the Servant word.** Where Acts uses it of Jesus — 3:13, 3:26, 4:27, 4:30 — it is the Septuagint's word for the servant of Isaiah 42, 52 and 53, and the same book applies it to David and Luke applies it to Israel. It designates a **commissioned function**, someone appointed to a task by another, and the English "servant" or "child" both lose the point.

The correct conclusion from this table is narrow, and it is worth stating the boundary explicitly because it is easily overrun. Establishing that a title *can* be held by others tells us what the word permits. **It does not tell us what any given author meant by it.** A word with a wide range can still be used at full strength. What the table forecloses is only the argument that the title by itself settles the question — which is the argument most often made.

### 9.2 John 10:34: The Defence Is About Reception

One passage does what the table cannot: it records the argument being had, and it records the answer.

At John 10:33 the opponents state the charge in reported speech — ὅτι σὺ ἄνθρωπος ὢν ποιεῖς σεαυτὸν θεόν, *because you, being a man, make yourself God*. The identical construction appears at 5:18, likewise in the narrator's report of what opponents concluded. Both verses are textually secure, and both are frequently cited as evidence for what Jesus claimed. What they are evidence for is what his opponents took him to have claimed.

The reply is the datum. He quotes Psalm 82:6 — "I said, you are gods" — and gives the ground on which the word was applicable there: **πρὸς οὓς ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ἐγένετο**, *those to whom the word of God came*. Not those who were divine. Not those who claimed anything. **Those who received.** The defence offered against a charge of self-deification is an argument about reception, and it is a defence that would be unavailable to anyone whose position was that he was making an identity claim about himself.

This is where the two readings of the material separate most sharply, and where the identity frame runs out of moves. Read as an identity statement, the reply is a piece of rabbinic evasion — a lesser sense of θεός produced to deflect a charge the speaker means in a greater sense. Read as it stands, it is a straightforward statement of the actual position: the word applies to those through whom the word of God comes, and the objection is a category error made by hearers who have converted "the Father in me" into "making himself equal to God." **The text dramatises the literalising conversion**, twice, and gives the answer to it once. Whether one accepts that answer is a separate matter; that the text contains it is not in dispute, and no manuscript varies at either point.

### 9.3 John 1:34 and the Exchange of a Relation for an Identity

The clearest single instance of the drift is a variant that is not usually discussed as christological at all.

At John 1:34 the Baptist's testimony reads either οὗτός ἐστιν **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ**, *this is the Chosen One of God*, or οὗτός ἐστιν **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ**, *this is the Son of God*. The first is carried by the original hand of Sinaiticus, by **P5 in the third century**, by the Old Latin, by the Curetonian Syriac and by the Sahidic Coptic — a geographically dispersed early attestation. The second is the reading of the later tradition and of the printed text.

The point is not that a "low" reading was replaced by a "high" one, and that description should be refused. **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ is not a low designation.** It is the Servant language of Isaiah 42:1, and it makes a very strong claim. What it does not do is state an identity. It states a *relation* — chosen, by someone, for something — and it therefore points through the figure to the one who chose him. ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, by the time the exchange was made, was a fixed title of the creeds and points at the figure himself.

Four other passages in the corpus show the same exchange in different mechanisms, and they should not be lumped together because the mechanisms are genuinely different:

| Passage | The harder, earlier form | What replaced it | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 1:41 | **ὀργισθείς**, *moved with anger* (D, Old Latin a d ff2 r1) | σπλαγχνισθείς, *moved with compassion* | Scribal alteration: no one changes compassion into anger |
| John 1:34 | **ὁ ἐκλεκτὸς τοῦ θεοῦ** (ℵ*, P5, Old Latin, Curetonian, Sahidic) | ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ | Scribal alteration toward a creedal title |
| Mark 13:32 → Matthew 24:36 | **οὐδὲ ὁ υἱός**, *nor the Son* (retained in ℵ, B, D, Θ, f13, 33) | deleted in much of the Matthean tradition | Evangelist redaction, then further harmonisation |
| Mark 6:5 | **οὐκ ἐδύνατο**, *he could not* (all Greek) | *noluit*, *he would not* | Versional softening in the Old Latin only |
| Mark 10:18 → Matthew 19:17 | "Why do you call me good?" | "Why do you ask me about the good?" | Evangelist redaction — an author handling a difficult source |

The distinction in the right-hand column matters. Matthew rewriting Mark is **not corruption**: it is an author working with a source he finds theologically awkward, which is an ordinary compositional act and one his readers would have expected. What happens afterwards is a third thing again — Byzantine scribes pulling Matthew's wording back toward Mark's, undoing the evangelist's own change. Three processes, three different sets of hands, and the same net direction.

### 9.4 The Corpus Distribution

The last observation is distributional, and it is the one that connects the textual findings to the framework prediction stated in §2.1.

Sort the christological passages by *how the weight is carried* rather than by *what is concluded*, and the corpus separates into three groups:

| Corpus | How christological weight is carried | Where the disputes land |
|---|---|---|
| Johannine | Image, radiance, bosom, "has made him known," "the Father in me," neuter unity, anarthrous predicate before the verb | Grammar and lexical range; almost no textual variants |
| Undisputed Pauline | Function and office — ὤφθη, ὁρισθέντος, appointed, raised, sent, image of the invisible God | Lexical range; text secure |
| Pastorals, 2 Peter, Jude | **Stacked titles** — "our great God and Savior," "the only Master and Lord," "the God and Savior Jesus Christ" | Granville Sharp, punctuation, article placement, and the textual variants |

**Every Granville Sharp dispute and every comma dispute in the New Testament is in the epistolary corpus. Not one is Johannine.** Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, 2 Thessalonians 1:12, Jude 4, Ephesians 5:5, Romans 9:5 — the whole class turns on whether one article governs two nouns, or on where a comma is placed in manuscripts that have no punctuation. And the textual corruptions cluster in the same layer: 1 Timothy 3:16 is a Pastoral; Jude 4 gains θεόν in P72, Ψ and the Byzantine tradition where ℵ, A, B, C and 33 omit it; Philippians 2:6 gains the article τῷ before θεῷ in F and G, ninth-century manuscripts, against P46, ℵ, A, B and the Byzantine text.

This is a real pattern in the audit's data, and it is consistent with the prediction stated in §2.1: through-language where the composition is correspondential, title-predication where it is not, and the disputes concentrated in the title-stacking layer. But **it is not yet a test**, and it must not be presented as one. The sample is defined by the argument — these are the passages people fight about — and passages people fight about are, by construction, passages where the wording is decidable in more than one way. A genuine test requires the fourth step, which this audit did not perform: define the corpora in advance, define the measure (through-language against title-predication), state the prediction, and then apply it **to the passages nobody argues about**. Until that is done, what is reported here is a distribution, not a result.

The relation of the Johannine material to the Jamesian tradition, and the compositional stratum from which its through-language may derive, are treated in [*The Enochic Thread in the Johannine Corpus*](The%20Enochic%20Thread%20in%20the%20Johannine%20Corpus_%20Testing%20the%20Jamesian%20Origin%20Hypothesis.md) and [*The Correspondential Architecture of the Four Gospels*](The%20Correspondential%20Architecture%20of%20the%20Four%20Gospels_%20A%20Compositional%20Analysis%20Through%20the%20Lens%20of%20Ontological%20Correspondence.md); the linguistic distinctness of the Pastorals is documented independently in [*The Pastoral Epistles in Modern Scholarship*](The%20Pastoral%20Epistles%20in%20Modern%20Scholarship_%20A%20Critical%20Validation%20of%20Authorship%20and%20Dating%20Consensus.md).

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What the Manuscript Tradition Contributes

The manuscript tradition contributes less than either side of the public argument requires, and what it does contribute is more interesting than what is claimed for it.

It contributes **one clean forgery**. The Comma Johanneum has no ancient Greek existence, entered the Greek tradition by translation from Latin, and made its printed debut in the sixteenth century. It contributes **one decisive alteration**, at 1 Timothy 3:16, where a relative pronoun became the word *God* by the addition of a crossbar and an overline, and where the direction of change is observable in the correctors' hands rather than inferred. It contributes a handful of well-attested expansions — the Longer Ending of Mark, the woman taken in adultery, the fifteen verses of the Textus Receptus — none of which is doctrinal in the sense the argument needs. And it contributes a quantity of ordinary noise.

What it does not contribute is a programme. The evidence against a programme is not an absence of evidence but positive counter-evidence: the correctors of Sinaiticus working in opposite directions at two passages a page apart; the earliest witnesses at John 1:18 carrying the reading a programme would have to call late; the modern editors moving Jude 5 *toward* the loaded reading; and, most tellingly, the fact that the class of sayings with the strongest conceivable motive for intervention was left largely alone. Mark 9:1 and 13:30 and Matthew 10:23 promise the end within a living generation, in a document copied for centuries after that generation died. Mark 13:32 has the Son not knowing the day. Mark 10:18 has him decline the word *good*. Mark 6:5 has him unable, not unwilling. **Scribes who were systematically improving the christology had these to hand and did not take them.** That single observation constrains the sceptical reconstruction more tightly than any amount of argument about individual variants.

The corollary for the apologetic side is equally firm. A tradition this stable did not need improving, and it did not deliver what is claimed from it. The high christological passages are secure — and secure in wording that, read as it stands rather than through the categories of the fourth century, says something other than what is read off it.

### 10.2 What the Translation Layer Contributes

The translation layer contributes most of the distance, and it does so under three conditions that make it uniquely difficult to argue about.

It is **invisible to the instrument**. No apparatus in either testament records it, because it is not a variant in either language. It appears only when the two editions are opened simultaneously, and the popular argument, conducted in English, never opens either.

It is **innocent of motive**. Alexandrian Jewish translators in the third and second centuries BCE were not preparing evidence. Whatever direction their renderings took, no one can allege intention, and this removes the usual explanatory resource on both sides. The apologist cannot say the divergence is a Christian improvement; the sceptic cannot say it is a Christian invention.

It is **load-bearing out of all proportion to its visibility**. The incarnational argument of Hebrews 10 requires σῶμα, which is not in the Hebrew. The resurrection argument of Acts 2 and 13 requires διαφθορά, which is not in the Hebrew. The Gentile mission is authorised at the Jerusalem council from a verse that in Hebrew promises the possession of Edom. The virgin conception requires παρθένος, where Isaiah wrote עַלְמָה. Remove the Septuagint and four of the central claims of the New Testament lose the texts they are built on — not because the claims are refuted, but because the scriptural warrant offered for them is warrant that exists only in Greek.

And beneath all of it lies the chain of §5.3, in which a scribal practice adopted out of reverence for the divine name produces a Greek Bible in which two lexically distinct Hebrew words are one word, and in which the entire New Testament then addresses Jesus with that word.

### 10.3 Implications for the Framework Hypothesis

The framework hypothesis under test in this library holds that correspondential compositions, received by communities that do not hold the key, undergo literalization: dynamic states become static entities, interior qualities become exterior persons, relations become substances. [*Reversing the Arrow*](../08_Correspondential_Texts/Reversing%20the%20Arrow_%20'Gnosticism'%20as%20the%20Downstream%20Literalization%20of%20Correspondential%20Composition.md) argues that this operation is universal rather than the property of any one movement, and [*The Literalized Fall*](../08_Correspondential_Texts/The%20Literalized%20Fall_%20The%20Sethian%20Corpus%20as%20Correspondential%20Architecture%20Without%20the%20Key.md) traces it through a corpus where the correspondential original and the literalized reception can both be examined.

This audit bears on that hypothesis in a specific and limited way, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not do.

**It does not confirm the hypothesis.** No finding here requires a correspondential substrate to explain it. Every case is fully explicable by ordinary translation practice, ordinary editorial habit, and the ordinary tendency of readers to render figurative language into the nearest graspable sense.

**It does not damage the hypothesis either**, and this is not a trivial result. The strongest available objection to a correspondential reading of the christological material was that the phenomenon had already been explained without remainder: a Galilean teacher was elevated by scribes, and the drift from modest to exalted language is exactly what the manuscripts record. **That objection does not survive the audit.** The manuscripts do not record a systematic elevation; they record a small, mixed, bidirectional set of interventions inside a tradition that left the most embarrassing material intact. The drift that *is* uniform is a different drift, running in a different dimension — figurative to literal, not low to high — and it is produced predominantly by mechanisms that predate the christological dispute and have no stake in it.

**What it establishes is a coincidence of direction across independent hands.** Alexandrian translators with no christology, evangelists with one, and later scribes with a different one again all moved the same way: toward the concrete, the singular, the individual, the picturable. That is what the literalization hypothesis predicts, and it is also what the ordinary account of how metaphor ages predicts. The two descriptions are not distinguished by anything in this evidence. Recording that plainly is more useful than resolving it prematurely, because the distinguishing test exists and can be run: §9.4 states it, and it requires examining the passages nobody argues about, where the selection effect that contaminates the present sample does not operate.

### 10.4 The Problem of Direction in the Marcionite Witness

One question raised repeatedly by the audit could not be answered from the primary editions and is answered here from the library's own established position, which rests on evidence of a different kind.

The Marcionite *Evangelion* is shorter than canonical Luke. It lacked the infancy narrative entirely — Tertullian's summary in *Adversus Marcionem* IV runs from the fifteenth year of Tiberius directly to Capernaum, and Epiphanius, in *Panarion* 42, itemises what is missing: the preface, Elizabeth and Zacharias, the annunciation, Bethlehem, and also the baptism and the genealogy at 3:21–38. On Roth's grading the omission is **secure**; the wording of the splice from 3:1 to 4:31 is graded lower. Its Pauline collection, the *Apostolikon*, contained ten letters, and two facts about that list matter here: the letter the canon calls Ephesians was titled **πρὸς Λαοδικέας**, attested independently by Tertullian and Epiphanius — converging with the manuscript evidence at Ephesians 1:1 discussed in §4 — and the Pastorals were absent, along with Hebrews, which are precisely the letters that stand out most sharply on linguistic grounds.

The traditional account holds that Marcion cut. That account descends entirely from his theological opponents, and it has never been independently verified. The library's established position, set out in [*No Original Exists*](No%20Original%20Exists_%20The%20Universal%20Reconstruction%20of%20Biblical%20Texts%20and%20the%20Forensic%20Recovery%20of%20Proto-Luke.md), follows the New Marcionite Paradigm of Matthias Klinghardt, Jason BeDuhn and Markus Vinzent, and the decisive evidence is **editorial fatigue**: comparison of canonical Luke with the reconstructed Marcionite text shows the fatigue signature — an author beginning with a change and lapsing back into the source — in *canonical Luke*, not in the shorter text. The direction of dependence therefore runs from the shorter form to the longer. **The canonical author is expanding, not the heretic abridging.**

Two consequences follow for this thesis. First, the physicalising material in Luke 24 discussed in §3.4 and §4 sits inside a text that the independent line of transmission attests in reduced form, which strengthens the compositional case without depending on the Western Non-Interpolations. Second, and more important methodologically, the Marcionite witness is a **manuscript-class witness rather than an editor** — a second attestation of a Lukan text, transmitted outside the catholic tradition and preserved only in the citations of its enemies. The distinction between the text Marcion transmitted and the theology Marcion built on it is developed in [*Dancing with Fire*](../05_The_Self/Dancing%20with%20Fire_%20Marcion%20of%20Sinope%20and%20the%20Correspondential%20Key%20He%20Never%20Possessed.md).

### 10.5 Limitations and Lacunae

The following are limitations of this audit, stated without mitigation.

**The sample is defined by the argument.** Every passage examined is a passage someone contests. Passages become contested when their wording is decidable in more than one way, so a sample of contested passages is systematically enriched in ambiguity. The distributional finding of §9.4 is the most affected: it may reflect where arguments happen rather than how corpora are composed. This is the single largest weakness and it is not repairable within the present design.

**The two counter-examples in the translation set are the two the New Testament does not use.** As stated in §5.2, the uniform direction among the nine loci is partly an artefact of the New Testament authors having chosen the loci where the Greek helped them. The trend across the Septuagint as a whole was not measured.

**Reading one and reading two are not distinguished by any evidence gathered here.** The ordinary account of how figurative language concretises is sufficient for every case, and the correspondential account adds nothing this evidence can detect. The audit records the tie rather than breaking it.

**The falsifiability step was not performed.** The through-language measure of §9.4 was applied to the disputed passages only. Until it is applied to whole texts including the passages nobody argues about, and to a control corpus, the corpus distribution is a description and not a test.

**Several matters remain untraced** and are marked as such rather than filled in: the chronology of the Quirinius census, which requires Josephus and the Roman administrative record; the circumstances of the Erasmus undertaking and the production of Montfortianus; the direction of the Ebal/Gerizim variant at Deuteronomy 27:4; the bearing of 4QDeut-q on the Deuteronomy 32:43 line quoted at Hebrews 1:6; whether Romans 1:3–4 is a pre-Pauline formula; and whether Luke 1–2 was removed from the Marcionite text or never present in its exemplar.

**Two passages are recorded as contested and are not relied on anywhere**: 1 Thessalonians 2:14–16, which the balance of scholarship retains as Pauline, and Mark 1:1, where the *nomina sacra* sequence makes loss and addition equally easy.

**Finally, the audit does not settle the question it is nearest to.** Nothing here establishes whether Jesus was divine, and nothing here was designed to. Question one, two and three are questions about texts. What a text is *about* is a further question, and it is not answered by establishing what the text says.

## 11. Conclusion: Three Mechanisms, One Direction

The argument about what the New Testament says concerning Jesus is conducted over the apparatus, and the apparatus is not where the evidence is.

What the apparatus can see, this audit found to be narrow and largely undramatic. One clean forgery, with no ancient Greek existence and a defective first witness. One decisive alteration, produced by a crossbar inside a circle, and caught in the act because the three oldest manuscripts read *who* in their first hand and were all corrected to *God* by later ones. Fifteen absent verses that turn out to be harmonisations, a duplication, four glosses and two liturgical formulae, not one of them doctrinal. Eight Western Non-Interpolations whose treatment reversed when a third-century papyrus appeared — the standing demonstration that the discipline corrects itself against its own founders. And, running through all of it, a set of interventions too small, too mixed and too bidirectional to constitute a programme, in a tradition that left "nor the Son," "why do you call me good," "he could not," and the promise of an end within a living generation exactly where it found them.

What the apparatus cannot see turned out to carry most of the weight. **Translation**: nine loci where the argument lives in the Greek and cannot be made from the Hebrew, produced by Jewish translators in Alexandria centuries before the dispute existed — a body prepared where the Hebrew has ears dug out, decay where it has a pit, a virgin where it has a young woman, the seeking of God where it has the possession of Edom. **Selection**: New Testament authors moving between the two forms according to which yielded what the argument required, to the Greek at Isaiah 7:14 and to the Hebrew at Hosea 11:1, twenty verses apart in the same gospel. **Later reading**: an argument from prophecy resting on Daniel 9 and Isaiah 9:5–6, which no New Testament author cites, and on a clause of Psalm 22 that the crucifixion narratives never quote. None of these leaves a variant. All of them are in the secure text.

And beneath the three mechanisms lies a single structural fact that outweighs every individual finding. The Masoretes would not alter the consonants of the divine name, so they pointed it with the vowels of another word. The Greek therefore rendered both with κύριος. At Psalm 110:1 two lexically distinct Hebrew words became one Greek word twice. Matthew and Mark quote the Greek. And κύριος is the standing New Testament address for Jesus. **A distinction the Hebrew makes lexically is invisible in Greek, and the entire New Testament is in Greek.** There is no variant here, no interpolation, and nobody to blame. The collapse begins in an act of reverence.

Assembled, the evidence shows one direction, and it is not the direction the public argument is looking for. It is not from a low christology to a high one. It is from figurative to literal — from corporate to individual, from instrumental to physical, from spatial to sensory, from a relation to an identity. A collective seed acquires a masculine pronoun. A measuring line acquires a voice. The Chosen One of God becomes the Son of God. A relative clause about manifestation acquires a subject. And this same movement is visible in three sets of hands with nothing in common: translators with no christology at all, evangelists with one, and later copyists with another.

That convergence is what the correspondential hypothesis predicts, and this thesis stops there deliberately. **The ordinary explanation is sufficient**: figurative language concretises whenever it crosses a language or a generation, because readers render what a phrase means to them, and what it means to them is the nearer sense. Nothing in this evidence requires more than that, and nothing in this evidence rules more out. What the audit does establish is that the objection which used to close the question — that scribal elevation had already explained the whole phenomenon — does not survive contact with the manuscripts. The elevation is not there. Something else is, and it runs deeper, earlier, and through hands that had no reason to move it.

The last word belongs to a verse that no one disputes and that both sides pass over. When the charge in John 10:33 is that a man is making himself God, the answer given is Psalm 82:6 and a reason: **πρὸς οὓς ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ἐγένετο** — *those to whom the word of God came*. Not those who were divine. Not those who claimed. Those who received. The text records the literalising conversion being made by the hearers, and it records the answer to it, and the manuscripts have transmitted both without a variant for eighteen centuries while the argument was conducted elsewhere.

*The apparatus was never going to see it. It was never a variant.*
