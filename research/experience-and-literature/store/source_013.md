# Source 013 — Alvarado 1997: what spontaneous out-of-body experiences contain

**Citation**: Carlos S. Alvarado, *Psychological and Phenomenological Characteristics of Out-of-Body Experiences* (PhD thesis, University of Edinburgh, 1997). Open access: https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/6963.

**Accessed**: 2026-11-03. PDF text downloaded by the research agent. Read myself: Table 21 (Study 3), the passages on prior knowledge and belief (pp. 38–39 and the Study 3 results), and appendix cases SC1003 (pp. 266–267) and SC1049 (p. 280).

## What it says

**Study 3** (N = 88, mainly British, recruited through newspaper and periodical appeals). Table 21, feature percentages, each with its own N:

| Feature | % |
|---|---|
| Stayed in usual surroundings | 69 |
| Saw physical body | 65 |
| Saw surroundings from above | 63 |
| Sensation of rising on leaving | 56 |
| Saw lights around or at a distance | 33 |
| Entered place different from usual surroundings | 28 |
| Passed through tunnel, enclosure or place on leaving | 18 |
| Saw spiritual entities or beings | 18 |
| Saw images of events of earlier life | 3 |
| Saw ray of light, cord, ribbon or rope connecting the out-of-body self to the physical body | 2 |

**Prior knowledge does not drive content.**
- Among single experiencers, prior knowledge of OBEs did not correlate with the number of features reported (r = .08, p = .32).
- Participants recruited through parapsychology-related publications reported *fewer* features (mean index 7.21, N = 19) than newspaper respondents (9.45, N = 55). This was "the opposite of my" hypothesis.

**Content may generate belief, rather than belief generating content** (pp. 38–39): "OBE content may be a function of previous expectations and life experiences, but the experience of particular features may generate belief as well. For example, encountering other dimensions or spiritual entities could foster belief and a desire to continue having experiences with such transcendental components."

**Naming comes later, from reading.**
- Case SC1003 (pp. 266–267): a nurse told her "she wouldn't mention to anyone as people might think I was a bit odd … I was overjoyed to read about the same experience in the Reader's Digest 10 years later. I then knew I was not odd."
- Case SC1049 (p. 280): "Six years after my experience I read about OBE in Reader's Digest magazine and that was the first time I realized that what had happened to me was not unique."

**Reported by the agent from the thesis, not checked myself.**
- Study 2 (N = 492 readers of a Spanish New Age magazine, a self-selected and likely primed group) reports more "other dimension" (34%), entities (29%) and voices (28%).
- Among single experiencers, people claiming OBEs at will did not differ from others in the number of features (3.60 vs 3.48, p = .67). The richer content of self-inducers reflects how often they have the experience.
- In a reanalysis of published cases, near-death OBEs more often include tunnels (24% vs 0%), entities (49% vs 23%) and lights (25% vs 7%) than non-near-death OBEs.

## Why it matters

1. **The ordinary spontaneous OBE is simple.** It stays in ordinary surroundings and sees the body and the room from above. Entities appear in fewer than one in five accounts in the British sample. No study codes for a cosmology, named beings or places with names, and nothing in these tables resembles a star address.
2. **Content is not driven by prior reading** (r = .08). If anything, the parapsychology-literate reported less. Interpretation, by contrast, is shaped afterwards. Two cases name a Reader's Digest article read 6 and 10 years later as the moment they understood what had happened. This matches source 005 (Yuill 1991) exactly.
3. **Richer, other-worldly content concentrates in groups primed by a literature** (the New Age magazine readers) **and in near-death cases.** This needs care. The near-death excess is not an artefact of reading; in Alvarado's analysis it is tied to the near-death condition.

## Limits

- Appeal samples are self-selected.
- Survey "yes" answers to OBE questions are not validated by interview (Alvarado, *Psi Encyclopedia*, via the agent).
- The two Reader's Digest cases are illustrations, not a rate.
