# Source 029: The Destruction(s) of the Library of Alexandria — Progressive Loss

**Status**: Gap F research.

## The Core Finding

The Library of Alexandria was not destroyed in a single catastrophic event. It declined progressively over centuries through multiple partial destructions — each one corresponding to a stage in the loss of the correspondential tradition. The scholarly consensus is that the Library was effectively dead long before the Arab conquest (642 CE).

## The Timeline

| Date | Event | What happened | What was lost |
|------|-------|--------------|---------------|
| **145 BCE** | Ptolemy VIII purges intellectuals | Drove out scholars including head librarian Aristarchus. Nature of patronage changed. | The living intellectual community — the scholars who USED the Library. The building remained; the minds left. |
| **48 BCE** | Caesar's fire | Warehouses near the docks burned during the siege. Seneca: 40,000 scrolls destroyed. | Partial loss — Strabo visited the Mouseion c. 20 BCE, decades later. Not a total destruction. |
| **272 CE** | Aurelian's attack | Roman emperor retook Alexandria from Palmyrene rebels. The Bruchion district (where the Library stood) was destroyed. | Possibly the effective end of the main Library. Membership appears to have ceased by the 260s. |
| **297 CE** | Diocletian's siege | Emperor leveled the Bruchion district again. | Whatever remained of the main Library facility. |
| **391 CE** | Theophilus destroys the Serapeum | Bishop of Alexandria led an attack on the temple of Serapis, which housed a "daughter library." Imperial decree sanctioned temple demolition. | The Serapeum collection — the last institutional repository. Orosius is the only source mentioning books being destroyed. |
| **642 CE** | Arab conquest | The story of Caliph Umar ordering the burning is a 13th-century fabrication (Ibn al-Qifti). Bernard Lewis: "It is surely time that the Caliph and Amr were acquitted of this charge." | Nothing — there was nothing left to burn by this date. |

## The Correspondential Reading

The progressive destruction maps onto the progressive loss of the correspondential tradition:

1. **145 BCE — Loss of the scholars**: The living carriers of knowledge leave. The texts remain but the readers are gone. This is the beginning of the end — the key survives in the building but nobody is using it.

2. **48 BCE — Partial fire under Caesar**: Collateral damage to the physical repository. The Ptolemaic decline in patronage had already weakened the institution. This is the natural degree decaying.

3. **272-297 CE — Roman military destruction**: The main Library facility destroyed. This coincides with the rise of the institutional church under Demetrius and his successors — the correspondential tradition is being driven from its institutional base at the same time the physical building is demolished.

4. **391 CE — Theophilus destroys the Serapeum**: The Christian bishop destroys the last temple that housed a library. This is the institutional church completing what the military destruction started — the elimination of the physical repository of pagan and correspondential knowledge. The timing is precise: Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter ordering the purging of heretical books was issued in 367 CE. The NHL was buried around this same period. The Serapeum's destruction 24 years later was the final blow.

## What the Library Contained

Besides Greek literature, the Library incorporated:
- Berosus's history of Babylonia (Chaldean knowledge)
- Hermippus's commentary on "two million verses" of Zoroaster
- Buddhist writings (through Ashoka's exchanges)
- The Septuagint (produced there for the Jewish community)
- Egyptian priestly texts (access to the temple tradition)

This was the PHYSICAL hub of the synthesis. When it was destroyed, the texts scattered — some into private collections, some into monastic libraries (where the NHL codices were produced), some lost forever.

## Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholars agree:
- The Library was not destroyed in one event but declined gradually
- Loss of royal patronage (from the late Ptolemies onward) was the primary cause
- The real tragedy is not who to blame but how much was lost
- By 391 CE, there was "no remaining 'Great Library' in the sense of the iconic vast, priceless collection" (Jonathan Theodore)
