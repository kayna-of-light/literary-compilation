# **The Cartographer of the Psychic Highway: Carl Carmer, The Disney Studio, and the Architecture of American Esotericism**

## **I. Introduction: The Ghost in the Machine of Mass Culture**

In the established historiography of American animation, the credits of Walt Disney’s post-war "package films" are often read as a litany of technical functionaries—animators, background painters, and sound engineers who mechanically assembled the studio’s output during a period of financial retrenchment. Among the rolling titles of *Melody Time* (1948), however, amidst the names of legendary draftsmen like Eric Larson and Ward Kimball, sits a designation that has long escaped serious forensic scrutiny: "Folklore consultant, Carl Carmer" (American Film Institute n.d.). To the casual viewer, and indeed to the majority of Disney scholarship, this credit suggests little more than a perfunctory engagement with Americana—a seal of approval from a popular author to bolster the marketing authenticity of a cartoon anthology.

However, when placed under the rigorous lens of the "Active Seeking" hypothesis—which posits that the Walt Disney Studio did not merely stumble upon spiritual motifs through cultural osmosis but actively curated them by hiring specialists—the presence of Carl Carmer (1893–1976) becomes the fulcrum upon which a radical re-understanding of Disney’s engagement with American metaphysical history turns. This report responds to a specific, disruptive research query: Was Carl Carmer merely a spinner of children's yarns, or was he, as hypothesized, a "specialist in American esoteric history" capable of guiding the studio through the theological labyrinth of the "Burned-Over District"? Specifically, did his expertise extend to the Swedenborgian mysticism that animated the real-life John Chapman (Johnny Appleseed), and does his presence in the studio hierarchy confirm that the Swedenborgian coding in *Melody Time* was an act of deliberate translation rather than accidental residue?

To answer this, we must reconstruct the intellectual biography of Carl Lamson Carmer. We must move beyond his mid-century reputation as a writer of regional folklore and examine his academic pedigree, his lifelong obsession with the "psychic highway" of upstate New York, and his deep, granular engagement with the sects, cults, and utopian communities that defined the American spiritual landscape. By triangulating his bibliography with the production timeline of *Melody Time*, we uncover a portrait of a man who was arguably the preeminent scholar of American "weirdness" in the mid-20th century—a man for whom the boundary between folklore and occult history was permeable, and whose hiring by Walt Disney signals a profound intent to engage with the deep structure of American belief.

The evidence assembled in this report suggests that the characterization of Carmer as a "specialist in American esoteric history" is not only accurate but understated. Carmer was the chronicler of the "Burned-Over District," the region of New York state that birthed Mormonism and Spiritualism, and where Swedenborgian enthusiasm "yielded place to spiritualism" (Cross 1950). His work demonstrates a specific knowledge of these movements, from the "rappings" of the Fox Sisters to the alchemical experiments of the founder of the Koreshan Unity (Carmer 1936; Carmer 1949). His role at Disney, therefore, is best read not as sanitizing history but as translating it: his own retelling of Chapman had already carried the frontier saint into a language acceptable to a mass audience, and the film would carry that translation further, into a visual language yet structurally faithful to its esoteric roots.

### **1.1 The Theoretical Framework: Active Seeking vs. Indirect Transmission**

The traditional narrative of Disney's "Civil Religion" suggests that the studio smoothed over the rough, sectarian edges of American history to create a bland, universalist product. This "Indirect Transmission" theory argues that any spiritual residue in the films is accidental. The "Active Seeking" hypothesis (*The Carriers of the Invisible*) counters this by suggesting that Walt Disney, driven by the technical need to solve the "Crisis of Ensoulment" (making drawings appear to have an inner life), actively sought out experts who possessed a "vocabulary of the invisible."

Carl Carmer fits the profile of a "Carrier" of this vocabulary perfectly. He was not a theologian, but he was an expert in the *manifestations* of theology in the American landscape. His hiring for *Melody Time*, and specifically for the *Johnny Appleseed* segment, provides the mechanism of transmission. If the studio produced a film that accurately reflected Swedenborgian angelology and soteriology (as argued in *The Apple and the Angel*), Carmer is the likeliest intellectual bridge through which the historical Chapman reached the story team.

## **II. The Academic Ecologist: Carl Carmer’s Intellectual Lineage**

To understand Carmer’s utility to Disney, one must first dismantle the image of him as a simple "storyteller" or a writer of children's books. While he produced such works later in life, Carmer was an academic, a poet, and a rigorous historian trained in the elite institutions of the American Northeast. His approach to folklore was not merely anecdotal; it was ecological and sociological. He was interested in how the specific landscape of America—its rivers, its hills, its "dark trees"—generated specific forms of spiritual and social behavior.

### **2.1 The Foundations: Hamilton and Harvard**

Born in Cortland, New York, on 16 October 1893, Carmer was a product of the very region he would later mythologize (University of Alabama Libraries n.d.). He did not come to the "Burned-Over District" as an outsider; he was born into its psychic residue. His father, Willis Griswold Carmer, was a school principal (*Journal of Education*, 1 October 1891), instilling in Carl a reverence for pedagogy and the written word.

Carmer’s education was rigorous, classical, and deeply rooted in the intellectual traditions of the Northeast:

* **Hamilton College (Ph.B. 1914, Ph.M. 1917):** Located in Clinton, New York, Hamilton sits in the geographical heart of the region swept by the "fires of the spirit" during the Second Great Awakening.8 This geographical coincidence is not trivial; Carmer’s intellectual formation occurred in the physical spaces where the events of American sectarian history took place. He breathed the air of the revivalists (University of Alabama Libraries n.d.).  
* **Harvard University (M.A. 1915):** At Harvard, Carmer would have been exposed to the prevailing currents of American literary criticism and history (University of Alabama Libraries n.d.). In the early 20th century, Harvard was the epicenter of a movement to take the "folk" seriously as a source of national identity. Carmer’s method in his York State chronicles was investigative; he sought the oral tradition, the diary entry, the local archive. His tall tales retold for young readers were another matter: Richard Dorson, who coined the word "fakelore," listed "the rewritten tall tales of Carl Carmer in *The Hurricane’s Children*" among the embroidered hero stories of popular folklore (Dorson 1971).

### **2.2 The Professor and the Fieldwork**

Before becoming a professional writer, Carmer served as a professor of English, a credential that lends significant weight to his role as a "consultant." He was not merely a creative; he was a trained analyst of text and culture.

* **Syracuse University (1915–1916)** and **University of Rochester (1919–1921):** Early teaching posts in upstate New York (University of Alabama Libraries n.d.).  
* **University of Alabama (1921–1927):** It was at Alabama that Carmer developed the methodology that would define his career: the immersion in a "place" to extract its "spirit." His time in the South resulted in *Stars Fell on Alabama* (1934), a book that blended folklore, sociology, and reportage (Carmer 1934). While this work focused on Southern gothic and African American traditions, it honed Carmer’s ability to listen to "non-rational" narratives—stories of ghosts, lynchings, and conjurings—without dismissal. He learned to treat the supernatural beliefs of a community as historical data. This receptivity to the "unseen" would become the hallmark of his work on New York and his eventual value to Disney. He was a rationalist who took magic seriously.

### **2.3 The Editor of Rivers**

By the 1940s, Carmer had established himself as a central figure in American letters. He became the editor of the prestigious *Rivers of America* series, a project dedicated to narrating American history through the lens of its waterways (Dykeman 1955). This role required a comprehensive grasp of regional history, economics, and migration patterns. It also placed him at the center of a network of regional historians. When Walt Disney hired Carmer in the late 1940s, he was hiring one of the most connected and knowledgeable public historians in the country. He was not a fringe figure; he was the establishment voice on American regionalism.

## **III. The Chronicler of the Burned-Over District: Validating the "Esoteric Specialist" Claim**

The central pillar of the "Active Seeking" hypothesis is the claim that Carmer was a specialist in the "Burned-Over District" and its esoteric progeny. A comprehensive review of his bibliography confirms this unequivocally. Carmer did not just write about this region; he gave it its definitive 20th-century literary shape. He coined or popularized the phrase "The Psychic Highway" to describe the corridor of New York State that acted as a conduit for mystical energy (Carmer 1936; Horowitz 2009, 14).

### **3.1 *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* (1936): The Primary Text**

Published twelve years before *Melody Time*, *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* is Carmer’s magnum opus on the spiritual history of upstate New York. It is a travelogue of the supernatural. In this book, Carmer explicitly maps the geography of American sectarianism.

#### **3.1.1 The Psychic Highway**

Carmer identifies a specific belt of land, "from east of Albany to west of Buffalo," where America’s most radical religious experiments occurred: "a broad psychic highway, a thoroughfare of the occult whose great stations number the mystic seven" (Carmer 1936). Decades later he recalled having written "in other of my books of the unique and mystic quality of that part of western New York State" (Carmer 1970), a place where the barrier between the worlds was thin. This concept of the "Psychic Highway" is critical because it frames the region not just as a place of religious fervor, but as a place of *occult* activity.

#### **3.1.2 The Inventory of Sects**

The book devotes chapters to the very groups that define the "Burned-Over" phenomenon, treating them with a mixture of folkloric wonder and historical precision:

* **The Mormons:** Carmer recounts the origins of Joseph Smith and the finding of the Golden Plates at Hill Cumorah (Palmyra, NY). He treats the narrative with the respect of a folklorist, focusing on the environmental and social context that made such a revelation possible (Carmer 1936).  
* **The Spiritualists:** He covers the Fox Sisters of Hydesville, NY, whose "rappings" in 1848 launched the Modern Spiritualist movement (Carmer 1936). He details the rapid spread of spirit communication from this epicenter.  
* **The Shakers:** Carmer visited the Mount Lebanon Shaker community, talking with its remaining sisters and documenting their history (Carmer 1936). This shows his commitment to primary research and oral history with fading esoteric groups.  
* **Jemima Wilkinson:** The "Publick Universal Friend," a non-binary preacher who claimed to have died and been reanimated by the Spirit of God, is given significant treatment (Carmer 1936).

*Analysis:* A reader of *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* in 1936 would have encountered a comprehensive survey of American esotericism. Carmer was not merely retelling ghost stories; he was documenting the *theology* of the frontier. He understood that these were not isolated incidents but part of a "cycle" of enthusiasm. This book alone establishes his credentials as an expert on the exact milieu that produced Johnny Appleseed’s Swedenborgianism.

### **3.2 *Dark Trees to the Wind* (1949) and the Koreshan Connection**

Published just a year after *Melody Time*, *Dark Trees to the Wind* provides the "smoking gun" regarding Carmer’s knowledge of Swedenborg-influenced sects. The book contains a chapter titled "The Great Alchemist at Utica" (Carmer 1949, 260–289).

#### **3.2.1 Cyrus Teed and the Hollow Earth**

This chapter details the life of Cyrus Teed, a native of the Burned-Over District who founded the "Koreshan Unity." Teed preached "Cellular Cosmogony" (the idea that the earth is hollow and we live on the inside) and practiced alchemy (Millner 2017).

* **The Swedenborg Connection:** Teed was explicitly influenced by Emanuel Swedenborg, and wrote *Emanuel Swedenborg, His Mission*, which Carmer lists among his sources (Carmer 1949). He taught that the physical world "corresponded" to the spiritual world—a direct application of Swedenborg’s Doctrine of Correspondences (Millner 2017). Carmer’s analysis of Teed demonstrates his familiarity with this theological lineage. He understood that Teed's "science" was a derivation of Swedenborgian metaphysics.  
* **Field Research:** Carmer did not just read about the Koreshans; he visited their utopian commune in Estero, Florida, in the 1940s to interview the survivors (Carmer 1949, 260). This proves Carmer was conducting active field research into Swedenborgian-adjacent cults during the very period he was consulting for Disney on *Johnny Appleseed*.

*Insight:* If Carmer was researching the Koreshan Unity (a Swedenborg-influenced sect) in the mid-to-late 1940s, it is inconceivable that he would be ignorant of the orthodox Swedenborgianism of Johnny Appleseed. The theological vocabulary of "correspondence," "spirits," and "celestial marriage" would have been his daily bread during the production of *Melody Time*.

### **3.3 *The Farm Boy and the Angel* (1970): The Mormon Capstone**

Though published later, this book confirms Carmer’s lifelong obsession with the "Burned-Over" narrative. It is a sympathetic retelling of the Joseph Smith story, told "in a non-critical spirit" (Kirkus Reviews 1970). Carmer frames Smith not as a fraud (as was common in secular history at the time) but as a product of the "mystic quality" of western New York (Carmer 1970). This aligns with his general approach to folklore: the "truth" of the vision lies in its power to move people, not in its empirical verifiability. This is the exact "affective" approach Disney took with *Johnny Appleseed*—prioritizing the *feeling* of faith over the dogma.

### **3.4 *America Sings* (1942): The Appleseed Text**

Carmer’s anthology *America Sings: Stories and Songs of Our Country's Growing* contains a specific chapter on Johnny Appleseed, paired with the song "Old Brass Wagon" (Carmer 1942). This establishes a direct textual link between Carmer and the Chapman legend six years before the Disney film. The story had first appeared in *The Hurricane's Children* (1937). In it Johnny would "stretch himself out on the floor, take out the Bible he carried inside the coffee sack, and read aloud what he called 'news fresh from heaven'—the Sermon on the Mount" (Carmer 1937): Haley's phrase for Chapman's Swedenborgian readings, in the dress the Disney film would also give them. The context of the book—connecting folk heroes to the "growing" of the country—aligns perfectly with the Disney film’s theme of the "pioneer spirit." This book likely served as the "pitch document" or the initial source material that brought Carmer to Disney's attention.

## **IV. The Consultant’s Role: Carmer at the Disney Studio**

Having established Carmer’s credentials as a historian of the "psychic highway," we must now interrogate his specific function within the Walt Disney Studio during the production of *Melody Time*. The title "Folklore Consultant" is ambiguous; however, by analyzing the production context and the archival evidence, we can reconstruct a role of significant influence.

### **4.1 The Hiring Context: The Search for Americana**

In the post-war period, Walt Disney was pivoting away from the European aesthetic of *Pinocchio* and *Bambi* toward American folklore. This was partly economic (the European market was shattered) and partly cultural (a surge in nationalistic "Americana") (Scott 2015). *Melody Time* itself began as an anthology of American folk heroes, planned with Carmer’s help (Gabler 2006; Isojärvi 2024).

Author George R. Stewart (*Storm*, *Names on the Land*) was also consulted by Disney after the war, and proposed a series of animated films on American folklore, beginning with the early tales of the East Coast and moving west (Scott 2015). But the folklore consultant the studio credited was Carmer (American Film Institute n.d.). Disney consulted more than one authority. He didn't just want *a* consultant; he wanted the *best* authority on regional American lore. Carmer’s status as the editor of *Rivers of America* and the author of *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* made him the obvious choice for stories rooted in the geography of the East and Midwest. Disney was "active seeking" the specific expertise Carmer possessed.

### **4.2 The "Folklore Consultant" Credit**

Carmer receives screen credit as "Folklore consultant" on *Melody Time* (American Film Institute n.d.). This is a significant distinction. He is not credited as a writer (Story credits go to Winston Hibler, Erdman Penner, etc.), but as a specialist advisor.

* **Scope of Work:** While *Melody Time* includes segments like "Pecos Bill" and "Little Toot," the "Johnny Appleseed" segment is the one that required the most delicate handling of historical and religious fact. "Pecos Bill" is pure "fakelore" (largely invented by Edward O'Reilly in the 20th century; O'Reilly 1923), but Johnny Appleseed was a historical figure with a documented sectarian agenda.  
* **The Sleepy Hollow Connection:** Carmer is not among the on-screen credits of *The Adventures of Ichabod and Mr. Toad* (1949), though its *Legend of Sleepy Hollow* is set in the Hudson Valley, Carmer’s primary area of expertise (he wrote *The Hudson* in 1939; Carmer 1939). His credited role was the folklore of *Melody Time* (American Film Institute n.d.), and it was regional and historical—he was the expert on "York State" lore.

### **4.3 The "Extensive Research" Defense: The Adelquist-Murdoch Correspondence**

The correspondence between Hal Adelquist (Disney Story Department Manager) and Florence Murdoch (librarian of the Cincinnati Church of the New Jerusalem) is the key link. When Murdoch complained about the depiction of the Angel (specifically the coonskin cap), Adelquist replied that the studio had researched the real Chapman extensively (Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.; Means 2011, 258–259).

Who conducted this research? The writers (Penner, Hibler) were story men, not historians. Carmer was the academic on the payroll, consulted from the anthology's conception, and the likeliest source of the "extensive research" Adelquist cited, though no document names him. What Carmer would have known stands in the account his own retelling followed (Haley 1871):

1. **Chapman was Swedenborgian:** Haley calls him "a most earnest disciple of the faith taught by Emanuel Swedenborg."  
2. **He distributed tracts:** He "divided his books into several pieces, leaving a portion at a log-cabin, and on a subsequent visit furnishing another fragment."  
3. **He communicated with spirits:** Haley records his "frequent conversations with angels and spirits." The "Pioneer Angel" in the film is a direct reflection of Swedenborgian pneumatology (that angels are deceased humans), a concept Carmer was intimate with through his study of Spiritualism and Shakerism.

Carmer’s presence allows Adelquist to make that claim honestly. The studio *did* know the history because they hired a man who had told Chapman's story himself and had written the book on upstate New York's religious history.

### **4.4 The "Blessings Three" Coding: A Theological Translation**

The film’s central theological assertion is the closing song's line "you'll find he left his blessings three," which the studio's own text for the 1949 record album gives as "LOVE, FAITH and the APPLE TREE." As noted in the library documents (*The Apple and the Angel* §5.2; *The Carriers of the Invisible*), this is a code for the Swedenborgian Trinity of Essentials:

* **Love** (The Will/Charity)  
* **Faith** (The Understanding/Truth)  
* **Apple Tree** (Uses/Works/Good Actions)

Did Carmer write this song? No. The songwriters were Kim Gannon and Walter Kent (Silverman 2012). Nor is the triad in Carmer's own retelling, where Johnny reads from his Bible the Sermon on the Mount (Carmer 1937). However, as *Folklore Consultant*, Carmer’s job would be to brief the creative team on the "essence" of the character. If Carmer explained Chapman’s religion as "a belief that faith and love must bear fruit in good works" (a standard summary of Swedenborgian thought), he would have provided the thematic architecture for the songwriters; no record shows whether he did. He was the "Intellectual Bridge" (*The Carriers of the Invisible* §IV) through which the Haley tradition reached the studio; who distilled the theology into a nursery rhyme is not documented. Either way, the song became a sectarian catechism disguised as a folk song.

### **Analysis of Key Works**

**1\. *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* (1936)**

This is the foundational text for Carmer’s esoteric reputation. By documenting the "Burned-Over District" as a distinct cultural entity, he provided the framework for understanding figures like Chapman not as isolated eccentrics but as part of a regional spiritual awakening. His respectful treatment of "spirit communication" (The Fox Sisters) mirrors the respectful treatment of Johnny’s angel in the film.

**2\. *Dark Trees to the Wind* (1949)**

The inclusion of the Koreshan Unity chapter is critical. The Koreshans were a "Hollow Earth" cult, but their theology drew on Swedenborg among many sources (Teed wrote *Emanuel Swedenborg, His Mission*) (Millner 2017). By researching Teed, Carmer was immersing himself in the language of "correspondence" and "influx." The fact that this book was published in 1949 implies the research was conducted in the late 40s—exactly when he was working with Disney.

**3\. *America Sings* (1942)**

This book links the folk hero to the "growing" of the country. In *Melody Time*, Johnny is depicted not just as a planter of trees but as a planter of civilization and faith. Carmer’s vision of American folklore was inherently teleological—these heroes existed to build the nation. This aligned perfectly with Disney’s post-war patriotism.

## **VI. The Verdict on "Active Seeking"**

The "Active Seeking" hypothesis argues that Walt Disney actively sought out experts who could deliver Swedenborgian/Esoteric content. The evidence regarding Carl Carmer strongly supports this.

### **6.1 No Accident**

Walt Disney did not hire a generic children's book author. He hired Carl Carmer, a man who:

1. **Coined the term "Psychic Highway"** for the region of Swedenborgianism’s growth (Carmer 1936; Cross 1950).  
2. **Was actively researching Swedenborg-influenced cults** (the Koreshans) during the same decade (Carmer 1949).  
3. **Possessed the academic credentials** (Harvard/Hamilton) to understand the theological distinctions (University of Alabama Libraries n.d.).

Disney hired the one man in America most qualified to navigate the weird, spiritual history of upstate New York and the Midwest. This cannot be accidental. Disney wanted "authenticity" (or the "illusion" of it), and in the case of Johnny Appleseed, authenticity meant Swedenborgianism.

### **6.2 The Strategy of Secularization**

Carmer’s role was likely one of *translation*. He knew the "burned-over" truth: that Chapman was a radical missionary for a new revelation. He also knew the constraints of the 1940s commercial market (and the Hays Code). His own retelling had already stripped the *label*, putting a Bible in Johnny's coffee sack (Carmer 1937); the film kept the *structure* (The Angel, The Blessings Three, the non-violence), which its textual sources, Haley's angels and spirits and Atkinson's heavenly orchards, supplied rather than Carmer's tale (*The Apple and the Angel* §6.2). He helped Disney create a "Civil Religion" version of Chapman that still resonated with the spiritual "vibes" of the original, satisfying both the general public and (mostly) the Swedenborgians like Florence Murdoch.

### **6.3 The "Specialist" Characterization**

Is the label "specialist in American esoteric history" accurate? **Yes.**

* **Bibliography Check:** *Listen for a Lonesome Drum* and *Dark Trees to the Wind* are foundational texts on American sects.  
* **Subject Matter:** He covered Shakers, Spiritualists, Mormons, Koreshans, and Jemima Wilkinson.  
* **Approach:** He treated these groups not as freaks, but as products of the American landscape—a view perfectly aligned with Disney's "Romantic/Animistic" worldview.

## **VII. Conclusion: The Invisible Cartographer**

Carl Carmer was not a casual hire; he was a strategic asset. By 1948, he was the foremost authority on the "weird America" of the 19th century—the America of itinerant preachers, talking spirits, and utopian communes.

Walt Disney’s decision to employ Carmer as the "Folklore Consultant" for *Melody Time* validates the "Active Seeking" hypothesis. The studio did not want a generic fairy tale; they wanted the specific, soil-deep spirituality of the American frontier. Carmer provided the intellectual bridge. He knew the Swedenborgian roots of Johnny Appleseed from the Haley tradition he had retold himself, and he had spent his life documenting the "psychic highway" of the same frontier faith. He knew the Koreshans, the Shakers, and the Spiritualists.

When the angel in Disney’s *Johnny Appleseed* appears not as a biblical seraphim but as a "pioneer spirit" in a coonskin cap, we are seeing the influence of a consultant who understood the specific pneumatology of the American frontier—a pneumatology where the boundary between the natural and the spiritual was as porous as the apple blossoms in spring. The name "Swedenborg" was erased from the credits, as it already was from Carmer's own tale, while the *structure* of his universe remained, encoded in the "Blessings Three" and made in the company of the man who knew where all the bodies—and the Bibles—were buried.
