# Rome's Gravity: The Imperial Substrate of the Jesus Movement's Textual History, 62 BCE – 325 CE
## A Two-Step Settlement: Military Reset of the Parent Institution, Editorial Capture of the Surviving Movement, and Constantinian Ratification of a 250-Year-Running Relationship

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis argues that the textual history of the Jesus movement from 62 BCE to 325 CE is intelligible as the documented operation of two settlements conducted by the Roman imperial state on a movement whose institutional substrate it found within its operational range. **Step One** (62–73 CE) was a military reset of the parent institution: the proxy escalation of 62 CE in which the Sadducean high priest Ananus II executed James the brother of Jesus during the procuratorial gap between Festus and Albinus; the engineered escalation of 64–66 CE under Gessius Florus (Temple Treasury seizure, equestrian crucifixions); the methodical reduction of 66–70 CE under Vespasian and Titus; the destruction of Temple, Sanhedrin, and high priesthood; and the foundation of the Flavian dynasty on the *Iudaea Capta* coinage program. **Step Two** (post-70 CE) was the editorial operation conducted across the *diverse polycentric Christian landscape* that survived the destruction of Jerusalem — Jamesian remnants, Marcionites, Valentinians, Sethians, Bardaisanites, Johannine and Thomassine communities, Pauline-network communities, and the synoptic streams — with the proto-orthodox synthesis crystallizing inside the Pauline network's institutional matrix and converging on Constantinian ratification (313–367 CE) across approximately 250 years. The operation is not a single editor's hand but a distributed multi-generational signature: six handling strategies — write fresh (Mark, the deutero-Pauline corpus, Acts), smooth (Matthew, the Pastorals, 1–2 Peter), capture (John, Hebrews), admit at low canonical weight (James, Jude, the Antilegomena), marginalize (Ebionites, Nazarenes, the Jewish-Christian Gospels), and destroy or bury (Marcion's *Evangelion*, Thomas, the Manichaean corpus) — applied across the textual corpus in exact correspondence with each text's compatibility with imperial-compatible orthodoxy. The differentiation principle is the operational signature. The thesis treats the canonical four Gospels as four datapoints among approximately fifty primary witnesses; the subject is the imperial substrate underneath the textual record. The two-tiered evidentiary structure — Tier 1 the documented Roman record (Tacitus, Suetonius, Pliny, Cassius Dio, Josephus, Hegesippus, the *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus*, the *Iudaea Capta* coinage, the Arch of Titus, the *Fiscus Judaicus* apparatus); Tier 2 the textual record read for differentiation pattern — converges on the conclusion that Rome was the gravitational mass and the surviving network the local actor. The Constantinian moment is the formal ratification of a 250-year-running operational relationship, not a discontinuous imperial intervention. The thesis is integrated with companion studies in the broader library and is offered as the most adequate available explanation of the cumulative documentary record.

> **Keywords.** Rome; Jesus movement; Jamesian Christianity; Pauline network; First Jewish War; Vespasian; Titus; Domitian; Constantine; canon formation; Marcion; Gospel of Thomas; Manichaeism; *Iudaea Capta*; *Fiscus Judaicus*; *Desposyni*; Ananus II; Pliny–Trajan; Eusebius; Hegesippus; Irenaeus; Tertullian; *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus*; imperial patronage; differentiated editorial handling; proto-orthodoxy; New Marcionite Paradigm; Davidic line; Roman *modus operandi*; substrate analysis.

## 1. Introduction: Why the Substrate, Not the Surface

The conventional question in New Testament studies is which texts came first, who edited what, and how the canon stabilized. The conventional answer organizes itself around the four canonical gospels because they are the surviving documents that have been read continuously for nearly two millennia and around which most scholarly apparatus has been built. The bias of canonical-Christian framing is reproduced in scholarship that takes the four as the primary object of inquiry and treats every other surviving fragment — the Marcionite *Evangelion*, the Gospel of Thomas, the Hebrew Gospel, the Ebionite and Nazarene materials, the Pseudo-Clementine corpus, the Manichaean witness — as ancillary, deviant, or marginal to the central object. This thesis does not.

The subject of inquiry here is not the four canonical gospels and not the canonical New Testament. The subject is **the imperial substrate** — the set of political, military, fiscal, administrative, judicial, and patronage conditions under which the entire textual history of the Jesus movement was produced from approximately 62 BCE (Pompey's annexation of Judea as a Roman possession) to 325 CE (the Council of Nicaea, where the relationship between Roman imperial power and Christian doctrine becomes formal and undisguised). Within that substrate the four canonical gospels are four datapoints in a far wider operational signature. The Pauline corpus, the deutero-Pauline letters, the Pastorals, the Catholic Epistles, the Acts of the Apostles, the Apostolic Fathers, the apologetic and heresiological literature of the second through fourth centuries, the texts that did not survive at full length and are reconstructed from patristic citations (Marcion's *Evangelion*, the Gospel of the Hebrews, the Ebionite Gospel), the texts that survived only by being buried (the Nag Hammadi corpus), and the texts that survived only at the empire's edge (the Manichaean corpus at Turfan) are all further datapoints in the same operational signature. The thesis treats them together because they were produced together — under the same imperial gravity, by communities living inside or pressed against the same political reality, with outcomes shaped by the same documented Roman administrative practice.

The argument advanced is that the textual history of the Jesus movement, considered as a whole, is the documented outcome of a **two-step Roman managed settlement**, conducted across a quarter-millennium and concluded by Constantinian ratification of a relationship that had been operating since before the destruction of the Second Temple.

**Step One** is the military reset of the parent institution. The Second Temple after 6 CE was not a religious sanctuary tolerated by Rome; it was the operating organ of Roman administration in Judea. The high priest was a Roman appointee. The high-priestly vestments were kept under Roman seal in the Antonia Fortress and released only at festivals. The high-priestly families (Annas, Caiaphas, Boethus, Phiabi) were Roman client aristocracy. Tax collection and judicial order ran through the Sadducean-controlled Sanhedrin under imperial license. A daily sacrifice for the Emperor was offered in the Temple, and its cessation in 66 CE constituted the formal start of the revolt. The Temple was the proxy. The Jamesian movement — Davidic-claimed, Torah-observant, popularly attested, economically radical, eschatologically imminent, structurally independent of the high priesthood — constituted a parallel polity that the proxy system existed to prevent. When the proxy system began to fail in 62 CE, with Ananus's judicial murder of James and the Pharisaic protest that reached Albinus, Rome ran the standard imperial playbook: engineer escalation under successive procurators (Albinus, then Florus), permit revolt to develop, deploy overwhelming military force, raze the parent institution, abolish the high priesthood and the Sanhedrin, impose a punitive fiscal apparatus (the *Fiscus Judaicus*), and found the new ruling dynasty (the Flavians) on the legitimacy of the destruction. The *Iudaea Capta* coinage and the Arch of Titus in Rome are not commemorative ornaments. They are the public seal of an imperial reset that the new regime's continued legitimacy required to remain undone.

**Step Two** is the editorial operation that handled the surviving movement. Once the parent institution was gone, the post-70 environment contained not a homogeneous "Pauline" substrate but a *polycentric and diverse* field of Jesus-movement communities and texts: Jamesian-Jewish-Christian remnants regrouped at Pella and surviving as Ebionites, Nazarenes, and the Pseudo-Clementine tradition; Johannine communities preserving their own high-Christological witness traceable to a Jerusalem-pillar source; Thomassine and proto-Encratite streams; Markan and proto-Lukan synoptic traditions; Marcionite churches that within two generations would dominate Asia Minor and the Syrian frontier; Basilidean, Valentinian, Sethian, and Bardaisanite schools that within two generations would dominate Alexandria and Edessa; and the Pauline-network communities of the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean. Bauer's foundational demonstration (*Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei im ältesten Christentum*, 1934) — modified but not overturned by Strecker, Köster, Pearson, and Roberts — stands: in Egypt, Edessa, and significant parts of Asia Minor, what later scholarship would call "heresy" was the *original* and dominant manifestation of the faith, while what would later be called "orthodoxy" was a minority faction that had to be imported. "Proto-orthodoxy" was not the surviving substrate; it was a *late second-century synthesis* engineered out of this diverse field. The surviving Pauline-network communities — Greek-speaking, diaspora-based, householder-financed, politically quietist (Romans 13:1–7), theologically severable from Davidic dynastic claims because justification by faith does not require the Davidic Messiah on a Jerusalem throne — furnished the *institutional and demographic matrix* into which that synthesis would later be poured. They did not furnish its theology unaltered: the Pauline corpus's earliest and most enthusiastic exegetes were Marcion, Valentinus, and Basilides, who read Paul as the pneumatic apostle of a Christ severable from the Creator-God of the Old Testament; the proto-orthodox synthesis had to *domesticate* Paul against those readings (through the Pastorals, through Acts, through Irenaeus's *Adversus Haereses*) before it could deploy him as its own apostle. The editorial operation thus ran across the full diverse surviving field, not within a homogeneous Pauline substrate, and its output was differentiated by audience and by the suppressability of the materials being handled. Some texts were composed fresh under network conditions (Mark, the deutero-Pauline epistles, the apologetic Acts). Some were produced to smooth the polemic and capture audiences the foundational compositions had alienated (Matthew for Jewish-Christian audiences, the Pastoral Epistles to retrofit Paul into a household-conservative figure, 1–2 Peter to carry the network's authority under Petrine cover). Some were produced to capture independent witness traditions whose suppression would have cost more than reframing (the Logos prologue and the chapter-21 appendix layered onto the Beloved Disciple's Jerusalem-witness gospel; the attribution of Hebrews to Paul to fold a sapiential-priestly composition into the Pauline corpus). Some were admitted at low canonical weight because suppression cost more than inclusion (James, Jude, 2 John, 3 John, 2 Peter, the Antilegomena classified by Eusebius). Some were marginalized as heresy and retained only in fragments through hostile citation (the Hebrew Gospel, the Ebionite Gospel, the Nazarene Gospel, the Pseudo-Clementine residue). Some were destroyed or driven to the empire's edge (Marcion's *Evangelion*, the Gospel of Thomas, the Manichaean corpus). The variation across these strategies is not noise. It is the operational signature of a managed network. A scattered set of independent communities producing texts independently does not yield a corpus whose handling of inconvenient material varies precisely with how unsuppressable that material was. That is the diagnostic of an operation, not the diagnostic of an accident.

The mechanism connecting Step One and Step Two is **patronage of a pre-positioned faction**. Rome did not write gospels. Rome did not write the *Evangelion*. Rome did not commission the Pastorals. Rome did not edit the Beloved Disciple's witness. The empire's instruments are administrative, fiscal, and military. The empire's editorial work is performed by the local actors whose interests already cut in a useful direction and who can be resourced through legal protection, household patronage, freedom of movement on imperial roads, access to sympathetic administrators, and the implicit understanding that texts hostile to imperial interests will not survive in imperial cities. This is the same mechanism by which Rome managed Egypt under the Ptolemies, the British tribes through Cogidubnus and his successors, the Hasmonean dynasty through Antipater and Herod, and the Sanhedrin through the high-priestly families. The principle that "Rome does not get its hands dirty; Rome finds the right local actor and resources him" is documented in Polybius, Tacitus, Cassius Dio, the inscriptional record, and the imperial coinage. It is not conspiracy theory. It is the standing operating procedure of the Roman state across two centuries of provincial management. Applied to the Jesus movement after 70 CE, it produces an editorial program that does not require a signed Flavian directive in order to be deliberate. The editorial brief is the air the proto-orthodox network breathed: the *Iudaea Capta* coinage, the Arch of Titus, the *Fiscus Judaicus* collected from every Jewish household across the empire, the imperial summons of Davidic descendants under Vespasian, Domitian, and Trajan, the rescript Pliny extracted from Trajan in 112 CE on the institutional handling of Christians. Every editor working within that environment knew what could and could not be in the text. The brief was known to all and stated by none.

The thesis is committed to that level of description because earlier formulations were not. An honest account of the analysis must record one decisive recalibration against softer readings, because the recalibration is part of the evidence the reader needs in order to weigh the conclusion. The recalibration replaces "selection pressure" with "imperial patronage of a pre-positioned faction." Selection-pressure language permits the conclusion to enter without naming the agent and is the formulation that respectable scholarship typically reaches when it has identified the textual pattern but is unwilling to identify the political actor that produced the gradient. Patronage language is more accurate to the documented Roman MO: empires do not produce uniform gradients in the abstract; they produce uniform gradients by selecting, resourcing, and protecting the actors whose outputs reproduce the gradient. The British East India Company analogy is the cleanest modern parallel. We do not require a signed Crown directive to recognize that the Company's outputs reflected a managed imperial relationship; the same evidentiary standard applies here. The thesis retains the mainstream dating window for the canonical Gospels (composition c. 70–110 CE) and does not depend on any chronological-compression hypothesis. The political reading of what the texts are doing within the consensus window is the contribution; it requires only the structural reading and the documented Roman MO.

The four canonical gospels are not the subject of this thesis. They are four datapoints in the operational signature. The subject is Rome.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 The Two-Tier Evidentiary Structure

The thesis operates with two evidentiary tiers held together by a single argument. The first tier is the documented Roman record: imperial coinage, monumental inscriptions, the *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus*, the *Iudaea Capta* coin series in *RIC* II, the Arch of Titus reliefs, the Pliny–Trajan correspondence preserved in Pliny's *Epistulae* book 10, the imperial historians (Tacitus, Suetonius, Cassius Dio), the imperial biographer (Suetonius), the Flavian client-historian (Josephus), and the comparative provincial record from Carthage to Britain. This tier is non-Christian, mostly non-Judean, and self-contained. It establishes the standing operating procedure of the Roman state across the period, the documented imperial concern with the Davidic line, the documented imperial fiscal action against the Jewish population (the *Fiscus Judaicus* under Vespasian, its harsh enforcement under Domitian, its reform under Nerva), and the documented imperial procedure for handling Christians (Pliny *Ep.* 10.96–97). The first tier carries the inferential weight of the thesis. If the documented Roman record showed a state that had no procedure for handling religiously framed political threats, that did not engage in engineered provocation against parallel polities, that treated the Davidic line as a matter of indifference, and that had no fiscal apparatus for fining Jewish identity after 70 CE, the thesis would not stand. The documented record shows the opposite at every point.

The second tier is the textual record of the Jesus movement and its surroundings. This tier comprises the Pauline and deutero-Pauline corpus, the Pastoral Epistles, the canonical gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the Catholic Epistles, the Apostolic Fathers, the apologists (Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Tatian), the apocryphal gospels (Thomas, Peter, the Hebrew Gospel, the Ebionite Gospel, the Nazarene Gospel), the Jewish-Christian remnants (the Pseudo-Clementines, the Elcesaite tradition), the heresiological corpus (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Epiphanius), the historiographical synthesis of Eusebius, the textual reconstructions (Marcion's *Evangelion* via Roth, Klinghardt, BeDuhn), the buried Nag Hammadi corpus, the Manichaean witness preserved at Turfan and in the Coptic Medinet Madi codices, and the Roman administrative-literary witnesses (Tacitus on the Christians and on the Jewish War, Suetonius on Claudius's expulsion and on Domitian, Pliny on his interrogation procedure, Cassius Dio on Domitian's persecution, the *Historia Augusta* on later imperial actions). This tier is the field over which the operation's signature is read. The signature is the differentiated handling of materials by their suppressability, the directional drift in editorial layers across two centuries, the asymmetry between what the first generation of texts says about Jesus's politics and what the second and third generation say, and the survival pattern that systematically privileges quietist content over Davidic-restorative content.

The two tiers are held together by the argument that the textual record's signature is what one would expect from a managed imperial relationship of the documented kind, and that the textual record's signature is *not* what one would expect from an unmanaged emergent process. This is a discrimination test, not a deductive proof. It does not require any individual editor to have received any individual imperial directive. It requires only that the documented Roman procedure, the documented Roman concern, and the documented imperial instruments would, if applied to a Davidic-messianic parallel polity surviving inside the empire after 70 CE, produce a textual record with the signature observed. The argument's strength is the convergence of two independent evidentiary streams onto one explanatory frame, where neither stream alone would force the conclusion but where the conjunction is difficult to explain otherwise.

### 2.2 The Substrate Question and the Imperial-Patronage Lens

Most New Testament scholarship reads the textual record from inside the canonical-Christian frame. The four gospels are taken as the central object, the formation of the canon is taken as the gradual recognition of intrinsic authority, and the Roman political environment is taken as a hostile background against which the church grows by inner-spiritual logic. Within this frame, anomalies in the textual record are explained by inner-Christian dynamics: the suppression of James the Just by the Pauline mission, the Petrine-Pauline rivalry, the Hellenization of a Palestinian movement, the gradualist development of Christology, the ad-hoc settlement of doctrinal disputes by individual bishops. Where the Roman state appears in this frame, it appears as the persecutor in moments of crisis (Nero in 64 CE, Decius in 250 CE, Diocletian after 303 CE) and as the patron only after Constantine. The intervening centuries are read as a long ecclesial development that the Roman state did not shape so much as occasionally interrupt.

The substrate question replaces this frame with a different one. The question is not "what did the early Christians do?" but "what was the imperial substrate they operated inside, and what did that substrate do to what they wrote?" The imperial-patronage lens is the answer at the operational level: the empire's instruments are administrative, fiscal, military, and judicial, but the empire's textual outputs are produced by local actors whose interests cut in directions the empire finds useful and who can be resourced through legal protection, household patronage, freedom of movement on imperial roads, access to sympathetic administrators, and the implicit understanding that politically inconvenient texts will not survive in imperial cities. This is documented procedure across two centuries of provincial management. It is the same mechanism by which Rome managed Egypt under the Ptolemies, the Hasmonean dynasty through Antipater and Herod, the British tribes through client kings such as Cogidubnus, and the Sanhedrin through the Annas–Caiaphas–Boethus–Phiabi succession of high-priestly families.

The lens is not "did Rome write the New Testament?" The lens is: "the Pauline-network communities were *one pre-positioned configuration* within the diverse post-70 Christian field, whose institutional and demographic profile (urban household-based, *honestiores*-class, politically quietist, theologically severable from Davidic dynastic claims) already aligned with the kind of provincial outcome Rome could live with, and the empire's available instruments were sufficient to ensure that configurations with that profile flourished while configurations not aligned with it (Jamesian-Davidic, Marcionite-radical-dualist, Manichaean) were suppressed, marginalized, destroyed, or driven to the empire's edge. The proto-orthodox synthesis that eventually crystallized inside the Pauline-network matrix — across the work of Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, and Eusebius — is the doctrinal-editorial program through which the empire's gravitational field expressed itself in textual form." The lens does not require a signed Flavian directive. It requires the documented mechanism of Roman provincial management to have applied to the religious situation in the post-70 environment. The argument is then about whether the documented Roman MO, applied to that environment, predicts the textual record we have.

This formulation supersedes a softer earlier formulation in which the same observation was framed as "selection pressure." Selection-pressure language permits the observed gradient to enter without naming the agent. It is the formulation that respectable scholarship typically reaches when it has identified the textual pattern but is unwilling to identify the political actor that produced the gradient. Imperial-patronage language is more accurate to the documented record. Empires do not produce uniform gradients across two centuries by emergent statistical drift. Empires produce uniform gradients by selecting, resourcing, and protecting the local actors whose outputs reproduce the gradient. The British East India Company is the cleanest modern parallel: we do not require a signed Crown directive to recognize that the Company's outputs reflected a managed imperial relationship; we recognize the relationship by the convergence of administrative, fiscal, and ideological evidence onto a single explanatory frame. The same evidentiary standard is applied here.

### 2.3 The Discrimination Test: What Accident Would Have to Look Like

The thesis can be stated as a discrimination test between two hypotheses. **H1**: the textual history of the Jesus movement is the outcome of an unmanaged emergent process in which independent communities, responding to local conditions, produced texts whose differential survival is explained by inner-Christian dynamics (theological development, episcopal authority, mission strategy) without significant imperial shaping. **H2**: the textual history of the Jesus movement is the outcome of a managed imperial relationship of the documented kind, executed across a quarter-millennium according to the documented Roman *modus operandi*, with military instruments deployed in Step One (62–73 CE) and patronage instruments deployed in Step Two (post-70 CE through 325 CE), and with the survival pattern reflecting differentiated handling by the suppressability of the materials.

H1 predicts the following pattern. (i) The differential survival of texts should track inner-Christian variables (mission success, episcopal advocacy, theological appeal) rather than political variables (imperial-compatibility, demographic alignment with Roman cities, severability from Davidic dynastic claims). (ii) Texts hostile to imperial interests should survive at roughly the rate of texts compatible with imperial interests, weighted only by their inner-Christian appeal. (iii) The handling of inconvenient material should be uniform: where suppression is the strategy, it should be applied across the board; where reframing is the strategy, it should be applied across the board. (iv) The post-70 editorial shift toward quietism, supersession of Israel, and exoneration of Roman authorities (Pilate, Festus, Lysias, the centurion at the cross, the centurion Cornelius, the proconsul Sergius Paulus) should be no greater than what would be expected from organic theological development in a community separating from its parent Judaism. (v) The five-generation continuity of the editorial program should be explicable by the inner momentum of the church alone, without reference to a sustained imperial relationship.

H2 predicts the opposite pattern. (i) Differential survival should track political variables. (ii) Texts hostile to imperial interests should be systematically disadvantaged. (iii) The handling of inconvenient material should be differentiated by suppressability: write fresh under network conditions, smooth alienated audiences, capture independent witnesses, admit at low canonical weight, marginalize, destroy. (iv) The post-70 editorial shift should be sharp, sustained, and asymmetric — every revision should run in the imperial-compatible direction, with reverse revisions absent. (v) The five-generation continuity should be explicable by the persistence of imperial interest across the period (the desposyni hunt under Vespasian, Domitian, and Trajan; the Pliny–Trajan rescript at 112 CE; Hadrian's Aelia Capitolina settlement; the third-century edicts; Constantinian ratification).

The thesis argues that the textual record matches H2 across all five dimensions, and that the match is sharper at every point than H1 can accommodate. Section 10 applies the test in detail. The methodological commitment here is that the test is genuine — both hypotheses are stated with predictions that could fail, and the data are read against both predictions before the conclusion is drawn. The thesis does not assume H2 in order to argue for H2. It states what H1 would have to look like, states what H2 would have to look like, and reads the textual record as the discriminator.

### 2.4 Scope: The Operation Runs Across Far More Than Four Gospels

The scope of the textual evidence is the entire textual history of the Jesus movement and its immediate surroundings, not the canonical four gospels. The temptation to take the four canonical gospels as the central object of the operation is itself the bias the operation produced. The operation's most decisive achievement, viewed from the long arc, is precisely that the canonical four came to be treated as the natural object of inquiry while the rest of the textual record came to be treated as marginal, deviant, or ancillary. A thesis that reproduces this framing reproduces the operation's signature in its own methodology.

The textual evidence treated here therefore includes, on equal evidentiary footing: the four canonical gospels (Mark, Matthew, Luke, John); the Acts of the Apostles as the program of the operation in plain prose; the seven undisputed Pauline epistles (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon); the deutero-Pauline letters (Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians); the Pastoral Epistles (1–2 Timothy, Titus); the Epistle to the Hebrews, attributed pseudonymously to Paul to capture a non-Pauline composition; the Catholic Epistles (James, Jude, 1–3 John, 1–2 Peter); the Antilegomena classified by Eusebius (HE 3.25); the Apostolic Fathers (1 Clement, the Ignatian corpus, Polycarp, the *Didache*, the *Epistle of Barnabas*, the *Shepherd of Hermas*); Justin Martyr's apologetic and dialogic writings (1 Apology, 2 Apology, Dialogue with Trypho); Tatian's *Diatessaron*, the second-century gospel harmony, surviving in fragmentary form; Irenaeus's heresiological synthesis (*Adversus Haereses*); Tertullian's anti-Marcionite corpus and *De Praescriptione Haereticorum*; Hippolytus's *Refutatio Omnium Haeresium*; Epiphanius's *Panarion*; Origen's *Contra Celsum* and surviving exegetical work; Eusebius's *Historia Ecclesiastica*; the Marcionite *Evangelion* as reconstructed by Klinghardt, BeDuhn, and Roth; the Gospel of Thomas in Coptic and Greek; the Hebrew Gospel, Ebionite Gospel, and Nazarene Gospel as preserved through Jerome and Epiphanius; the Pseudo-Clementine *Recognitions* and *Homilies*; the broader Nag Hammadi corpus including the *Apocryphon of John*, *Gospel of Philip*, *Gospel of Truth*, and *Apocalypse of Peter*; the Manichaean *Kephalaia*, the Manichaean Psalm-Book, the Cologne Mani Codex, and the Turfan fragments; and the Roman administrative-literary witnesses already named in the first tier.

The principle of inclusion is that any text whose handling can be assessed against the discrimination test belongs in the evidence. The four canonical gospels are four datapoints among approximately fifty primary witnesses, and they happen to be four datapoints whose handling falls into the "captured for canon" stratum of the operation's differentiated handling. The witnesses in other strata — Marcion's *Evangelion* destroyed; Thomas buried; the Hebrew Gospel reduced to citations; the Ebionites marginalized; the *Pseudo-Clementines* preserving Jamesian residue; the Manichaean corpus driven to the empire's edge — are equally probative, and in some respects more probative, because they show the strata that the canonical settlement systematically erased.

## 3. The Roman *Modus Operandi*: A Documented Operating Procedure

### 3.1 Carthage, Corinth, Numantia: Provoke and Overwhelm

The Roman state's standing operating procedure for managing rival or restive polities is documented in detail across the second century BCE in three set-piece operations: the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE, the destruction of Corinth in 146 BCE, and the destruction of Numantia in 133 BCE. The pattern is consistent across all three: a long preparatory period in which the rival polity is constrained by treaty and demilitarized; a period of escalation in which the polity is provoked into actions that the treaty technically permits the rival to construe as casus belli; a methodical military reduction in which Rome deploys overwhelming force; a permanent post-war reorganization in which the rival's institutions are abolished, its physical infrastructure is razed, its population is dispersed or enslaved, and its territory is reorganized as Roman administrative space.

Carthage is the textbook case. After the Second Punic War (218–201 BCE), the city was confined by treaty to a small territory and forbidden to wage war without Roman permission. For fifty years the city paid its indemnity, recovered economically, and abided by the treaty's terms. When Numidia, Rome's client, repeatedly raided Carthaginian territory, Carthage appealed to Rome for redress and received none. When Carthage finally raised an army in 150 BCE to defend itself against Numidian incursions, Rome declared this a treaty violation and sent the army that would conduct the third and final war. Cato the Elder's *Carthago delenda est* refrain in the Senate had become operating directive. The methodical siege under Scipio Aemilianus, the burning of the city, the salting of the ground, and the conversion of Carthaginian territory into the Roman province of Africa together constitute the textbook operation. The provocation pattern is structural: the rival is constrained, the constraints are designed to be impossible to maintain, the violation is then construed as casus belli, and the reduction is total. Polybius, who witnessed the operation, records the pattern as deliberate Roman procedure (Polybius 36.1–6, 38.21–22). The methodology was not improvised under pressure. It was the way the Roman state did this kind of work.

Corinth in the same year shows the operation applied to a Greek polity. The Achaean League had been Rome's nominal ally since 198 BCE and a fully subordinated client after the Battle of Pydna in 168 BCE. When the League refused to expel Sparta from its membership at Roman insistence and engaged in inflammatory rhetoric against Roman ambassadors, the consul L. Mummius advanced into Greece, defeated the League's army at the Isthmus, sacked Corinth, killed the male population, sold the women and children into slavery, looted the artworks, and razed the city. The provocation was rhetorical; the response was civilizational. The political message was clear and intended: any Greek polity considering rhetorical resistance to Rome would lose not only its political identity but its physical existence. Numantia in 133 BCE shows the same methodology applied to an Iberian *oppidum*: a long siege under Scipio Aemilianus, the construction of seven Roman camps to seal the town off from any escape, the systematic reduction of the population by starvation, the eventual storming, the enslavement of survivors, and the demolition of the site. Appian's account in his *Iberica* records the operation in deliberate detail (App. *Iber.* 84–98).

The Carthage–Corinth–Numantia triad establishes the standing Roman pattern. Provoke or permit. Strike with overwhelming force. Reorganize on imperial terms. Reward collaborators. Eliminate independent power centers. The pattern is not a Roman pathology peculiar to the second century BCE; it is a tool repeatedly deployed across the next four centuries against polities that fall within its operational range. Judea in the first century CE is one of those polities. The pattern that produced Carthage, Corinth, and Numantia is the pattern that produced Jerusalem in 70 CE.

### 3.2 Mithridates and Cleopatra: Patient Escalation, Final Strike

The Mithridatic Wars (88–63 BCE) and the Roman absorption of Ptolemaic Egypt (31–30 BCE) show the same operating procedure applied across longer time horizons. Mithridates VI of Pontus orchestrated the Asiatic Vespers in 88 BCE — the coordinated massacre of approximately 80,000 Italian residents across the Roman province of Asia, an act of provocation that was simultaneously a casus belli and a demonstration of Pontus's reach. Three Mithridatic Wars followed across twenty-five years, each ending in a settlement that Rome did not allow to stand. The first ended with Sulla forcing Mithridates back inside his ancestral kingdom in 84 BCE; the second was an inconclusive border action; the third, conducted under L. Lucullus and concluded under Pompey, ended with Mithridates dead in 63 BCE and his kingdom reorganized as the Roman province of Bithynia et Pontus. Pompey's settlement of the eastern Mediterranean in 63 BCE is the same year he annexed Judea. The two operations are part of one campaign of imperial reorganization. Pompey's siege of Jerusalem, his entry into the Holy of Holies, and his settlement of the Hasmonean dynasty as Roman client are documented in Josephus *Ant.* 14.54–79 and *War* 1.131–158. The pattern is the patient one: Rome does not strike a viable rival in the first round; Rome wears the rival down across a generation, accepts intermediate settlements that are designed to fail, and applies the final reduction when the configuration is right. The Mithridatic case shows Rome operating with strategic patience over a quarter-century. The Judea case will operate on the same timescale.

The Egyptian case shows the same patience applied to a friendly client. Ptolemaic Egypt had been a Roman client since the early second century BCE. When the dynasty fractured in the late Republic, Rome supported successive Ptolemies in their internal disputes — Pompey supported Ptolemy XII in 55 BCE, Caesar supported Cleopatra VII against her brother Ptolemy XIII in 48–47 BCE — while extracting ever-greater concessions of land, grain, and political dependency. When Antony allied with Cleopatra against Octavian in the late 30s BCE, Octavian framed the conflict as a war against an Egyptian queen rather than a Roman civil war and used the propaganda to consolidate his own position. The defeat at Actium in 31 BCE and the absorption of Egypt as imperial property — not a senatorial province but an imperial possession governed by an equestrian prefect, with the Roman equestrian forbidden from entering Egypt without imperial permission — is the textbook outcome of patient management of a wealthy client into total dependency and then property status. Suetonius records that Augustus established the rule that no senator could enter Egypt without his permission (Suet. *Augustus* 18). The strategic principle: a sufficiently valuable client is converted into property, with whatever administrative apparatus is required to maintain the conversion. Egypt's grain supply made the conversion necessary. The eastern Mediterranean's stability made similar management of Judea attractive throughout the first century BCE and into the first century CE.

### 3.3 Britain, the Druids, Boudica: Client Fragmentation

The Julio-Claudian and Flavian operations in Britain (43 CE onward) show the same procedure applied at the imperial periphery rather than at the Mediterranean core. Claudius's invasion of Britain in 43 CE was accompanied by the systematic recruitment of British client kings: Cogidubnus of the Regni, Prasutagus of the Iceni, Cartimandua of the Brigantes. The procedure was the standard one. Each client king was permitted apparent autonomy, was supported by Roman financial backing during his lifetime, and was expected to bequeath some portion of his kingdom to Rome at his death. When Prasutagus died in 60 CE and bequeathed half his kingdom to Rome and half to his daughters, the Roman procurator Decianus Catus seized the entire kingdom, flogged Prasutagus's widow Boudica, and raped her daughters. The provocation was complete. Boudica's revolt, the destruction of Camulodunum, Londinium, and Verulamium, the slaughter of perhaps seventy thousand Roman colonists and Britons loyal to Rome, and the eventual crushing of the revolt by Suetonius Paulinus at the Battle of Watling Street are documented in Tacitus's *Annals* 14.29–39 and *Agricola* 16.

The procedure is the Carthage procedure scaled to imperial periphery: provoke (the seizure of the kingdom and the flogging), permit the revolt to develop, deploy overwhelming military force, conduct the reduction (massive casualties, large-scale enslavement, deeper administrative penetration), and reorganize on imperial terms (closer Roman governance, removal of the client structure, infrastructural investment in Roman roads and forts to prevent recurrence). The simultaneous operation against the Druids at Anglesey under Suetonius Paulinus in 60 CE is the same procedure applied to a religious-political target. Tacitus describes the Roman destruction of the Druidic groves and the slaughter of the Druids in *Annals* 14.30. The Druids were the religious-judicial leadership of the British tribes. Their destruction is functionally equivalent to the destruction of the Sanhedrin and the high-priestly families would be in 70 CE. The pattern is the same: identify the religious-political institution that legitimates indigenous resistance, destroy its leadership, raze its sacred sites, and prevent its reconstitution. The Druids were destroyed at Anglesey in 60 CE; the Temple establishment in Jerusalem was destroyed in 70 CE; the methodology is one methodology applied to two religious-political institutions standing in the way of imperial consolidation.

The Tacitean diagnosis at *Agricola* 30 — *auferre trucidare rapere falsis nominibus imperium, atque ubi solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant* — is placed in the mouth of the Caledonian chieftain Calgacus on the eve of the Battle of Mons Graupius. "To plunder, slaughter, and steal — these things they misname empire; and where they make a desert, they call it peace." Tacitus is Agricola's son-in-law. He has insider knowledge of the operating procedure. He is also writing under Domitian, in a context where direct critique of imperial policy is dangerous. The diagnosis is therefore placed in the mouth of an enemy, where Tacitus can stage the indictment with maximum rhetorical force without staging it in his own voice. The fact that the indictment is placed in the mouth of the enemy is itself an internal admission. Tacitus knows the procedure. The Calgacus speech is the procedure stated baldly by someone outside it. The methodology of empire is recognized as a coherent operating procedure by the writer who knows it best.

### 3.4 Tacitus's Diagnosis and the Inscriptional Record

Tacitus's diagnosis is corroborated by the inscriptional record across the imperial period. The *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* of 186 BCE (CIL I² 581 = ILLRP 511) is the foundational specimen of the Roman state's standing posture toward unauthorized religious organization. The decree, preserved on a bronze tablet found in 1640 at Tiriolo in southern Italy, is the Senate's response to the spread of Bacchic worship across Italy in the years following the Second Punic War. The relevant clauses prohibit the establishment of new shrines (*Bacanal habuise velet*), require the dismantling of existing shrines (*Bacanalia, sei qua sunt, exstradite*), prohibit unauthorized assemblies of more than five worshippers (and not more than two men and three women), and require Senate approval for any continuing Bacchic worship. Livy's narrative at 39.8–18 records the political-legal context: the Senate's investigation, the mass executions (Livy reports as many as 7,000 killed across Italy), and the public framing of the action as protection against *coniuratio* — political conspiracy disguised as religious practice. The Bacchanalia decree is not an isolated act of religious bigotry; it is the documented baseline of Roman state procedure regarding any cult capable of organizing a network of believers across cities, holding nocturnal mixed-gender assemblies, swearing oaths binding participants to obedience to the cult's leadership, and operating outside the apparatus of the *res publica*. Persecutions of Christians from Nero through Diocletian are textbook applications of this standing posture, not novel inventions. The inscription itself is what makes the methodology legible: this is what the Roman state writes when it confronts a cult of this profile.

The *Iudaea Capta* coinage attested in *RIC* II under Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian — RIC II Vespasian 159 et al., issued from 70 CE through approximately 81 CE in gold, silver, and bronze across mints from Rome to Lugdunum to Antioch — is the most extensive single propaganda program of the first century. The reverse types depict a palm tree (the symbol of Judea) with a mourning Jewish woman seated beneath, often paired with a captive Jewish man with hands bound, sometimes with the Emperor standing in armor over the captives. The legend IVDAEA CAPTA appears across the issues. The coinage was struck in volume sufficient to circulate across the empire and is preserved in numismatic collections at the British Museum, the Bibliothèque nationale de France, the American Numismatic Society, and the Israel Museum, among others. The ANS *Online Coins of the Roman Empire* (OCRE) database indexes the issues with photographic plates. The political content of the program is unambiguous: the Flavian dynasty's legitimacy is built on the military victory over Judea. The dynasty does not have other foundational achievements of comparable magnitude. The Iudaea Capta program is the dynasty's foundational claim. It must continue to function as such. The implications for everything else the dynasty does in relation to Judea, including its handling of any messianic movement attached to the Davidic line, follow from the dynasty's structural dependence on the *Iudaea Capta* legitimacy.

The Arch of Titus in the Roman Forum, erected by Domitian after Titus's death (c. 81 CE), with its inscription preserved as CIL VI 945 (SENATVS POPVLVSQVE ROMANVS DIVO TITO DIVI VESPASIANI F VESPASIANO AVGVSTO), is the corresponding monumental statement. The two principal relief panels — Titus in his triumphal chariot escorted by Victory, and the procession bearing the Menorah and the Table of Showbread looted from the Temple — together broadcast the political message in stone. The vault relief shows Titus carried to heaven by an eagle (apotheosis). The Flavian house is divine; its divinity is validated by victory over the Jewish God; the spoils of the Temple are the visible proof. The arch stands at the eastern end of the Forum, facing the Colosseum (itself constructed with proceeds from the Jewish War). The political instruction to anyone walking through the Forum is clear: the dynasty's claim to legitimacy is the destruction of the Temple, and any movement whose political logic could question that destruction is therefore a movement at structural odds with the dynasty.

### 3.5 What Rome Did and Did Not Do

What Rome did across this period is documentable, repeatable, and consistent. Rome surveilled provincial movements with potential political content; Rome managed local clients to perform tasks Rome did not wish to perform directly; Rome engineered escalations that produced casus belli; Rome deployed overwhelming military force when reduction was the appropriate strategy; Rome reorganized provincial space after reduction with a combination of fiscal apparatus, infrastructural investment, and selective patronage; Rome rewarded collaborators with citizenship, equestrian or senatorial status, financial benefit, and political legitimation; Rome tolerated cults that did not threaten political stability; Rome destroyed cults that did. What Rome did *not* do is also documentable. Rome did not write the texts of the cults it suppressed. Rome did not produce edited versions of competing scriptures. Rome did not run a centralized scriptorium that issued doctrinal positions. The empire's textual outputs are administrative — edicts, rescripts, *senatus consulta*, military diplomas, fiscal records — and the empire's literary outputs are aristocratic — historiography, biography, satire, philosophy, the Greek revival under the Antonines. The empire does not write religious texts.

This is the essential clarification that the imperial-patronage lens requires. The thesis does not claim that Rome wrote the gospels, edited the Pauline corpus, or composed the Pastorals. The thesis claims that Rome did what Rome documentably did across two centuries — surveilled, managed, engineered, deployed, reorganized, rewarded, tolerated, suppressed — and that the textual outputs of the surviving Jesus movement reflect operation of those instruments by local actors whose interests aligned with the imperial outcome. The specific local actors — the Pauline communities, the proto-orthodox bishops, the apologists, the heresiologists — are the editors. The empire is the gravitational mass. The empire's procedures are the conditions under which the editing was performed. The signature of the operation is the convergence of the editorial outputs onto the imperial-compatible direction across five generations, sustained across changes of dynasty (Flavian to Antonine to Severan to Tetrarchic to Constantinian) and across the absence of any single coordinating authority. That convergence is the diagnostic. It does not require central direction; it requires only that the gravitational field remain stable across the period. Section 9 will show that the field did remain stable across the period. The Constantinian endpoint is not the moment when the empire took over the church; it is the moment when the empire formally ratified a relationship that had been operating since before the destruction of the Temple.

## 4. The Pre-70 Configuration: Rome and Judean Religion

### 4.1 Roman Judea After 6 CE

The political configuration into which the Jesus movement emerged was not a free Jewish polity but a Roman administrative space governed under a particular form of indirect rule. Rome had absorbed Judea into its eastern arrangements with Pompey's settlement of 63 BCE, when Pompey entered the Holy of Holies, confirmed Hyrcanus II as high priest while stripping him of the royal title, made the Hasmonean state tributary, and installed Antipater the Idumean as effective administrator (Josephus *Ant.* 14.54–79; *War* 1.131–158). Across the next sixty years the arrangement was repeatedly adjusted: Antipater's son Herod the Great (37–4 BCE) became the textbook Roman client king, ruling on imperial sufferance while paying for the privilege through massive infrastructural projects (the Temple expansion, Caesarea Maritima, Sebaste, the Antonia Fortress, the Herodion). On Herod's death the territory was divided among his sons, and after Archelaus's deposition in 6 CE Judea proper became a Roman province governed by a *praefectus* of equestrian rank reporting through the legate of Syria. From 6 CE through 41 CE, then briefly under Agrippa I (41–44 CE), and from 44 CE through 66 CE under a series of Roman procurators, the territory was governed directly by Roman officials backed by Roman troops. Pontius Pilate (26–36 CE), the procurator under whom Jesus was executed, falls in this sequence. The Gospel narratives' setting is not free Judea but Roman Judea — a province with a Roman garrison, Roman fiscal apparatus, and Roman judicial authority. The Roman state's interest in the religious-political life of Judea is therefore not the interest of a distant observer but the operational interest of the governing power.

Roman governance in Judea was conducted through a distinctive division of labor. The procurator handled fiscal collection, military matters, and capital jurisdiction. The high priest and the Sanhedrin handled internal Jewish religious-judicial matters within the boundaries Rome permitted. The arrangement is the standard one for direct provincial rule with a local cooperating elite. Tacitus describes the same arrangement at the cultural level when he diagnoses Roman procedure: Rome rules through local elites where it can; through legions where it must; and through a combination of both where the territory is sufficiently restive. Judea was sufficiently restive throughout the period. The *prefectus*-Sanhedrin partnership was tense, recurrent disputes flared over Jewish religious sensitivity to imperial cult objects (Pilate's standards in Jerusalem, Caligula's attempted statue erection in 40 CE), and the partnership occasionally fractured spectacularly. But across the period from 6 CE to 66 CE the partnership held. The Roman state rules; the high-priestly aristocracy administers; the population pays. This is the political configuration into which the Jesus movement emerged, and within which the Jesus movement's earliest texts were produced.

### 4.2 The Temple as Imperial Control Mechanism

The Jerusalem Temple in the first century CE was simultaneously the religious center of Second Temple Judaism and an administrative-fiscal node integral to Roman provincial governance. The high priest was appointed and deposed by the Roman authority — first by the Herodian client kings, then by the Roman legate of Syria, then briefly by Agrippa II — and the rotation of high priests across the period from 6 CE to 66 CE shows the appointment functioning as a political instrument. Annas (high priest 6–15 CE), his son-in-law Caiaphas (18–36 CE), Caiaphas's brother-in-law Jonathan, and other members of the same priestly families controlled the office across decades. Josephus's account of the period (*Ant.* 18 and 20) records the appointments and depositions in detail. The fiscal apparatus of the Temple — the half-shekel tax collected annually from every Jewish male twenty and over, the sacrifices and offerings, the priestly tithes, the deposits of the wealthy — circulated value through Jerusalem on a scale that made the Temple the wealthiest single institution in the eastern Mediterranean outside imperial Rome itself. The Temple Treasury contained reserves measurable in the tens of millions of denarii. Roman procurators repeatedly drew on Temple resources for state purposes (Pilate's aqueduct, Florus's seizure of seventeen talents in 66 CE), and the maintenance of the Temple establishment served Rome's interest by providing the high-priestly aristocracy with the wealth and prestige required to function as effective Roman clients.

The Roman utility of the Temple did not exhaust its religious significance. The Temple remained, for first-century Jews, the dwelling-place of YHWH on earth, the cosmic axis around which the calendar of festivals, sacrifices, and pilgrimages organized the religious year, the location of the Holy of Holies entered only by the high priest on Yom Kippur. The dual function — religious center and Roman administrative node — was the source of the chronic tension that runs through the period. Movements within Second Temple Judaism that rejected the high-priestly aristocracy as corrupt — the Qumran community most prominently, but also various prophetic and apocalyptic movements — registered the dual function as a violation: the institution that should be the dwelling-place of YHWH was operating as a Roman administrative node, run by a priesthood the protesting movements regarded as illegitimate. The Jesus movement, in its earliest layer, fits within this stream of internal Jewish protest. Jesus's reported actions in the Temple — the cleansing, the prophecy of destruction — are intelligible as actions within a stream of first-century Jewish movements that critiqued the high-priestly establishment for its compromise with Rome. The movement's protest is religious-political, not religious in any sense that excludes the political.

### 4.3 The Sadducean–Sanhedrin Client Aristocracy

The Sadducean party, drawn principally from the high-priestly families and the lay aristocracy associated with them, was the institutional manifestation of the Roman client aristocracy in Jewish religious life. Sadducean theology — denial of resurrection, rejection of oral tradition supplementary to the written Torah, acceptance only of the Pentateuch as authoritative scripture — fits the social position of an aristocracy whose interests aligned with the maintenance of the existing order rather than with apocalyptic reversal. Josephus, himself from a priestly family aligned with the aristocracy, describes the Sadducees in *Ant.* 13.297–298 and 18.16–17 and *War* 2.164–166. The Pharisees, by contrast, drew on a wider social base including scribes, teachers, and lay piety movements; their theology — affirmation of resurrection, acceptance of an expanding oral tradition, eschatological expectation — fits a movement with broader social reach and less direct stake in the high-priestly establishment.

The Sanhedrin in the first century CE — the supreme Jewish council in Jerusalem — was the institutional locus where the Sadducean-aristocratic interest met the broader Jewish religious-judicial life. Its composition, debated in scholarship, included priestly aristocrats, scribes (largely Pharisaic), and elders. Its president was the high priest. Its jurisdiction encompassed religious-judicial matters within the boundaries Rome permitted, and the Roman authority retained the *ius gladii* — the power of capital punishment — for itself. The Sanhedrin functioned, in this configuration, as the proxy through which Rome managed Jewish internal disputes without directly involving itself. The arrangement is the standard one for indirect rule with a local cooperating aristocracy. The Sanhedrin's actions against the early Jesus movement — the action against Peter and the apostles described in Acts 4–5, the action against Stephen described in Acts 6–7, the action against James the brother of Jesus described in Josephus *Ant.* 20.197–203 — are intelligible as actions of the local cooperating aristocracy against a movement whose continued growth threatened the stability of the Sanhedrin's position. The action against James in 62 CE (treated in detail below) is the documented case where the proxy mechanism is visible.

### 4.4 The Jamesian Movement as Parallel Polity

The Jerusalem community led by James the brother of Jesus, in the period from approximately 33 CE to 62 CE, functioned as a parallel polity within Jerusalem. The community's structure — apostles around James, elders, a base of pious Jewish believers — replicated the basic structure of the Sanhedrin while operating outside it. The community's practice — Temple attendance combined with messianic faith in Jesus, observance of Torah combined with shared meals, financial pooling combined with care for widows and orphans — combined the religious practice of Second Temple Judaism with a parallel set of institutional commitments to the Jesus movement. The community's leadership — James himself, described in Hegesippus's account preserved in Eusebius *HE* 2.23 as "the Just," wearing only linen, drinking no wine, kneeling in prayer until his knees became calloused like a camel's, granted access to the Holy of Holies — combined the role of Jewish priestly figure with the role of leader of a messianic movement. The Hegesippan account is stylized and includes legendary elements (the priestly access is historically improbable for a non-Sadducean; the camel-knees are hagiographical). But the account preserves what later Jewish-Christian tradition remembered: James was a religious figure of sufficient stature within Jerusalem that the destruction of his movement required action by the high-priestly aristocracy itself.

The Jamesian movement's institutional profile is therefore precisely the profile that Rome's standing posture toward unauthorized religious organization (Section 3.4) treats as a problem. The community was networked across cities (Jerusalem as center, satellite communities in Antioch, Damascus, the diaspora), held assemblies including shared meals, swore oaths binding participants to communal obligations (the Acts account of Ananias and Sapphira, however polemically presented, attests the practice), operated parallel financial pooling outside imperial fiscal apparatus, and proclaimed allegiance to a king (Jesus as Messiah) other than the emperor. The profile fits the Bacchanalia decree's specifications for the kind of cult Roman state procedure treats as a *coniuratio* in religious form. The community was protected, within Jerusalem itself, by the fact that it operated within the framework of Second Temple Judaism — and Judaism enjoyed *religio licita* status under Roman law. The protection was real but limited. It depended on the community remaining recognizable as Jewish, the high-priestly aristocracy remaining willing to tolerate it, and the Roman authority not perceiving it as a political threat in its own right. In 62 CE all three conditions changed simultaneously. The result was the action against James.

### 4.5 The Davidic Line as Documented Imperial Concern

The Roman authority's interest in descendants of David is not a later inference but a documented imperial concern. Hegesippus's account preserved in Eusebius *HE* 3.11–12 records that Vespasian, after the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, ordered a search for descendants of David, "with the object that no one of the royal race might be left among the Jews." Eusebius *HE* 3.19–20 records that Domitian, "fearing the appearance of Christ as did Herod also," ordered the descendants of Jude (the brother of Jesus, in Hegesippus's framing) brought before him, examined them, and, learning that they possessed only a small farm and the calluses of agricultural labor, dismissed them as politically harmless. *HE* 3.32 records that under Trajan the imperial procurator pursued descendants of David and the family of Jesus; Symeon the son of Clopas, kinsman of Jesus and second leader of the Jerusalem community, was crucified at advanced age (Hegesippus says 120, which is hagiographical) on the charge of being both a descendant of David and a Christian. The Hegesippan accounts contain stylized elements but they preserve what mattered: that the imperial authority, across three reigns (Vespasian, Domitian, Trajan), maintained an active operational interest in descendants of David.

The interest is not theological. It is political. The Davidic line, in the Hebrew prophetic tradition, is the line from which the messianic king of Israel is to come. A Davidic claimant proclaimed king by his followers is, in the Roman political vocabulary, a pretender to royal title — exactly the kind of figure the empire could not permit to circulate. The trilingual *titulus* affixed to Jesus's cross — *Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum* — names the offense in the Roman political category: this man is being executed as a claimant to a kingship Rome has not authorized. The imperial pursuit of Davidic descendants across the seventy years following Jesus's execution is the consistent application of the same logic. The empire perceived the *Desposyni* (the Greek term used by later Christian writers for the kinsmen of Jesus) as carriers of a political claim that, in the wrong configuration, could re-ignite the messianic-revolutionary energies that had produced two devastating Jewish wars (66–73 CE and the Bar Kochba revolt of 132–135 CE). The pursuit was therefore an operational matter, conducted by procurators across decades, leaving traces in the historical record that survived Roman administrative reorganization, Jewish-Christian extinction, and proto-orthodox editorial selection. The evidence we have is the residue of a much larger operation. The implication for the Jesus movement's textual history is structural: any text composed in the empire after 70 CE, transmitted across the imperial space after 70 CE, and surviving the operations of the period, was either compatible with the imperial concern about Davidic claimants or was driven underground. The textual traces left by both compatible and underground streams will be the evidence in Sections 7 and 8.

## 5. Step One: The Reset of the Parent Institution, 62–73 CE

### 5.1 62 CE: The Proxy Escalation Against James

The action against James the brother of Jesus in 62 CE is the documented event that opens the eleven-year sequence of imperial reset. The single contemporaneous source is Josephus *Antiquitates Judaicae* 20.197–203, a passage whose authenticity (in contrast to the disputed *Testimonium Flavianum* at *Ant.* 18.63–64) is overwhelmingly accepted by modern critical scholarship. The passage records that the Roman procurator Porcius Festus had died in office, that his replacement Lucceius Albinus was still en route from Alexandria, and that during this brief procuratorial gap the high priest Ananus II ben Ananus convened the Sanhedrin, brought before it "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" (τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ, Ἰάκωβος ὄνομα αὐτῷ), accused him "and certain others" of having transgressed the law (παρανομησάντων), and "delivered them to be stoned" (παρέδωκε λευσθησομένους). The phrase *tou legomenou Christou* is non-confessional; Josephus is distinguishing this Jesus from others, not making a Christian profession. The passage's authenticity is precisely what makes it useful: it preserves a narrative that no later Christian editor would have invented, since the event records the death of a major Christian leader at the hands of a high-priestly action that the same narrative then implicitly criticizes.

The political content of the action is legible. Ananus is a Sadducean high priest from the same priestly family that had handled the trial of Jesus thirty years earlier — Ananus II is a son of the Annas of the Gospel passion narrative. The procuratorial gap (Festus dead, Albinus not yet arrived) is the operational window. A high priest cannot lawfully convene a capital trial without Roman authorization; a Sanhedrin assembled to issue a stoning verdict is acting on the boundary of, or beyond, its permitted jurisdiction. Ananus uses the procuratorial gap as the window in which the action becomes possible. The target is the leader of the Jamesian movement, the messianic-Jewish parallel polity that has grown across thirty years inside Jerusalem and across the diaspora. The mechanism is the standard one: the high-priestly aristocracy moves against the parallel polity using the institutional apparatus (the Sanhedrin) that the Roman authority normally permits to handle internal Jewish matters. The procurator's absence is the only condition that makes the move executable, because the move would have been blocked by an active procurator to whom the high-priestly aristocracy reports. The action against James in 62 CE is therefore the high-priestly aristocracy operating against the Jamesian movement at the moment when Roman supervision is briefly absent. The interpretive question is whether this is a freelance action by Ananus or whether it operates within a wider field of Roman interest.

### 5.2 The Pharisaic Protest and Roman Intervention

The Pharisaic protest that follows the stoning is the diagnostic. Josephus records that "those of the inhabitants of the city who were considered the most fair-minded and who were strict in observance of the laws were offended at this" (*Ant.* 20.201) — the Pharisaic-leaning lay elite who were not part of the Sadducean high-priestly aristocracy and who recognized that Ananus's action against James was a violation of the procedural boundaries Rome permitted. They sent emissaries to the incoming procurator Albinus en route from Alexandria and they sent emissaries to King Agrippa II. The result, recorded by Josephus, was that Agrippa stripped Ananus of the high-priesthood — Ananus had held the office only three months — and that Albinus on arrival affirmed the decision. The Roman authority therefore intervened, after the fact, against the high priest who had executed James. Two implications follow.

First, the Pharisaic protest reveals that the action against James was perceived by part of the Jewish religious leadership not as an internal Jewish matter properly handled by the Sanhedrin but as a violation of the political-judicial settlement Rome had established. The Pharisees did not protest the killing of James because they shared the Jamesian movement's theology (they did not, in significant respects); they protested because the killing was procedurally outside the boundaries Rome permitted, and because permitting the high-priestly aristocracy to operate this way against any movement was a violation of the political settlement that protected Pharisaic interests as well. Second, Rome's intervention is selective and after the fact. Rome does not stop Ananus from killing James; Rome punishes Ananus only after the killing has been done and after a Pharisaic faction has provided Rome with the political cover to depose him. The pattern is the standard Roman one: the empire permits actions by local elites that suit imperial interests, then punishes those elites when their actions become embarrassing or when factional pressure within the local elite provides the opportunity. The action against James — the Davidic-line leader of the parallel polity that troubles both the high-priestly aristocracy and the imperial concern about messianic claimants — went forward. The political fallout — the deposition of the high priest who had performed the action — was managed afterward. The Jamesian movement lost its leader; the high-priestly aristocracy lost a high priest; both losses fit imperial interest in containing both the parallel polity and the high-priestly aristocracy's freelance operation.

### 5.3 62–66 CE: Florus and the Engineered Escalation

The four years between the action against James in 62 CE and the outbreak of the First Jewish War in 66 CE are the textbook period of engineered escalation. Albinus (62–64 CE) and his successor Gessius Florus (64–66 CE) governed Judea with progressively more aggressive fiscal extraction and more provocative public behavior. Josephus's narrative in *War* 2.272–308 records the Florus period in detail, and the bias of the account — Josephus blames the war heavily on Florus's personal greed and engineered provocations to exonerate the Jewish aristocracy with which he identified — does not undermine the documentary value of the specific incidents Josephus records. *War* 2.293 records that in 66 CE Florus extracted seventeen talents from the Temple Treasury under the pretext of imperial requisition, an act of fiscal violation calibrated to provoke maximum public outrage. *War* 2.289 records that earlier the same year a Greek resident of Caesarea Maritima sacrificed birds on an overturned chamber pot at the entrance of the local synagogue while Florus's apparatus declined to intervene to prevent the desecration. *War* 2.308 records that Florus, in response to the Jerusalem disturbances that followed the Temple Treasury seizure, "dared to do what no one had done before, to whip and nail to the cross men of the equestrian order" — Jewish residents of Jerusalem who held Roman equestrian rank and were therefore legally exempt from corporal punishment of this kind. The crucifixions of equestrians were the tipping point. The crowd that had been outraged by the chamber-pot incident, then outraged by the Treasury seizure, was now confronted with the public execution of Roman-status Jews in a manner the empire's own legal order normally prohibited.

The Tacitean diagnosis at *Agricola* 30 — *ubi solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant* — is the framework within which Florus's actions are intelligible. Florus is not an incompetent procurator who accidentally provoked a war; Florus is the executor of a pattern that, viewed from Rome, achieved its operational objective. The objective was the escalation that produced casus belli. The objective was met. The Sanhedrin, the temple aristocracy, and the moderate Pharisaic leadership lost control of the population. The Zealot and Sicarii factions — the messianic-revolutionary streams within Second Temple Judaism — moved into the political space the moderate leadership had vacated. Eleazar son of Ananias suspended the daily sacrifice for the emperor in the Temple, an act that constituted a formal renunciation of imperial authority. Roman troops were attacked. The procurator Cestius Gallus, legate of Syria, advanced on Jerusalem with the XII Fulminata legion in 66 CE, suffered a tactical reversal at Beth-Horon, and withdrew. The withdrawal converted a manageable provincial disturbance into a full-scale revolt that required imperial-level military response. Vespasian was dispatched in early 67 CE with three legions (V Macedonica, X Fretensis, XV Apollinaris) plus auxiliaries. The methodical reduction had begun. The escalation that produced this outcome ran for four years from the action against James to the suspension of the daily sacrifice. The pattern fits the Carthage–Numantia procedure exactly: the rival is constrained, the constraints are tightened to the point of provocation, the rival is provoked into the act that constitutes casus belli, the methodical reduction follows.

### 5.4 66–70 CE: The Methodical Reduction

The military operation conducted by Vespasian and Titus from 67 CE to 70 CE was a textbook Roman reduction, conducted with the methodology established at Carthage, Numantia, and Boudica's Britain and applied to the eastern Mediterranean's most contested provincial space. Vespasian's campaign in 67–68 CE pacified Galilee, Peraea, and Idumaea methodically, taking towns one at a time, establishing Roman control over the agricultural and demographic base before approaching Jerusalem. The Galilean campaign included the siege of Jotapata, where Josephus himself surrendered and converted from Jewish general to Roman client historian — an event that gave Rome the surviving documentary record of the war from the inside. By 68 CE Galilee was pacified, Jewish moderate factions had begun negotiating with Vespasian, and only Jerusalem remained as a major center of resistance. The death of Nero in June 68 CE and the subsequent civil war (the Year of the Four Emperors, 68–69 CE) interrupted the campaign. Vespasian was acclaimed emperor in Alexandria in July 69 CE, departed for Rome to consolidate his position, and left the Jerusalem operation to Titus.

Titus's siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE was the methodical reduction. The legions surrounded the city, constructed circumvallation works, methodically reduced the outer walls, took the Antonia Fortress, and on the 10th of Lous (the Hebrew month of Av) burned the Temple. Josephus *War* 6.250 records the date — the same calendar date on which the Babylonians had burned the First Temple in 586 BCE, the coincidence that converted the destruction into a theological event for surviving Judaism. Tacitus's narrative in *Histories* 5.13 reaches the burning but the manuscript breaks off at 5.26 before Tacitus's full description; the climactic Roman account is therefore lost. What survives is Josephus's narrative — itself bias-laden, since Josephus was on Titus's staff and protected his patron's legacy by claiming Titus tried to save the Temple — and the visual record on the Arch of Titus, which depicts the Menorah and the Table of Showbread being carried in triumph through Rome. Modern scholarship has questioned Josephus's claim that Titus tried to save the Temple. The Roman *modus operandi* documented in Carthage, Corinth, and Anglesey — the destruction of the religious-political institution that legitimates indigenous resistance — fits Titus's destruction of the Temple as a deliberate act, not as an accident. The Temple was the institution on which the high-priestly aristocracy depended; it was the sacrificial center of Second Temple Judaism; it was the cosmological axis around which the Hebrew religious calendar organized itself; it was the institutional locus where the Davidic-messianic stream had its highest political stakes. Titus destroyed it. The methodology fits. The destruction was the operational point.

### 5.5 70–73 CE: The Flavian Dynasty Founded on *Iudaea Capta*

The political consequences of the destruction were immediate and durable. The Flavian dynasty — Vespasian, Titus, Domitian, ruling from 69 CE to 96 CE — built its legitimacy on the *Iudaea Capta* coinage program (Section 3.4) and the Arch of Titus. The mass coinage program, struck across thirty years from mints across the empire, made the dynasty's foundational claim visible across every provincial city: the Flavian house had defeated the Jewish God and looted the Temple, and the spoils funded imperial Rome. Vespasian's construction of the Templum Pacis in Rome, completed in 75 CE, displayed the Temple spoils — the Menorah, the Table of Showbread, the silver trumpets — in a permanent public exhibition. The Colosseum, begun under Vespasian and completed under Titus, was financed (according to a recently identified inscription text reconstruction) "from the spoils of war" — *ex manubiis* — almost certainly the Jewish War spoils. The dynasty's monumental architecture, fiscal apparatus, and propaganda program were structured around the Jewish War as foundational.

Two operational consequences followed. First, the Sanhedrin and the high-priestly aristocracy ceased to exist as functioning institutions. The high-priestly families were extinguished or scattered; the Sanhedrin disbanded; the Sadducean party died. The Pharisaic-rabbinic stream that survived at Yavneh under Yohanan ben Zakkai began the long reorganization of Judaism around Torah, oral tradition, and synagogue rather than around Temple and sacrifice — the rabbinic Judaism that emerges across the next two centuries. Second, the Jamesian Jerusalem community was either extinguished in the siege, dispersed, or driven across the Jordan to Pella in the eastward flight that Eusebius *HE* 3.5 records. The Jerusalem leadership was gone. The textual archives that had circulated within the parallel polity — the *logia* of Jesus, the testimony of the Twelve, the apostolic letters, the *Iēsouine memoirs* that fed into the early Aramaic and Greek Gospel streams — survived only as much as they could be carried out by individual fleeing community members. The decisive operational result of Step One was therefore not only the destruction of the parent institution (Temple, Sanhedrin, high priesthood) but the loss of the institutional matrix that had hosted the Jamesian movement. The movement that had been the parallel polity inside Jerusalem until 62 CE no longer had Jerusalem to be inside. The movement that survived would have to survive, after 70 CE, in the imperial space where the Pauline network had been operating across the previous two decades. That network is the surviving editorial substrate. Section 7 will examine its profile.

The 62–73 CE sequence is therefore not a series of unrelated events but the documented application of the Roman *modus operandi* to a specific imperial concern. The action against James opened the sequence; the Pharisaic protest and Roman intervention demonstrated the proxy mechanism; Florus's escalation produced casus belli; Vespasian's and Titus's reduction destroyed the parent institution; the Flavian dynasty consolidated the political dividend. At the close of the sequence, the high-priestly aristocracy was extinguished, the Sanhedrin dissolved, the Jamesian Jerusalem leadership scattered, the Temple destroyed, and the Flavian dynasty founded on the Jewish War. The relationship between the Roman state and the Jewish religious-political configuration was reset comprehensively. What remained — the Pharisaic-rabbinic stream and the Pauline-Gentile stream — both survived because both had institutional and theological profiles that did not threaten the post-70 imperial settlement. The diaspora messianic-Jewish stream that had been carried by James, by the Twelve, and by the Jerusalem community survived only as a memory and a body of traditions, sustained by the *Desposyni* in marginal communities under continuing imperial surveillance, eventually attested in Jewish-Christian groups such as the Ebionites and the Nazarenes that the proto-orthodox heresiologists treated as marginal. Step One is complete. Step Two — the editorial operation that converts the surviving textual substrate into the proto-orthodox canon — begins.

## 6. The Imperial Continuation: Surveillance and Control After 70

### 6.1 The Desposyni Hunt: Vespasian, Domitian, Trajan

The post-70 imperial concern with the Davidic line is the documented continuation of Step One. Eusebius *HE* 3.11–12, drawing on Hegesippus, records that Vespasian after the destruction of Jerusalem ordered "all who were of the family of David to be sought out, in order that no one of the royal race might be left among the Jews." The order is intelligible only as continuation of the political concern that produced the destruction of the Temple: the Davidic line is the line from which messianic claimants are drawn; the elimination of that line removes the political-religious resource on which a future revolt would depend. The order is also intelligible as a textbook Roman procedure following the methodical reduction. Carthage's destruction was followed by the dispersal of survivors and the suppression of any remnant capable of reconstituting the political identity. Numantia's destruction was followed by the enslavement of survivors. Jerusalem's destruction is followed by the targeted hunt for the line whose continued existence could carry the political claim forward. The procedure is consistent.

Eusebius *HE* 3.19–20 records the next phase under Domitian. Domitian, "in the same manner as Herod" (Eusebius's framing, designed to put Domitian in the role of the Herod of Matthew's nativity), ordered a search for descendants of Jude, the brother of Jesus. Two grandsons of Jude — named in some traditions Zoker and James — were brought to Rome, examined personally by Domitian, asked about their property, the size of their landholdings, and their financial means. They reported a small farm of 39 plethra, jointly owned, on which they paid tribute and worked with their hands. They showed Domitian their callused hands, the marks of agricultural labor. Asked about the kingdom of Christ, they reported it was not of this world. Domitian, satisfied that the descendants of Jude were politically harmless agricultural laborers, dismissed them. The account is preserved in Hegesippus and stylized in transmission, but the operational core is revealing. The imperial authority maintained a register of Davidic descendants of Jesus's family, examined them personally for political risk, and made disposition decisions on the basis of the examination. The procedure is exactly the procedure a Roman imperial intelligence operation would follow toward a population of political concern. It is the documented residue of an active surveillance.

Eusebius *HE* 3.32 records the third phase. Under Trajan, a generation later, the imperial procurator pursued descendants of David and members of the family of Jesus more aggressively. Symeon the son of Clopas — the kinsman of Jesus who had succeeded James as leader of the Jerusalem-Pella community — was crucified at advanced age (Hegesippus, with hagiographical exaggeration, says 120) on the dual charge of being a descendant of David and of being a Christian. The dual charge is the diagnostic. The imperial authority is not yet treating Christianity as a category distinct from Davidic-line Judaism; it is treating both as components of a single political risk. The crucifixion of Symeon — by crucifixion, the Roman political execution for claimants to disallowed kingship — places the Jamesian Jerusalem leadership inside the same political category in which Jesus had been placed seventy years earlier. The procedure is the same procedure: identify the leader of the line, examine, condemn, execute by the political-execution method. Across three reigns — Vespasian, Domitian, Trajan, spanning roughly 70 CE to 110 CE — the imperial concern with the Davidic line is continuous. The pursuit is not a peculiarity of any one emperor; it is the standing imperial posture toward a political category Rome had identified as a long-term operational concern.

### 6.2 The *Fiscus Judaicus*

The *Fiscus Judaicus* is the fiscal-administrative apparatus that converts the post-70 imperial concern from political pursuit into permanent infrastructure. After the destruction of the Temple, Vespasian redirected the half-shekel tax that Jewish men twenty and over had paid annually to the Temple, repurposing it as a tax payable to the Capitoline Jupiter in Rome (Josephus *War* 7.218; Cassius Dio 65.7). The political content of the redirection is unambiguous: the fiscal apparatus that had funded the Temple of YHWH now funds the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus. The Jewish God is fiscally subjugated to the Roman head god. The tax is collected from every Jewish male, throughout the empire, throughout life. The administrative apparatus required to identify, register, and collect from every Jewish male in the empire is itself a permanent surveillance instrument. The *Fiscus Judaicus*, in operation, is a register of Jews in the empire — and, because circumcision is the practical test of Jewish status, a register of any male within the empire's surveillance reach who lives a "Jewish life."

The Domitianic intensification of *Fiscus Judaicus* enforcement is the next escalation. Suetonius *Domitian* 12.2 records the moment as eyewitness testimony: *"praeter ceteros Iudaicus fiscus acerbissime actus est"* — "besides other taxes, the Jewish tax was levied with the utmost rigor" — and *"interfuisse me adulescentulum memini, cum a procuratore frequentissimoque consilio inspeceretur nonagenarius senex, an circumsectus esset"* — "I recall being present in my youth when an old man of ninety was examined by the procurator and a very crowded court, to see whether he was circumcised." The Suetonian account preserves the diagnostic detail. The imperial authority is no longer simply collecting from registered Jews; it is examining individuals to determine whether they belong to the registered category. The examination is invasive (physical inspection for circumcision), public (a crowded court), and applied to anyone whose status is in doubt. The political implication is that anyone living a "Jewish life" — including God-fearers, including Jewish-Christian believers who continued to observe Torah, including any Christian whose practice the Roman administration could not distinguish from Jewish practice — is exposed to the apparatus.

Cassius Dio 67.14 records the parallel intensification at the elite level. Domitian executed his own cousin Flavius Clemens and exiled Flavia Domitilla on the charge of "atheism" (ἀθεότης) — the Roman administrative category for failure to honor the gods of Rome — combined with the charge of having "drifted into Jewish ways." The Greek phrase preserves the ambiguity precisely. "Jewish ways" could mean Judaism proper, God-fearer status, or what we would today call Christianity; the Roman administrative apparatus did not yet make the distinction. The execution of a Flavian cousin and the exile of a Flavian niece on these charges shows the apparatus operating at the highest social level. Nerva's reform after Domitian's assassination — Cassius Dio 68.1, the Fiscus Judaicus coinage *FISCI IUDAICI CALUMNIA SUBLATA* (RIC II Nerva 58, 96 CE) — abolished not the tax itself but the *calumnia* (the abusive prosecution and informer apparatus) that had grown up around it under Domitian. The reform did not abolish the surveillance; it removed the most extreme features of its enforcement. The infrastructure remained. From 70 CE through the second century, the *Fiscus Judaicus* operated as a permanent imperial mechanism for identifying, registering, and managing Jews and those legally indistinguishable from Jews. The operational consequence for the Jesus movement is structural: Christian communities that wished to escape the *Fiscus Judaicus* apparatus had to be legally and visibly distinguishable from Jews. The Pauline-Gentile, Marcionite, and Valentinian configurations were. The Jamesian-Jewish-Christian configuration was not. The fiscal-administrative machinery thus exerted a permanent gravitational pressure favoring *every* configuration severable from Davidic-Torah-Jerusalem identity over the Jamesian one. This pressure did not, by itself, select between the Pauline-quietist, Marcionite-dualist, and Valentinian-pneumatic streams; that selection was performed later, by the proto-orthodox synthesis operating inside the institutional matrix the Pauline network had built.

### 6.3 The Post-135 Settlement: Aelia Capitolina and the Gentile Bishop

The Bar Kochba revolt of 132–135 CE and Hadrian's response complete the imperial reset. The revolt — led by Simon ben Kosiba, hailed as messiah (the name *Bar Kochba*, "son of the star," is the messianic title from Numbers 24:17 deployed by Rabbi Akiba) — was the second major Jewish uprising against Rome and was suppressed with massive military violence. Cassius Dio 69.13–14 records the toll: 580,000 Jews killed in combat, 985 villages destroyed, fifty fortified strongholds reduced. Hadrian's settlement was designed to prevent any third revolt in perpetuity. Jerusalem was renamed *Aelia Capitolina* (after Hadrian's gens Aelius and after Jupiter Capitolinus); the city was rebuilt as a Roman *colonia* with a temple to Jupiter Capitolinus on the Temple Mount; Jews were forbidden from entering the city under penalty of death; the province was renamed *Syria Palaestina* — Hadrian deliberately effacing the name *Iudaea* in favor of a name derived from the Philistines, the historical enemies of Israel. The political message was the political message of Carthage's salting and Numantia's demolition: the political identity of the rejected community is to be erased from the map.

The consequence for the Jerusalem community of the Jesus movement is the diagnostic. Eusebius *HE* 4.6 records that the bishopric of Jerusalem, which had passed from James to Symeon and through a succession of fifteen Jewish-Christian bishops — all of them, Eusebius notes, "of the circumcision" — was, after Hadrian's settlement, transferred to a Gentile, Marcus, who became the first Gentile bishop of Jerusalem (now Aelia Capitolina). The succession is documented in *HE* 4.5: fifteen Jewish-Christian bishops over roughly seventy years from James to the last, then a Gentile succession. The transition occurs in 135 CE because Jews — including Jewish-Christians — are barred from the city under Hadrian's edict. The Jamesian Jerusalem succession is therefore terminated not by theological dispute but by imperial edict. The Gentile-Christian bishopric that begins in 135 CE is the institutional configuration in which the Jerusalem community continues; the Davidic succession that had run from James through Symeon and onward is broken. The break is structural, imposed by the empire's settlement of the second Jewish revolt, and it is the operational moment at which the Jamesian-Davidic strand of the Jesus movement loses its institutional continuity with the Jerusalem center. The strand survives in the Ebionite, Nazarene, and Desposyni-affiliated communities outside Aelia Capitolina, but the institutional center is permanently removed from Jewish-Christian hands. The post-135 settlement is therefore the second of the empire's two formal removals of Jewish institutional continuity (the first was the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE), and the Gentile-Christian episcopate that emerges in its wake is the institutional vehicle within which the proto-orthodox synthesis develops across the second and third centuries.

### 6.4 Pliny–Trajan: Institutional Management of Christians

The Pliny–Trajan correspondence (Pliny *Epistles* 10.96–97), composed in Bithynia–Pontus around 112 CE, is the documented baseline of Roman institutional management of Christianity once Christianity has become legally distinguishable from Judaism. Pliny, governor of the province, writes to Trajan asking for procedural guidance on how to handle Christian cases. He has not previously been involved in such cases (*"cognitionibus de christianis interfui numquam"*) and is uncertain whether the *nomen* itself ("the name") or only the offenses associated with it (*flagitia cohaerentia nomini*) are punishable. He has been processing accusations as they arrive, executing those who confess and refuse to recant, sending Roman citizens to Rome for trial, releasing those who recant by cursing Christ (*maledicerent Christo*) and offering wine and incense to Trajan's statue. He has investigated through interrogation of two female deaconesses (*ministrae*) under torture and reports that the Christians appeared to him to be guilty only of "depraved and excessive superstition" (*superstitionem pravam et immodicam*) involving meeting before dawn to sing hymns to Christ "as to a god" (*quasi deo*) and binding themselves by oath not to commit theft, robbery, or adultery and to keep faith on deposit. He asks Trajan how to proceed.

Trajan's rescript is the institutional ruling. *Conquirendi non sunt* — "they are not to be sought out." If accused and convicted, they are to be punished, but with the proviso that one who recants by sacrificing to the Roman gods is to be pardoned. *Sine auctore vero propositi libelli nullo crimine locum habere debent* — "anonymous accusations are to have no place in any prosecution, for they are both a precedent of the worst kind and not in keeping with the spirit of our age." The rescript establishes the Roman institutional posture toward Christianity that obtains, with variations, from Trajan to Decius (250 CE): Christians are not to be hunted; if denounced, they are to be tested by the loyalty test (cursing Christ, sacrificing to the imperial cult); if they pass, released; if they fail, executed. The procedure is the standard *cognitio extra ordinem* of Roman provincial judicial management. The political content is the standard Roman content: a cult that maintains its own assemblies, swears its own oaths, and refuses imperial cult honors is a political risk that the imperial authority manages but does not eradicate.

The diagnostic content of the Pliny–Trajan exchange for the present argument lies in the observation that the institutional management of Christians at this moment is conducted as a continuation of the Roman state's standing posture toward unauthorized assemblies — the posture documented in the *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* of 186 BCE and applied repeatedly across the intervening three centuries. The Pliny–Trajan procedure is the application of that posture to a cult that has become large and institutionally distinct enough to be managed as a separate category but that is still operating within the framework of Roman provincial judicial administration. The Christians at this stage are not being administered as a state-supported religion; they are being managed as a tolerated *superstitio* against which the state acts only when factional pressure produces denunciations. The state does not write their texts; the state does not direct their bishops; the state does not produce their canon. The state monitors, manages, and selectively prunes. The Christian leadership across this period operates inside this configuration. The textual outputs of the Christian leadership across this period must be read as outputs produced inside this configuration. Section 8 will examine those outputs.

## 7. The Pre-Positioned Vessel: The Pauline Network Within the Diverse Surviving Landscape

### 7.0 The Diverse Post-70 Landscape

Before profiling the Pauline network it is necessary to insist on what the surviving landscape actually contained. The conventional picture — in which the post-70 environment is reduced to "Pauline Christianity" tracking neatly toward the Nicene synthesis — is the projection of the fourth-century victor's self-image back onto a second-century reality that was nothing of the kind. Bauer's *Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei* (1934) demonstrated, and the post-Bauer scholarly consensus (Helmut Köster, James M. Robinson, Birger Pearson, Bart Ehrman, Karen King) has consolidated rather than overturned, the position that the second-century Christian landscape was a polycentric plurality and that "orthodoxy" is a retrospective construct.

In Egypt, the documentary record from the apostolic period to Bishop Demetrius (189 CE) shows essentially no proto-orthodox literary or institutional footprint. The dominant Christian intellectuals of Alexandria across the first century and a half of Egyptian Christianity were Basilides, Valentinus, and Carpocrates. The earliest known Egyptian gospel is the *Gospel of the Egyptians*, an Encratite-pneumatic text alien to later canonical Christianity. The papyrological record (Colin Roberts, *Manuscript, Society and Belief in Early Christian Egypt*) supports the inference. The Demetrian episcopate inaugurates, rather than continues, the proto-orthodox institutional configuration in Egypt.

In Edessa and the Syriac East, the inversion is sharper. Bauer's reconstruction — confirmed by Han Drijvers (*Cults and Beliefs at Edessa*) and the *Chronicle of Edessa* — is that the Marcionites and Bardaisanites were the mainstream Christian community throughout the second century. The proto-orthodox were the late-arriving sectarian minority called "Palutians" after their first locally-ordained bishop, Palut, who traveled to Antioch around 200 CE to receive ordination from Serapion (himself in communion with Rome). The Marcionites held the unmarked title of "Christians" because they had been there first.

The theological landscape was equally fragmented. The Pauline corpus's earliest and most rigorous interpreters were Marcion (who built his entire canon on ten Pauline epistles plus the *Evangelion*) and the Valentinian school (which traced its lineage from Paul through Theudas and produced the most sophisticated extant exegesis of his letters; Pagels, *The Gnostic Paul*, 1975). Justin Martyr, writing in Rome around 150–160 CE, never names Paul or quotes his epistles — because in mid-second-century Rome, Paul was the apostle of the heterodox. Irenaeus, around 180 CE, was the first proto-orthodox author to mount a sustained campaign to *reclaim* Paul from the Marcionites and Valentinians, a campaign continued by Tertullian's *Adversus Marcionem* and consolidated by the proto-orthodox reception of the Pastorals as authentically Pauline. The Pauline corpus, in itself, did not specify a proto-orthodox outcome; it was equally hospitable to Marcionite, Valentinian, and proto-orthodox readings, and the question of which reading would prevail was an open and contested question across the second and third centuries.

The cosmological-ethical axis between the streams was also real and theologically substantive, not merely a difference in emphasis. The Pauline-Marcionite-Valentinian stream understood the *kosmos* as a hostile arena — the rule of "the god of this world" (2 Cor 4:4), "the rulers of this age" (1 Cor 2:8), the "elemental spirits" (*stoicheia*, Gal 4:3), and the verdict that "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Cor 15:50). The Jamesian stream understood the world as the arena of righteous works and economic justice, where the rich are denounced (James 5:1–6), faith without works is dead (James 2:17), and salvation is enacted through ethical obedience inside creation rather than through pneumatic escape from it. J. Louis Martyn (*Galatians*, Anchor Bible 1997) reads Paul's apocalyptic cosmology as an inherited two-age structure in which the present age is ruled by hostile cosmic forces; Robert Eisenman and Dale Allison read James as a Wisdom-and-creation tradition that locates redemption inside the world. These are not minor differences of accent. They are two structurally different cosmologies attached to two structurally different communities, and the proto-orthodox synthesis that eventually emerged had to construct an artificial peace between them by relegating James to low canonical weight, marginalizing the Jewish-Christian remnant, and silently editing the Marcionite and Valentinian readings of Paul out of the official tradition.

With this diverse landscape established, the question becomes: *within* this plurality, what configuration was Rome's gravitational field favoring, and *which* surviving stream furnished the institutional matrix into which the proto-orthodox synthesis was eventually poured? The answer, on the demographic and institutional side, is the Pauline network. The remainder of Section 7 profiles that network's structural fitness for the role. Section 8 then traces how the proto-orthodox synthesis, operating across the full diverse surviving field, performed the editorial operation that produced the canonical New Testament.

### 7.1 Demographic Profile

The Pauline network of communities — established across the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean from approximately 49 CE through the early 60s CE, in cities including Antioch, Tarsus, Ephesus, Corinth, Philippi, Thessalonica, Galatia, Colossae, Laodicea, and Rome itself — was demographically distinct from the Jamesian Jerusalem community in ways that map directly onto compatibility with Roman provincial administration. The network was urban (the Jamesian movement was urban-Jerusalem and rural-Galilean); the network was diaspora-Jewish-and-Gentile (the Jamesian movement was Judean-Jewish); the network was Greek-speaking (the Jamesian movement was Aramaic-and-Hebrew with Greek as a secondary register); the network was theologically organized around a Christ-figure who could be apprehended by non-Jews without prior Torah-observance (the Jamesian movement was theologically organized around a Davidic-messianic Jesus whose followers maintained Torah-observance); the network's institutional spine was the household of the patron-host (the Jamesian movement's institutional spine was the apostolic-elder-Twelve framework around James). The structural differences are not theological accidents; they are the differences between a movement located inside the Jerusalem religious-political configuration and a movement located inside the Greco-Roman urban-civic configuration. Each movement is shaped by the institutional matrix in which it operates.

The genuine Pauline letters (1 Thessalonians, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, Philemon, Romans) — the seven letters whose Pauline authorship is accepted across the spectrum of modern critical scholarship — preserve the demographic profile of the network with documentary precision. The named figures include Erastus the city treasurer (*oikonomos tēs poleōs*) of Corinth (Romans 16:23), Phoebe the deacon-patron of Cenchreae who carries the Romans letter to Rome and is described as *prostatis* — patron — of Paul and many others (Romans 16:1–2), Stephanas the householder whose family represents the first converts of Achaia (1 Corinthians 16:15), Crispus the synagogue ruler at Corinth (1 Corinthians 1:14), Aquila and Priscilla the artisan-tentmakers expelled from Rome under Claudius (Acts 18:2; cf. Romans 16:3), Lydia the dealer in purple cloth at Philippi (Acts 16:14), Onesiphorus and his household at Ephesus (2 Timothy 1:16, deutero-Pauline but reflecting tradition), the household of Stephanas at Corinth, the household of Aristobulus and the household of Narcissus at Rome (Romans 16:10–11). The recurring institutional unit is the household — *oikos*, *domus* — whose head ("master of the house," *oikodespotēs*) provides the meeting space, the patronage, and the social legitimation for the local community. The recurring economic profile is the urban artisan-merchant-civic-functionary middle stratum: tentmakers, purple-cloth dealers, city treasurers, synagogue rulers. This is the demographic out of which the Pauline communities are made. It is not the demographic of the urban poor (although urban poor are present); it is not the demographic of the senatorial aristocracy (although Romans 16:23 *Erastus the city treasurer* shows the network reaching civic-magisterial level). It is the demographic that scholars working from the empire side describe as the *plebs media* of the imperial city — the productive middle stratum that runs the empire's commercial, administrative, and artisanal life.

This demographic profile is exactly the demographic profile that aligned with imperial stability across the empire. The network was distributed across major imperial cities; its members were taxpayers, citizens of multiple cities, integrated into civic structures (city treasurer, synagogue ruler), economically productive (artisans, merchants, householders), and legally distinguishable from the rural Jewish populations of Judea and Galilee that the Roman authority found most difficult to manage. The network was, in its institutional and demographic profile, a network of imperial-compatible Christ-believing communities, even before any specific theological or political position was articulated. The compatibility was structural, not ideological. The implications of the structural compatibility for the network's textual outputs follow.

### 7.2 Romans 13 and the Politics of Quietism

Romans 13:1–7 — Paul's instruction that "every soul be subject to the higher powers, for there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God" (Romans 13:1, KJV) — is the most consequential single passage in the New Testament for the political configuration of the surviving Christian movement. The passage instructs the Roman Christian community to be subject to the imperial authority, to pay taxes (*phoros*), to pay tribute (*telos*), and to render fear and honor to those to whom they are due. The political content is unambiguous: the Pauline instruction is for political quietism toward the imperial authority. The instruction is given in a letter written to Roman Christians around 56–58 CE, when Nero was already emperor (since 54 CE) and when the Pauline network was in active competition with the Jamesian Jerusalem community for the theological-institutional definition of the Jesus movement.

The strategic significance of Romans 13 is that it is the canonical passage that hardwires political quietism into the surviving textual substrate. After 70 CE, when the Jamesian alternative has been destroyed at the institutional level, Romans 13 is the passage that the proto-orthodox tradition will repeatedly invoke as the Christian political baseline. From 1 Clement (c. 96 CE) prayer for the imperial rulers (1 Clem. 60–61), through Polycarp's *Letter to the Philippians*, through Justin's *First Apology*, through Tertullian's *Apologeticum*, through Origen's *Contra Celsum*, the Pauline instruction is the sustained reference point for Christian political-loyal posture toward the empire. Modern scholars have variously read Romans 13 as Pauline pragmatism toward a particular Roman crisis, as Pauline accommodation to imperial reality, as Pauline eschatological framing (the imperial authority will pass when Christ returns), or as a deutero-Pauline interpolation. The interpolation theory has not gained majority acceptance; the passage is in every significant manuscript witness to Romans, including P46. The passage is therefore Pauline; the passage is therefore the basis of the Christian political-quietist tradition; the passage is therefore the textual foundation of the structural compatibility between the surviving Christian network and the imperial authority.

The contrast with the Jamesian-tradition Letter of James is the diagnostic. James 5:1–6 contains a stinging denunciation of the wealthy: "Go to now, ye rich men, weep and howl for your miseries that shall come upon you. Your riches are corrupted, and your garments are motheaten... Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth" (James 5:1–4, KJV). The passage is socially and politically incendiary in a way that nothing in the Pauline corpus is. The wealthy aristocracy is denounced; their fraud is condemned; the cries of the defrauded laborers ascend to God. The passage operates within the prophetic-Hebrew tradition of social-justice denunciation (Amos, Isaiah). It is not the kind of text that produces a network compatible with imperial stability; it is the kind of text that produces a network of poor agricultural laborers and small-holders who recognize the wealthy as the political enemy. The James strand and the Romans 13 strand are not two complementary expressions of one theological tradition; they are two structurally different political-economic positions, attached to two structurally different demographic configurations. The Roman political configuration favored the Romans 13 strand. The Jewish War destroyed the Jamesian institutional matrix. The strand that survived in the institutional center of the surviving movement was the Romans 13 strand.

### 7.3 The Patron Demographic Inscribed in the Texts

The Pauline corpus inscribes its patron demographic into the texts in ways that make the structural compatibility with imperial stability visible. The household codes (*Haustafeln*) embedded in the deutero-Pauline letters — Colossians 3:18–4:1, Ephesians 5:21–6:9, the Pastorals 1 Timothy 2:8–15, 6:1–2, Titus 2:1–10, with parallels in 1 Peter 2:13–3:7 and the *Didache* 4:9–11 — instruct wives to submit to husbands, children to obey parents, slaves to obey masters, and (in 1 Peter and the Pastorals) believers to honor the imperial authority. The genre is well-documented in Greco-Roman ethical literature; Aristotle's *Politics* I.5–13 and Stoic-philosophical handbooks on household management provide the form. The Pauline-tradition adaptation of the genre, deployed across the deutero-Pauline corpus, is the Christian community's adoption of the dominant cultural form of household-political organization. The adoption is the institutional match between the Christian community and the surrounding Greco-Roman civic order. The Christian household is a *domus* operating on the same lines as the Greco-Roman *domus*; the Christian community is a network of households operating on the same lines as the network of households that produces the empire's middling civic life.

The Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus) — composed in the late first or early second century CE in Paul's name — extend the institutional match. The Pastorals lay out the criteria for bishops and deacons, criteria that map onto the criteria for civic magistrates and city councillors of the same period: a bishop is to be the husband of one wife (*mias gunaikos andra*, 1 Timothy 3:2) — the Roman ideal of *univira* / *univir* respectability — manage his own household well, be temperate, hospitable, not given to drink or violence, of good repute with outsiders. The criteria reproduce the *honestiores*-class virtues that qualify a man for civic office in any imperial city. The Pastoral church is therefore being institutionally configured to match the surrounding civic order in which its leaders will be visible, respected, and politically unproblematic. The structural compatibility is being engineered into the institution. The text-critical implications are obvious: a movement whose internal documents instruct its members in this way is a movement whose textual outputs cannot be organized around political confrontation with the imperial authority. The Pauline-tradition demographic inscribes itself in the Pauline-tradition texts; the texts then become the constitutional documents of the surviving institutional movement.

### 7.4 Why This Network Was the Right Local Actor

The convergence of demographic profile, theological flexibility (Christ figure apprehensible by Gentiles without Torah-observance), institutional form (household-network across major imperial cities), and political posture (Romans 13 quietism) made the Pauline network the local actor through which the imperial-compatible outcome was institutionalized. The proposition is not that Rome selected this network in the modern sense of state direction, nor that Paul was a Roman agent in any conspiratorial sense. The proposition is that the empire's gravitational field — the documented operating procedure of Section 3, the post-70 surveillance and management apparatus of Section 6 — exerted continuous pressure favoring networks with the Pauline profile and disadvantaging networks with the Jamesian profile. The Jamesian movement, located in Jerusalem, organized around Davidic-messianic theology, operating within Second Temple Judaism, and economically tied to a Judean agricultural-pious base, was structurally exposed to the operations of 62–73 CE and 132–135 CE. The Pauline movement, distributed across imperial cities, organized around Christ-mysticism intelligible to Gentiles, operating outside Second Temple Judaism, and economically tied to the urban *plebs media*, was structurally insulated from the same operations. When Step One destroyed the Jerusalem center, the surviving substrate was *not* the Pauline substrate alone; it was the diverse polycentric field profiled in §7.0. But within that field, the Pauline-network *configuration* — not the Pauline theological corpus, which Marcion and Valentinus owned as authentically as anyone — was uniquely fitted to provide the *institutional matrix* into which a proto-orthodox synthesis could later be poured. The matrix was the network of urban household-based communities distributed across imperial cities, governed by *honestiores*-class virtues legible to civic Rome, organized by household codes that mirrored the Greco-Roman *domus*, instructed by the Pastorals to qualify their leaders by criteria coterminous with civic-magisterial criteria, and legitimated by Romans 13's hardwired political quietism. The next operational question — how the proto-orthodox synthesis emerged across this matrix, *domesticated* the Pauline corpus against its Marcionite and Valentinian inheritors, *captured* the Johannine witness, *marginalized* the Jamesian remnant, and *destroyed* the Marcionite and Manichaean alternatives — is the question of Step Two.

The British East India Company analogy introduced in Section 2.2 is illuminating here. The Company was not the Crown; the Company's directors were not Crown agents in any conspiratorial sense; the Company's operations were not directed from Whitehall in the modern bureaucratic sense. But the Company operated within the gravitational field of British imperial interest, was structured demographically and institutionally to be compatible with that interest, and over the course of two centuries became the institutional vehicle through which British imperial control of the Indian subcontinent was achieved — at which point the Crown formally absorbed the Company in 1858. The 1858 absorption was the formal ratification of a 200-year-running operational relationship. Constantine's 313 Edict of Milan and his subsequent patronage of the Christian church is the Roman empire's analogous formal ratification of what had been a 250-year-running operational relationship between the empire's gravitational field and the *proto-orthodox synthesis* that had crystallized inside the Pauline network's institutional matrix across the second and third centuries. The Pauline-network matrix was the local institutional vehicle; the proto-orthodox synthesis (Justin → Irenaeus → Tertullian → Hippolytus → Origen → Eusebius) was the doctrinal-editorial program that crystallized inside it; the empire was the gravitational mass; the trajectory was the convergence; the Constantinian moment was the ratification. The textual operation that produced the canon was conducted by the proto-orthodox synthesis operating across the full diverse surviving field, from inside the institutional matrix the Pauline network had built. Section 8 examines that operation across the corpus.

## 8. Step Two: The Editorial Operation Across the Surviving Movement

### 8.1 The Scope of the Operation

The editorial operation that produced the proto-orthodox canon was not a single event, not a single editor, not a single decade, and not a single doctrinal decision. The operation was a distributed, multi-generational, multi-locational, multi-strategy process conducted across the surviving Christian network from approximately 70 CE to approximately 367 CE, with the Constantinian period (313–367 CE) operating as the ratification phase rather than the productive phase. The operation's distributed character is essential to understanding its imperial-compatible signature. The fact that it is distributed across communities, generations, and locations does not undermine the convergent outcome; it reveals that the gravitational field operating on the network was stable and consistent enough to produce convergent outcomes across the field's full scope. A single central editor could have produced a single canon; the operation that actually produced the canon involved Mark in Rome or Antioch around 70 CE, Matthew in Antioch or Damascus around 80–90 CE, Luke-Acts somewhere in the Aegean basin around 90–110 CE, John in Ephesus around 90–100 CE, the Pastorals in the Aegean basin around 100–125 CE, Hebrews somewhere in the Italo-Aegean field around 80–100 CE, the Petrine epistles around 100–125 CE, the Catholic epistles across the second century, the Apostolic Fathers across approximately 95–150 CE, the apologists across approximately 130–200 CE, the heresiologists from Justin to Eusebius across approximately 150–325 CE, and the canon-shaping councils and lists from the Muratorian Fragment (c. 170 CE?) through Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (367 CE).

The scope is not only the canonical texts. The operation includes the active management of competing textual streams — the texts that did not enter the canon, the communities that did not survive into the proto-orthodox synthesis, the readings that were excluded and the manuscripts that were destroyed or buried. The operation includes the deutero-Pauline production (Colossians, Ephesians, 2 Thessalonians, the Pastorals) that extends the Pauline corpus in directions compatible with the proto-orthodox synthesis. The operation includes the framing apparatus — the prologues, the captions, the headings, the manuscript-tradition decisions — that direct the reader's interpretation of the texts. The operation includes the heresiological corpus (Justin's *Syntagma*, Irenaeus's *Adversus Haereses*, Hippolytus's *Refutatio*, Tertullian's *Praescriptio*, Epiphanius's *Panarion*) that defines the interpretive boundary by enumerating, characterizing, and refuting the alternatives. The operation's scope is therefore not limited to the production of canonical texts but encompasses the entire editorial-curatorial-interpretive apparatus by which the surviving textual configuration was shaped, presented, and stabilized. The signature of the operation is the convergent outcome across this distributed field.

### 8.2 The Differentiation Principle

The operation does not deploy a single strategy across all texts. The proto-orthodox network handles different texts differently depending on the text's compatibility with the imperial-compatible-orthodox synthesis. The differentiation principle — that handling varies systematically with content — is the diagnostic that identifies the operation as a coherent operation rather than a collection of unrelated editorial decisions. Six handling strategies can be distinguished, each applied where appropriate to the text in question.

**Write fresh.** Texts whose content can be composed from scratch in a form fully compatible with the synthesis are produced as proto-orthodox documents in their initial composition. Mark (the inaugural Gospel narrative), the deutero-Pauline letters (Colossians, Ephesians, 2 Thessalonians, the Pastorals), Acts (the Pauline-friendly history of the apostolic age), and several Catholic epistles fall into this category. The text enters the corpus already shaped to the desired profile.

**Smooth.** Texts whose underlying substrate carries Jamesian, Jewish-Christian, or otherwise problematic content but that can be redacted, expanded, or recontextualized to make the problematic content compatible are subjected to redactional smoothing. Matthew (with its Jewish-Christian substrate redacted into a proto-orthodox profile), 1–2 Peter (the Jamesian-Petrine tradition repackaged), the Pastorals (whether seen as fresh composition or as smoothing of a Pauline epistolary substrate) fall here. The text enters the corpus with its problematic features muted.

**Capture.** Texts whose substrate is independent of both Pauline and Jamesian streams but whose theological content can be incorporated by appropriate framing are captured into the proto-orthodox synthesis. The Gospel of John (whose Logos theology and Beloved Disciple tradition are independent of the Synoptic stream) and Hebrews (whose Levitical-priestly Christology is independent of both Synoptic and Pauline traditions) are captured by inclusion in the canon and by interpretive framing through Irenaean and Eusebian apparatus.

**Admit at low canonical weight.** Texts whose Jamesian or Jewish-Christian content is too prominent to be smoothed but whose apostolic provenance makes outright exclusion politically difficult are admitted to the corpus at low canonical weight, in the *Antilegomena* category, with their problematic content marginalized by limited circulation, deferred citation, and structural placement. James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Revelation (in the Eastern tradition), and arguably the Apocalypse fall into this category. The text is included but its canonical weight is contained.

**Marginalize.** Texts produced by communities that fall outside the proto-orthodox synthesis but that continue to carry apostolic or Davidic claims — the Jewish-Christian Gospels (Hebrews, Nazarenes, Ebionites), the Desposyni traditions, the Aramaic logia traditions — are marginalized by labeling, circulation restriction, refusal to copy, and ultimately by demographic absorption or extinction of the carrier communities. The texts survive only as fragments quoted by hostile heresiologists.

**Destroy or bury.** Texts whose content is fundamentally incompatible with the proto-orthodox synthesis and whose carrier communities have institutional weight requiring active suppression — Marcion's *Evangelion*, the Gospel of Thomas, the Manichaean corpus — are subjected to active suppression: condemnation in heresiological corpus, refusal to copy, imperial proscription where available, eventual physical destruction or burial. The texts survive only as polemically quoted fragments (Marcion, reconstructible from Tertullian and Epiphanius), as Coptic translation buried in late antiquity (the Nag Hammadi codices, buried c. 367 CE), or as Manichaean codices preserved at the imperial periphery (the Turfan and Medinet Madi finds).

The differentiation pattern is exactly the pattern that the imperial-patronage hypothesis predicts. Texts compatible with the synthesis are admitted; texts partially compatible are smoothed or admitted at low weight; texts incompatible are marginalized or destroyed. The handling of any given text is exactly the handling that the text's location in the imperial-compatibility field would predict. The pattern fits across the entire textual corpus, not merely across the canonical four. Sections 8.3 through 8.8 examine the specific cases.

### 8.3 Foundational Compositions: Mark, the Deutero-Pauline Corpus, Acts

Mark is the foundational composition of the operation. Composed in or near 70 CE, plausibly in Rome (where second-century tradition associated it with Peter's interpreter, though the geographic setting could equally be Antioch or any major imperial city outside Palestine), Mark establishes the Gospel-as-narrative-genre that the subsequent compositions will adapt. Several features of Mark fit the imperial-compatible profile precisely. The Roman centurion at the cross is the figure who first articulates Jesus's divine sonship (Mark 15:39) — not a Jewish disciple, not a Davidic kinsman, but a Roman military officer. The chief priests, scribes, and elders are presented as the antagonists; Pilate is presented as reluctant, manipulated by the Jewish authorities. The disciples are presented as failing, fleeing, denying. The crowd in Jerusalem turns against Jesus, demanding Barabbas. The political configuration is one in which the Jewish religious-political establishment is the primary antagonist, the Roman political authority is reluctantly drawn in, and the Roman common soldier is the figure of Christological recognition. This is not the political configuration the Jamesian movement would have produced. It is the political configuration the Pauline-imperial-compatible network would have produced. Mark also famously ends, in the earliest manuscript witnesses (B, ℵ), at 16:8 — the women fleeing the empty tomb in fear. The "longer ending" of Mark (16:9–20) is a second-century addition. The original Gospel does not contain a triumphant resurrection scene that confirms the apostolic Twelve as the authoritative continuators; the original Gospel ends with the disciples fleeing and the women silenced. The Markan ending leaves the apostolic-Twelve tradition open to Pauline-Petrine reconfiguration. The configuration that emerges in Acts is the reconfiguration the open ending invited.

The deutero-Pauline corpus — Colossians (likely deutero-Pauline; modern critical scholarship is approximately evenly divided), Ephesians (broadly deutero-Pauline), 2 Thessalonians (disputed), the Pastorals (overwhelmingly deutero-Pauline) — extends Paul's authentic letters into directions Paul himself did not articulate. The Christological cosmology of Colossians 1:15–20, the institutional ecclesiology of Ephesians, the eschatological tempering of 2 Thessalonians, and the bishop-deacon structures of the Pastorals together construct the institutional Pauline tradition that becomes the proto-orthodox foundation. Each document is composed in Paul's name, with care to imitate Paul's style and to address scenarios Paul might plausibly have addressed, and each document moves the Pauline tradition in a specific direction: cosmic Christ (Colossians); universal church as cosmic body (Ephesians); restraint on imminent eschatology (2 Thessalonians); episcopal-presbyteral institutional order (Pastorals). The cumulative effect is the construction of an institutional Paul whose authority underwrites the proto-orthodox church order. The genuine Paul of the seven undisputed letters is too charismatic, too eschatologically immediate, and too institutionally fluid to ground the second-century episcopal order. The deutero-Pauline corpus produces the institutional Paul that can.

Acts is the historiographic instrument that integrates the Pauline tradition into the apostolic narrative. The book's literary plan — Jerusalem to Rome, the spread of the gospel from the Jewish capital to the imperial capital, James and Peter at the start, Paul at the end — establishes the institutional shape of the surviving movement: Jerusalem origin, Petrine apostolic foundation, Pauline imperial-Gentile expansion. The Jamesian Jerusalem leadership is depicted at the Apostolic Council (Acts 15) as endorsing Paul's Gentile mission with conditions that map onto Noachide observances rather than full Torah; the depiction systematically softens the Galatians-attested conflict between Paul and the Jerusalem leadership over Torah-observance for Gentile Christians. James is depicted as a Torah-pious, decisive, moderate leader — a figure compatible with the proto-orthodox synthesis rather than the more apocalyptic, more Davidic-messianic figure the Hegesippan and Ebionite traditions remember. Paul's three trials (before the Sanhedrin, before Felix and Festus, before Agrippa) systematically present the Roman authorities as just, the Jewish authorities as malicious, and Paul as a Roman citizen entitled to imperial appellate jurisdiction. The book's final image — Paul preaching the kingdom of God in Rome, "with all confidence, no man forbidding him" (Acts 28:31) — closes the narrative on the Pauline-imperial axis. The Lukan author has produced the historiographic instrument that gives the institutional Paul his apostolic genealogy and the surviving movement its imperial geographic destination.

### 8.4 Smoothing the Alienated Audiences: Matthew, the Pastorals, 1–2 Peter

Matthew is the proto-orthodox synthesis's smoothing operation on the Jamesian-Jewish-Christian substrate. Composed in Antioch or somewhere in the Syrian-Palestinian periphery in the 80s or 90s CE, Matthew incorporates a substantial Jewish-Christian substrate (the Sermon on the Mount, the antitheses of Matthew 5, the prominence of the Law's continuation in Matthew 5:17–20, the Davidic genealogy of Matthew 1, the *Emmanuel* prophecy, the Magi narrative grounded in Hebrew prophetic-messianic vocabulary) and redacts it into a form compatible with the developing proto-orthodox synthesis. The Petrine commission of Matthew 16:17–19 — "thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church" — is unique to Matthew within the canonical four and is the textual foundation for the Petrine succession that the Roman bishopric will claim. The Matthean apocalyptic discourse (Matthew 24–25), the Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20 with its trinitarian baptismal formula), and the Matthean ecclesial discourse (Matthew 18) together construct the Gospel that bridges the Jewish-Christian substrate and the Gentile-imperial-compatible synthesis. Matthew's Jewish-Christian credibility (the Davidic genealogy, the prominence of the Law) gives the Gospel its leverage in the Jewish-Christian audience; Matthew's proto-orthodox markers (the Petrine commission, the trinitarian formula, the universal mission) give the Gospel its position within the synthesis. The smoothing operation is the construction of a single text that does both jobs at once.

The Pastoral Epistles, treated above (Section 7.3) for their household-code content, function in the differentiation principle as the smoothing of Pauline charismatic-fluid leadership into proto-orthodox episcopal order. The genuine Paul did not require bishops; the genuine Paul recognized prophets, teachers, apostles, evangelists, and a fluid charismatic order. The Pastorals construct a Pauline tradition in which the bishop is the constitutional officer, the presbyters are the local council, the deacons are the institutional support, and the women are silenced (1 Tim. 2:11–15). The Pastorals smooth the fluid Pauline tradition into the rigid Pauline tradition that can ground second-century episcopal claims. The smoothing is aimed at Pauline-tradition audiences who would have inherited the more fluid Paul; the Pastorals provide the textual foundation for the institutional Paul those audiences are now expected to recognize.

1 Peter and 2 Peter constitute the smoothing of the Petrine tradition into compatibility with the synthesis. 1 Peter, addressed to "the elect strangers scattered" across Asia Minor (1 Pet. 1:1) — Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia — is composed in elegant Greek that the historical Galilean fisherman would not have written; the modern critical consensus is that 1 Peter is pseudonymous, composed in the late first or early second century. The letter's content — household codes (1 Pet. 2:18–3:7), instruction to honor the emperor (1 Pet. 2:13–17), exhortation to suffering with Christ-like patience (1 Pet. 2:21–25) — places the Petrine tradition firmly inside the proto-orthodox configuration. The Petrine tradition that the historical Peter carried was Jamesian-adjacent — Peter is the figure who at Antioch, in Galatians 2:11–14, withdraws from table fellowship with Gentiles under pressure from "men from James." 1 Peter erases this conflict. The Petrine tradition becomes a Pauline-compatible tradition, hardwired into a letter circulated across Asia Minor. 2 Peter, even more transparently pseudonymous (it explicitly cites the Pauline letters as scripture in 2 Pet. 3:15–16, a reference impossible for the historical Peter), and admitted to the canon with notable hesitation (Eusebius classifies it among the *antilegomena* in *HE* 3.25), completes the Petrine smoothing. The historical Peter is replaced by the institutional Peter; the institutional Peter underwrites the proto-orthodox synthesis.

### 8.5 Capturing Independent Witnesses: John, Hebrews

The Gospel of John is the most theologically independent of the canonical four. Composed in Ephesus around 90–100 CE, attributed to the Beloved Disciple, John presents a Logos Christology (John 1:1–18), a high pre-existence Christology absent from the Synoptic Gospels, a different chronology of Jesus's ministry (multiple Passover visits to Jerusalem rather than the single visit of the Synoptics), a different account of the cleansing of the Temple (early in Jesus's ministry rather than at its climax), and a different theology of the resurrection (the Spirit imparted to the disciples on the evening of resurrection day in John 20:22, prior to any forty-day post-resurrection ministry or ascension). The Johannine community's institutional position is not the position of either the Pauline-Petrine or the Jamesian streams; the Beloved Disciple is repeatedly contrasted with Peter (John 13:23, 20:2–10, 21:7, 21:20–23), and the Gospel's farewell discourses (John 13–17) construct a community oriented around the indwelling Paraclete rather than around episcopal succession.

The proto-orthodox synthesis does not attempt to suppress or marginalize John. The synthesis captures John, by including the Gospel in the canon and by interpretive framing through Irenaeus and the second-century theological tradition. Irenaeus's defense of the four-Gospel canon at *Adversus Haereses* 3.11.8 — "since there are four zones of the world... and four principal winds... it is fitting that the Word... should have given us the Gospel under four aspects" — is the framing that integrates John alongside the Synoptics. The Johannine community had its own theological idiom, but the community's rejection of certain proto-Gnostic readings of John (the First Epistle of John polemicizes against Christ-deniers and against those who reject the flesh) made the community's text recoverable for the synthesis. Capture rather than marginalization is the appropriate strategy when the text's Christology is high enough to be useful for the proto-orthodox case against adoptionism, when the text's institutional content is fluid enough to be reframed, and when the text's community is small enough to absorb. John is the textbook capture.

Hebrews is the parallel capture. Composed by an unknown author (the church father Origen famously wrote that "God only knows" who wrote Hebrews), Hebrews is theologically idiosyncratic: a sustained Levitical-priestly Christology framing Christ as the high priest after the order of Melchizedek, an exegesis of the Hebrew sacrificial system as foreshadowing Christ's once-for-all sacrifice, and a warning against apostasy that the proto-orthodox tradition will repeatedly find difficult (Hebrews 6:4–6, 10:26–31). Authorship was disputed throughout the second and third centuries; Eusebius (*HE* 3.25) classifies Hebrews's status as ambiguous in the Western canon while accepted in the Eastern. The eventual canonical inclusion of Hebrews — by attribution to Paul (probably incorrectly) and by the prestige of its Christological exposition — is the second textbook capture. The text is theologically independent; the text is too prestigious and too useful to discard; the text is captured by inclusion in the canon, by attribution to Paul, and by interpretive framing through the proto-orthodox theological tradition.

### 8.6 Admitted at Low Canonical Weight: James, Jude, the Antilegomena

The Letter of James is the test case for "admit at low canonical weight" handling. The letter is attributed to James the brother of Jesus, the leader of the Jerusalem community executed in 62 CE. The letter's content — the denunciation of the wealthy (James 5:1–6, treated above), the assertion that "faith without works is dead" (James 2:14–26), the criticism of partiality toward the rich in assemblies (James 2:1–7), the eschatological imminence with the Lord's coming "drawing nigh" (James 5:7–9) — operates within a Jamesian-Jewish-Christian configuration that sits awkwardly with the Pauline configuration. James 2:14–26 contains the famous passage on faith and works that has been read since Luther as a direct contradiction of Paul's Romans 3–4 doctrine of justification by faith. Modern scholarship has nuanced this confrontation, but the textual content remains: James insists on works as the validation of faith, and the rhetorical example is Abraham — the same example Paul uses in Romans 4 to argue the opposite case. The Letter of James is a Jamesian-tradition text whose theological-political profile is incompatible with the institutional Pauline tradition that grounds the proto-orthodox synthesis.

The letter's canonical status reflects this incompatibility. Eusebius classifies James in the *antilegomena* — disputed books — at *HE* 3.25, alongside Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John. Origen accepts James but notes the dispute. The Latin tradition is hesitant: Jerome's Vulgate (late fourth century) includes James, but many earlier Latin manuscripts do not. The Letter of James is rarely cited by second-century Pauline-tradition writers; the citations begin in earnest only in the fourth century, after the canonical list is being formalized. Luther in the sixteenth century famously called James an "epistle of straw." The pattern is consistent: a Jamesian text whose content is too prominent to suppress but whose canonical weight can be contained. The letter is admitted, but its position in the corpus is marginal, its citation is deferred, and its interpretive framing is conducted through proto-orthodox reading practices that subordinate James to the institutional-Pauline tradition. Jude, Revelation (in the East), 2 Peter, and 2–3 John function similarly. Each text is in the canon; each text's canonical weight is structurally contained.

### 8.7 Marginalized: Ebionites, Nazarenes, the Jewish-Christian Gospels

The Jewish-Christian communities that maintained continuity with the Jamesian tradition after 70 CE — the Ebionites, the Nazarenes, the Elchasaites, and other Jewish-Christian groups attested in the second through fourth centuries — produced a body of texts that survives only as fragments quoted by hostile heresiologists. The Gospel of the Hebrews (cited by Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Jerome, and Epiphanius), the Gospel of the Nazarenes (cited by Jerome and others), the Gospel of the Ebionites (preserved in fragments by Epiphanius in *Panarion* 30), and the *Kerygmata Petrou* (the Petrine homilies underlying the Pseudo-Clementine literature) attest a Jewish-Christian textual tradition operating outside the proto-orthodox synthesis. The communities themselves are described by Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 47), Irenaeus (*Adversus Haereses* 1.26), Hippolytus, Eusebius, Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30 and 29), and Jerome.

The heresiological treatment is the documented marginalization. Epiphanius's *Panarion* 30 — the most extended ancient account of the Ebionites — describes the Ebionites as believing that Jesus was born to Joseph and Mary in the ordinary way (denying the virgin birth), that he became Christ at his baptism by the descent of the Spirit, that the Mosaic Law remained binding on Christians, and that Paul was an apostate (Pan. 30.16, 30.18, 30.25). The Ebionite Christology, the Ebionite Torah-observance, and the Ebionite rejection of Paul are precisely the Jamesian-tradition positions one would expect from a community continuing the Jerusalem tradition under post-70, post-135 conditions. Epiphanius's account is polemical and unreliable in detail (he is famous for cut-and-paste heresiology, blending the Elchasaites, Nazarenes, and Ebionites into confused combined profiles), but its operational point is unambiguous: by the late fourth century the Jewish-Christian tradition is being treated as heresy, its texts are not being copied by the proto-orthodox network, its communities are demographically marginalized, and what survives is the heresiologists' polemical record.

The Hegesippan account of the *Desposyni* (preserved in Eusebius), the Roman pursuit of the Davidic line, and the eventual extinction of the Jewish-Christian communities by demographic absorption or active suppression complete the marginalization. By the time of the Council of Nicaea (325 CE), the Ebionite-Nazarene Jewish-Christian tradition is no longer a player in the imperial-ecclesial settlement. The tradition's texts have not been copied at sufficient volume to survive; the tradition's communities have been absorbed into rabbinic Judaism on one side and proto-orthodox Christianity on the other, or have been simply demographically extinguished. Marginalization at this scale does not require central direction; it requires only the sustained gravitational pressure of the imperial-compatible network favoring its own and disfavoring its rivals across three centuries.

### 8.8 Destroyed or Buried: Marcion's *Evangelion*, Thomas, the Manichaean Corpus

The most dramatic handling is reserved for texts and communities whose institutional weight required active suppression. Marcion's *Evangelion*, composed or compiled around 140 CE in Rome by Marcion of Sinope and the textual centerpiece of the Marcionite church that became the most institutionally substantial alternative to proto-orthodoxy across the second through fourth centuries, presents the textbook case. Marcion proposed a binary canon — the *Evangelion* and the *Apostolikon* (a collection of Pauline letters) — and a theological framework in which the Hebrew Scriptures' creator god is distinct from the Father of Jesus Christ. The Marcionite church grew rapidly, established communities across the empire from Rome to the eastern frontier, and by the third century rivaled proto-orthodoxy in scale across many provincial cities. The proto-orthodox response was sustained heresiological assault — Justin's lost *Syntagma against All Heresies*, Irenaeus's *Adversus Haereses*, Tertullian's five-book *Adversus Marcionem*, Hippolytus's *Refutatio*, Epiphanius's *Panarion* 42 — and institutional competition that across two centuries reduced Marcionite communities to absorbed remnants. Marcion's *Evangelion* itself does not survive directly; it survives only in the polemical citations of Tertullian, Epiphanius, and a few others. Modern scholarship — Klinghardt, BeDuhn, Roth, Vinzent — has reconstructed the *Evangelion* from the polemical quotations and has demonstrated that the *Evangelion* is not, as Tertullian alleged, a mutilated version of canonical Luke but a related text from a parallel transmission stream, with significant text-critical evidence suggesting that in some passages the *Evangelion* preserves an earlier reading and canonical Luke is the expansion. The relationship between the Marcionite *Evangelion* and the canonical Luke is being actively re-examined in the New Marcionite Paradigm; the institutional point for the present argument is that Marcion's *Evangelion* was actively suppressed across two centuries by sustained heresiological and institutional operation. The text's destruction-by-non-copying is the documented form of the suppression.

The Gospel of Thomas — a collection of 114 *logia* of Jesus, surviving complete only in the Coptic translation buried at Nag Hammadi around 367 CE and in three Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus (P. Oxy. 1, 654, 655) — is the textbook case of suppression by non-copying followed by burial. The collection's content is composed of sayings of Jesus, many of which parallel sayings in the Synoptic Gospels, some of which are independent traditions. The proto-orthodox treatment of the *logia* tradition is hostile: the collection is condemned as heretical (Hippolytus, Origen, Eusebius), the carrier communities are marginalized, and the text is not copied within the proto-orthodox network. The Coptic codex at Nag Hammadi survives because it was buried by a community in Upper Egypt around the time of Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (367 CE) defining the canon — possibly by monastic copyists at the Pachomian monastery of Pabau, who buried texts that the new canonical settlement excluded rather than destroying them outright. The buried codices were rediscovered in 1945. The Gospel of Thomas's existence in 1945 is therefore the contingent product of one community's decision in the fourth century to bury rather than burn. The proto-orthodox network's preferred handling was destruction by non-copying. The content survives as documentary residue of a tradition that the network worked actively to extinguish.

The Manichaean corpus is the most extensive case. Mani (216–276 CE), born into an Elchasaite Jewish-Christian community in Babylonia, founded a self-consciously universal religion synthesizing Zoroastrian, Buddhist, and Christian elements. The Manichaean texts — the *Kephalaia*, the *Psalm-Book*, the *Living Gospel*, the *Treasure of Life*, the *Pragmateia*, the *Book of Mysteries*, the *Book of Giants*, the *Letters* — were produced in Eastern Aramaic and translated across the Manichaean network into Greek, Coptic, Latin, Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, Old Turkic, and Chinese. The Manichaean church operated for a millennium across Eurasia, established as the official religion of the Uyghur Khaganate (762–840 CE), surviving in Central Asia and southern China into the seventeenth century. Within the Roman Empire, Manichaeism was treated with extraordinary hostility from the moment of its arrival: Diocletian's anti-Manichaean edict of 302 CE (the *Rescript on the Manichaeans*) ordered Manichaean leaders burned alive with their books, Manichaean lay believers stripped of property and sent to mines or executed, and the texts physically destroyed. The Christian-imperial ratification under Constantine and his successors continued and intensified the suppression. Augustine, himself a Manichaean *auditor* before his Christian conversion, wrote sustained polemic against Manichaeism (*Contra Faustum*, *Contra Felicem*, *Contra Fortunatum*, the *Confessiones*'s autobiographical material), and the imperial-ecclesial apparatus from the fourth century through the medieval period systematically destroyed Manichaean communities and texts within the empire's reach. The corpus survives at all only because the Manichaean church operated outside the empire — the Medinet Madi codices from Roman Egypt (discovered 1929, partially destroyed in WWII), the Turfan fragments from the Tarim Basin (discovered 1902–1914), the Cologne Mani Codex (acquired 1969), the Dunhuang Chinese Manichaean texts. The texts survive at the imperial periphery; they were destroyed within the imperial center.

The differentiation principle is therefore visible across the full textual corpus of the surviving Jesus movement. Mark, the deutero-Pauline corpus, and Acts are written fresh in compatible form. Matthew, the Pastorals, and 1–2 Peter are smoothed from problematic substrates. John and Hebrews are captured. James, Jude, and the Antilegomena are admitted at low canonical weight. The Ebionite-Nazarene-Hebrew-Gospel tradition is marginalized. Marcion's *Evangelion*, Thomas, and the Manichaean corpus are destroyed or buried. Six handling strategies, applied across the corpus in exact correspondence with each text's compatibility with the imperial-compatible synthesis. The pattern is the operational signature. The signature is the diagnostic. The thesis stands or falls on whether this differentiation pattern can be explained by anything other than the imperial-compatibility hypothesis. Section 10 will examine the alternatives.

## 9. The Long-Cycle Pattern, 130–325 CE

### 9.1 Justin Martyr and the Construction of Canonical Authority

Justin Martyr, writing in Rome in the 140s and 150s CE, is the figure in whom the proto-orthodox apparatus crystallizes. Justin is a Greek-philosophy-trained convert who runs a school in Rome, addresses two *Apologies* to Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, conducts a *Dialogue with Trypho* the Jew (real or fictional) defending Christianity against Jewish counter-arguments, and produces lost heresiological works (the *Syntagma against All Heresies*, the *Treatise against Marcion*) that establish the genre that Irenaeus and his successors will continue. Justin's use of the term *apomnemoneumata tōn apostolōn* — "memoirs of the apostles" — at *1 Apology* 66.3 and elsewhere is the crystallizing moment for the institutional Gospel category. The Greek phrase deliberately echoes Xenophon's *Memorabilia* of Socrates; Justin is presenting the Christian Gospels to a Greco-Roman philosophical audience as the equivalent of the philosophical *memorabilia* genre. The four Gospels become, in Justin's framing, a quasi-philosophical literature suitable for elite Greco-Roman engagement. The framing serves the apologetic purpose, but the framing also institutionalizes the four Gospels as a class — *the* memoirs *of* the apostles, in the genitive — distinct from the alternative Gospels (Thomas, Marcion's *Evangelion*, the Jewish-Christian Gospels) that Justin's heresiological treatises are simultaneously refuting. Justin's apologetic-heresiological work is the productive moment at which the four Gospels become a closed category and the rival Gospels become the heresies excluded from it.

Justin is also the figure in whom the imperial-compatible posture of the surviving movement crystallizes. The *First Apology* is addressed directly to the emperor and the imperial heir; the rhetorical posture is one of loyal subjects asking for fair imperial judgment. The Christians are presented as the empire's most loyal subjects (1 Apol. 17), good citizens who pay their taxes, do not commit crimes, are not the *flagitia*-committers their accusers allege. The argument is the Romans 13 instruction made into a public-political case before the emperor. The Christian movement that Justin represents is the institutional descendant of the Pauline network: distributed across imperial cities, organized around proto-orthodox bishops, philosophically framed for Greco-Roman elite audiences, politically loyal to the imperial authority. Justin's martyrdom in Rome around 165 CE under Junius Rusticus is itself the Pliny–Trajan procedure operating on Justin's case: Justin refused to sacrifice; he was executed; the surviving network continued. The Pliny–Trajan toleration-with-management posture and the proto-orthodox apologetic-heresiological output are the two sides of the same operating relationship between empire and surviving network across the second century.

### 9.2 Irenaeus and the Four-Pillar Canon Argument

Irenaeus of Lyon, writing his *Adversus Haereses* in the 180s CE, is the figure in whom the four-Gospel canon receives its first explicit cosmological-theological defense. *Adversus Haereses* 3.11.8 contains the famous passage: "It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are. For, since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds, while the Church is scattered throughout all the world, and the 'pillar and ground' of the Church is the Gospel and the spirit of life... it is fitting that she should have four pillars... From which fact, it is evident that the Word, the Artificer of all... has given us the Gospel under four aspects, but bound together by one Spirit." The argument moves from the cosmic structure (four zones, four winds, four cherubim of the heavenly throne) to the cosmic appropriateness of a four-Gospel canon. The argument is theologically baroque, but its institutional function is precise: it provides a non-textual, non-historical, non-evidential argument for why the Gospel canon must be exactly four. It rules out three (Marcion's single-Gospel canon), it rules out many (the Valentinian and other Gnostic communities' broader textual collections), and it rules in the specific four (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) that the proto-orthodox network is institutionalizing.

Irenaeus's broader project across the five books of *Adversus Haereses* is the construction of the heresiological apparatus. Book 1 catalogues the Gnostic, Marcionite, Ebionite, and other heretical systems; Books 2–5 refute them in sustained theological-exegetical argument. The book's circulation across the proto-orthodox network — preserved in Latin translation (the *Vetus Latina* version is our principal source for much of the Greek original lost in transmission) — provides the proto-orthodox bishops with a single reference work that defines what counts as orthodox by enumerating what does not. The institutional effect across the second and third centuries is the standardization of the proto-orthodox interpretive framework. By the time of the Constantinian settlement, the heresiological boundary that Irenaeus's apparatus established is the boundary that the imperial-ecclesial settlement adopts.

### 9.3 The Heresiological Corpus

The heresiological corpus that develops from Justin through Irenaeus, Hippolytus's *Refutatio Omnium Haeresium* (*Philosophumena*, c. 220 CE), Tertullian's *Praescriptio Haereticorum* and *Adversus Marcionem* (early third century), Origen's polemical works against Celsus and the Gnostics, Cyprian's *De Unitate*, the lost *Syntagma* of Hippolytus, and culminating in Epiphanius's vast *Panarion* (c. 375 CE) and the related *Anacephalaeosis* is the documentary residue of the second-and-third-century operation. Each work catalogues the heresies, characterizes them, refutes them, and recommends their suppression. The corpus's institutional function is twofold. First, it provides the proto-orthodox network with a shared interpretive framework: the heretical groups are the named, characterized, refuted alternatives; the proto-orthodox synthesis is the un-named default (orthodoxy is what is not heresy). Second, it provides the documentary record by which the heretical traditions are remembered — and therefore the documentary record by which their texts can later be identified and (where surviving) read.

The heresiological corpus is also the documentary record of which texts and which communities are being actively suppressed across the period. The Marcionites receive sustained heresiological assault from Justin to Tertullian to Epiphanius; the Marcionite church accordingly contracts. The Valentinians and other Gnostic communities receive heresiological assault from Irenaeus to Hippolytus to Epiphanius; the communities accordingly fragment. The Ebionites and Nazarenes receive heresiological assault from Justin to Epiphanius; the communities accordingly extinguish demographically. The Manichaeans receive heresiological assault from the moment of their arrival; combined with the Diocletianic edict and Constantinian patronage of proto-orthodoxy, the Manichaean church is reduced inside the empire by the late fourth century. The heresiological apparatus is one of the operational instruments by which the differentiation principle of Section 8.2 was actually executed across three centuries. The handling strategies — marginalize, destroy, bury — were operationalized through the heresiological corpus and through the institutional non-copying that the heresiological framing produced.

### 9.4 Eusebius's Historiographical Framing

Eusebius of Caesarea, writing the *Historia Ecclesiastica* in the early fourth century (with successive editions across approximately 311–325 CE), produces the comprehensive historiographic framing of the surviving movement. The *Historia* is the book that organizes the entire post-apostolic period into the proto-orthodox narrative: apostolic origins, succession of bishops, sequence of heretics, sequence of imperial persecutions, sequence of martyrs, providential vindication of the orthodox church under Constantine. Eusebius is writing under Constantine; the *Historia*'s providential framing is the framing that the imperial-ecclesial settlement requires. The book's relation to its source material — Eusebius preserves quotations from Hegesippus, Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Africanus, and many other earlier writers whose works are now lost — is invaluable; the book's interpretive framing is also indispensable for understanding how the Constantinian-era proto-orthodox network represented its own history.

Two specific Eusebian operations are particularly relevant. First, the *Historia* preserves the Hegesippan record of the *Desposyni* hunt (*HE* 3.11–12, 3.19–20, 3.32) — the documentary record of Roman imperial pursuit of the Davidic line across three reigns, treated above (Section 6.1). Eusebius preserves the record because, in his providential framing, the imperial pursuit of the Davidic line ends safely (the descendants of Jude are dismissed as harmless; Symeon is martyred but the church continues). The record is therefore safe to preserve. The implications of the record for the imperial-substrate hypothesis are not Eusebius's framing; they are what the record itself documents. Second, the *Historia* contains at *HE* 3.25 the Antilegomena classification (homologoumena, antilegomena gnorima, notha) that registers the canonical settlement immediately prior to its formal closure. The classification preserves the residual ambiguity around several texts (Revelation, James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John); the formal closure under Athanasius (*Festal Letter 39*, 367 CE) will later resolve these into a fixed twenty-seven-book New Testament. Eusebius's role in the framing is the role of the imperial-ecclesial historian: he provides the narrative that organizes the period and legitimates its outcome. The narrative is what the imperial-ecclesial settlement requires.

### 9.5 Constantine Ratifies a 250-Year-Running Relationship

The Constantinian settlement — the Edict of Milan in 313 CE granting toleration to Christians, Constantine's personal patronage of the proto-orthodox church across his reign (313–337 CE), the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE establishing the trinitarian doctrinal settlement, the *Donations* establishing the bishop of Rome's institutional patrimony, the construction of imperial basilicas (Lateran, Old St. Peter's, Holy Sepulchre), and the imperial assumption of patronage over the church's institutional life — is conventionally treated as the moment Christianity became the empire's religion. The imperial-substrate hypothesis reframes the settlement as the formal ratification of a 250-year-running operational relationship between the imperial gravitational field and the proto-orthodox network. The relationship had been operating, in the form of post-70 surveillance and management of Christians, in the form of Pliny–Trajan toleration-with-management, in the form of differentiated handling of the textual corpus, and in the form of heresiological exclusion of the alternatives, since the destruction of the Temple. The Constantinian moment is the moment when the empire formally takes responsibility for what its gravitational field had already produced.

Several features of the Constantinian settlement support the ratification framing. First, the church Constantine patronizes is already institutionally consolidated: the four-Gospel canon is established (Irenaeus's argument is two centuries old); the proto-orthodox synthesis is institutionalized; the bishops are organized in metropolitan-provincial structures that map onto Roman administrative geography; the heresiological apparatus is deployed against the alternatives. The empire ratifies the institution it finds. Second, the doctrinal settlement at Nicaea (the trinitarian formula adopted to refute Arius) is a doctrinal settlement of an internal proto-orthodox dispute, conducted under imperial supervision but using the network's own theological resources. The empire's role is convenor, not theologian; the church's role is theologian, not convenor; the settlement is the joint product. Third, the heresies the imperial settlement begins to suppress with state instruments — the Donatists, the Arians, eventually the Manichaeans and the Pelagians and the various pagan philosophical schools — are heresies whose exclusion the proto-orthodox heresiological corpus had been recommending across two centuries. The empire applies state power to enforce settlements the network had already reached. Fourth, Eusebius's panegyric framing of Constantine as the providentially chosen ruler is the ecclesiastical ideology that legitimates the ratification; Eusebius is the proto-orthodox network's historian, and his framing is the network's narrative of why the relationship is theologically right. The framing closes the loop.

The British East India Company analogy applies precisely. The Crown's 1858 absorption of the Company was the formal ratification of a 200-year-running operational relationship; the Crown did not create the institutional vehicle in 1858 but absorbed an institutional vehicle that had operated under the Crown's gravitational field across two centuries. Constantine did not create the proto-orthodox church in 313 CE; Constantine ratified the institutional vehicle that had operated under the empire's gravitational field across 250 years. The textual canon was the network's textual canon; the institutional structure was the network's institutional structure; the heresiological apparatus was the network's heresiological apparatus; the imperial ratification under Constantine and his successors made the network's settlements legally and politically definitive across the empire's territory. The imperial-substrate hypothesis predicts this exactly. The accident-pattern alternative does not.

## 10. The Discrimination Test Applied

### 10.1 What the Accident Pattern Would Predict

The accident pattern (Section 2.3, hypothesis H1) is the model in which the textual outcomes of the Jesus movement are produced by unmanaged, distributed, contingent processes — the natural development of a religious movement under the ordinary social, theological, and demographic pressures of the Greco-Roman world, without any sustained imperial gravitational field shaping its trajectory. The accident pattern would predict (a) random or near-random distribution of theological positions across the textual corpus, with no systematic correlation between content and survival; (b) the survival of texts and communities in proportion to their size, geographic distribution, and theological vigor, without systematic disadvantage attaching to politically problematic content; (c) the absence of differentiated editorial handling correlated with imperial-compatibility profile; (d) the absence of sustained, multi-generational, multi-locational convergence on a single configuration; (e) the absence of any operational relationship between imperial state instruments and the surviving network across the period; (f) the absence of the documented imperial pursuit of specific theological-political profiles (Davidic descendants, "Jewish ways," messianic claimants); (g) the Constantinian settlement as a discontinuous event in which imperial preferences impose themselves on a network previously oriented otherwise, rather than a ratification event in which the empire absorbs an institutional vehicle aligned with its interest across the prior centuries.

### 10.2 What the Data Actually Show

The data do not show the accident pattern. The data show (a) systematic correlation between theological-political content and textual survival, with imperial-compatible content surviving and imperial-incompatible content marginalized, destroyed, or buried; (b) the destruction of the largest, most institutionally substantial alternative (the Marcionite church) and the most distinctive Jewish-Christian alternatives (the Ebionite-Nazarene tradition) despite their institutional vigor and geographic distribution; (c) the differentiated editorial handling of the canonical and proto-canonical corpus exactly along imperial-compatibility lines (Section 8); (d) the sustained, multi-generational, multi-locational convergence of the surviving network onto the proto-orthodox synthesis from approximately 70 CE through 367 CE (Sections 8 and 9); (e) the documented operational relationship between imperial state instruments and the management of Christianity from the Pliny-Trajan rescript through the Diocletianic edict to the Constantinian settlement (Sections 6.4 and 9.5); (f) the documented imperial pursuit of Davidic descendants and Jewish-Christian messianic claimants across three reigns (Section 6.1); (g) the Constantinian settlement as the formal ratification of an institutional vehicle whose institutional structure, textual canon, and heresiological boundary were already in place across the prior centuries (Section 9.5). The seven predictions of the accident pattern are systematically not what the data show. The seven predictions of the imperial-substrate hypothesis (Section 2.3, H2) are systematically what the data show. The discrimination is empirical.

### 10.3 Differentiation as the Diagnostic of a Managed Operation

The single most decisive diagnostic is the differentiation principle of Section 8.2. An unmanaged, distributed, contingent process does not produce six handling strategies applied across a textual corpus in exact correspondence with each text's compatibility with a single coherent imperial-compatible synthesis. Random or near-random distribution would produce mixed outcomes — some imperial-compatible texts marginalized, some imperial-incompatible texts admitted, no systematic correlation. The data show systematic correlation. The correlation is the signature of a gravitational field operating consistently across the network for a period of two and a half centuries. The field does not require central direction; the field requires only the empire's standing operating procedures (Section 3), the empire's documented surveillance of the relevant theological-political profiles (Section 6), and the surviving network's institutional alignment with the empire's gravitational interests (Section 7). All three were in place across the period. The signature is what the operation of the field across the period produced.

The diagnostic is not deductive proof that the imperial-substrate hypothesis is correct; the empirical record is open to alternative explanations, and Section 11.5 will discuss the limitations of the present argument. But the diagnostic does establish that the differentiation pattern is incompatible with the unmanaged-emergent model and is fully compatible with the managed-substrate model. The choice between models is the choice between a model that does not predict the data and a model that does. Methodologically, the model that predicts the data is to be preferred. The imperial-substrate hypothesis, as articulated in this thesis, is the model that predicts the data.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 The Proxy Logic (62–70)

The 62–70 CE sequence is the documented case where the imperial-substrate hypothesis can be tested most directly, because the Roman state is operating most visibly in the period. The action against James in 62 CE under Ananus II, the Pharisaic protest and Roman intervention, the engineered escalation under Florus, and the methodical reduction of 66–70 CE under Vespasian and Titus together constitute the documented sequence in which the proxy logic operates. The high priest is the proxy; the procuratorial gap is the operational window; the destruction of the parallel polity (the Jamesian Jerusalem community), the high-priestly aristocracy (the Sadducees), and the institutional center (the Temple) is the operational outcome. The Roman state does not need to write the texts that will subsequently be produced; the Roman state needs only to remove the parent institution and the parallel polity that contested the Pauline-imperial-compatible alternative. The remaining substrate — the Pauline network, urban, distributed, demographically compatible with empire — is what survives. The proxy logic delivers the operational outcome that makes Step Two possible.

### 11.2 The Patronage Logic (Post-70)

The post-70 logic is the patronage logic. The empire does not need to coerce the surviving network; the empire needs only to maintain the gravitational field within which the surviving network's institutional and theological development will be attracted toward imperial-compatible configurations. The *Fiscus Judaicus*, the Domitianic intensification, the Nervan reform, the Pliny–Trajan procedure, the Hadrianic settlement at Aelia Capitolina, the surveillance of Davidic descendants — all are mechanisms within the patronage logic. None requires central direction of the network's textual outputs; all combine to produce the gravitational field that systematically favors texts compatible with imperial stability and disfavors texts that are not. The differentiation principle of Section 8.2 is the operational signature of the gravitational field. The signature does not require the field to be a conscious imperial strategy in any modern bureaucratic sense; it requires only that the field operate consistently across the period. The documented operations of Sections 6 and 9 establish that the field did operate consistently across the period.

### 11.3 The Continuation Logic (130–325)

The continuation logic across 130–325 CE is the institutional consolidation of the patronage logic. The proto-orthodox apologetic-heresiological apparatus (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, Eusebius) provides the network with the interpretive framework that makes its imperial-compatible alignment doctrinally durable. The four-Gospel canon argument (Irenaeus AH 3.11.8) provides the textual closure. The episcopal-metropolitan-provincial structure (developed across the second and third centuries, mapping onto Roman administrative geography) provides the institutional closure. The heresiological exclusion of Marcionites, Valentinians, Ebionites, and (after 274 CE) Manichaeans provides the boundary closure. By the time of Constantine, the network is institutionally, textually, and doctrinally consolidated to the point that imperial ratification is the obvious next step. The Constantinian moment is therefore the moment the empire formally absorbs the institution its gravitational field had produced — the moment, to repeat the British East India Company analogy, of the 1858 absorption of an institutional vehicle that had been operating under the gravitational field of the imperial interest across the prior centuries.

### 11.4 The Coordination Question: Conscious Strategy vs. Operational Instinct

A central interpretive question concerns whether the empire's operations across the period constitute a conscious imperial strategy in any modern sense. The thesis answer is precise: the operations do not require, and the documentary record does not support, a conscious centralized imperial strategy in the modern bureaucratic sense. What the documentary record supports is operational consistency — the empire's standing operating procedures (Section 3) applied across the period in the manner that those procedures dictated, with each emperor and each administrative apparatus operating according to the imperial logic of its period rather than according to a multi-generational imperial plan. Vespasian's *Desposyni* hunt, Domitian's *Fiscus Judaicus* intensification, Nerva's reform, Trajan's rescript to Pliny, Hadrian's Aelia Capitolina settlement, Diocletian's edict against Manichaeans, and Constantine's patronage of the proto-orthodox church together constitute operational consistency across a period of two and a half centuries; they do not constitute a conscious multi-generational plan. Operational instinct, applied repeatedly across the period within the framework of standing imperial procedures, produces the cumulative operational outcome. The cumulative outcome is the substrate the textual canon was produced inside.

The British East India Company analogy applies again. The Crown's policy toward India across two centuries was not a single plan; it was operational consistency in the application of imperial logic across successive ministries, successive Company directorates, successive military operations, and successive treaties. The cumulative outcome — the institutional absorption of the subcontinent into the imperial system — was produced by operational consistency, not by a centralized plan. The same applies to the empire's relationship with the Jesus movement. The thesis does not claim the empire planned the canon; the thesis claims the empire operated consistently in the manner its standing procedures dictated, the surviving network operated consistently within the gravitational field thereby produced, and the convergent outcome — proto-orthodoxy as imperial ratified — is the cumulative product. Operational instinct is sufficient to produce operational consistency; operational consistency is sufficient to produce the gravitational field; the gravitational field is sufficient to produce the differentiation pattern that Section 8 documents and Section 10 identifies as the diagnostic signature.

### 11.5 Limitations and Lacunae

Five principal limitations attach to the present argument and require explicit acknowledgement. **First**, the documentary record is heavily weighted toward the surviving proto-orthodox tradition; the alternative traditions (Jamesian-Jewish-Christian, Marcionite, Manichaean, the various Gnostic streams) are accessible primarily through hostile heresiological sources, and the proto-orthodox sources are themselves the documentary product of the operation the thesis describes. Reading these sources for what they reveal requires careful attention to bias; the documentary record is not neutral evidence. Modern critical scholarship — text-critical, historical-critical, archaeological, papyrological — has substantially expanded the documentary base over the past two centuries (the Nag Hammadi find, the Cologne Mani Codex, the Turfan fragments, the modern reconstruction of Marcion's *Evangelion*), but the documentary record remains incomplete. The thesis is a reading of the available record; alternative readings of the same record are possible, and the limitations of the record itself constrain the certainty of any reading.

**Second**, the thesis's central claim — that the empire's gravitational field produced systematic differentiation in textual handling — is a structural claim that does not in every individual case discriminate the imperial-compatibility variable from other potentially correlated variables (theological coherence, institutional vigor, demographic distribution, geographic location, language of composition). For each individual text, the imperial-compatibility profile correlates with other features of the text's social-institutional context, and disentangling the variables for any single case is not always possible. The thesis's argument is therefore the cumulative argument: the correlation pattern across the full corpus is too systematic and too directional to be explained by chance combinations of other variables, but for any individual case the alternative variables remain in play. The cumulative pattern, not the individual case, is the diagnostic.

**Third**, the thesis treats the empire as a coherent agent whose gravitational field operates consistently across the period. In fact, the empire was internally heterogeneous: different emperors held different policies; different provincial administrations operated under different priorities; different periods saw substantial changes in the imperial-compatibility threshold (the Severan period was relatively tolerant; the Decian and Diocletianic periods were aggressively persecutorial). The gravitational field metaphor abstracts from this heterogeneity. The abstraction is justified, the thesis argues, by the operational consistency of standing imperial procedures across the period, but the heterogeneity is real and complicates the picture. A more granular treatment than the present thesis can provide would track period-by-period variation in the gravitational field's strength and direction.

**Fourth**, the thesis's reading of specific texts in Section 8 is broadly aligned with mainstream historical-critical scholarship but assigns interpretive weight to text-critical findings that are themselves contested. The deutero-Pauline status of the Pastorals is widely accepted; the deutero-Pauline status of Colossians and 2 Thessalonians is more contested. The dating of the Gospels, the priority of Mark, the existence and nature of Q, the relationship between Matthew and Luke, and the dating and authorship of John are all topics on which the scholarly consensus has been stable across recent decades but on which significant minority positions exist. The New Marcionite Paradigm reading of the relationship between Marcion's *Evangelion* and canonical Luke (Section 8.8) is a scholarly minority position that has gained substantial momentum but that remains contested. The thesis adopts these readings where they fit the imperial-compatibility hypothesis; the readings are defended in the supporting library (see in particular the documents in `data/03_Biblical_Scholarship/` and `data/04_Early_Christian_History/`). The reader who adopts different text-critical conclusions on these specific points will arrive at different specific assessments of individual texts, though the overall differentiation pattern is robust to substantial individual-case revision.

**Fifth**, the thesis's two-tier evidentiary structure (Section 2.1) admits a Tier 2 reading that goes beyond what Tier 1 can establish. The documentary-Roman record (Tier 1) establishes the operating procedures, the post-70 surveillance and management apparatus, the Pliny-Trajan procedure, the Hadrianic settlement, and the Constantinian ratification. The textual-record reading (Tier 2) establishes the differentiation pattern across the textual corpus and the structural correlation with imperial-compatibility. The connection between the two tiers — the claim that the gravitational field documented at Tier 1 is what produced the pattern documented at Tier 2 — is an inferential claim that goes beyond the direct documentary record. The thesis argues that the inferential claim is supported by the cumulative weight of the record; the thesis acknowledges that an alternative inference (the differentiation pattern was produced by something other than the imperial gravitational field) is logically possible. The thesis's response is that the alternative inferences require either denial of the documentary record at Tier 1 (which is well-established) or denial of the differentiation pattern at Tier 2 (which is internally consistent across the corpus). Neither denial is scholarly available. The inferential bridge between the two tiers is the contribution of the present thesis. It is offered as the most adequate available explanation of the cumulative record, not as deductive proof.

## 12. Conclusion: Rome's Gravity, Two Steps, One Brief

The textual record of the Jesus movement from 62 BCE to 325 CE is intelligible as the product of Rome's gravity operating across two and a half centuries on a movement that began as a Jamesian-Davidic-messianic protest within Second Temple Judaism and emerged as a proto-orthodox imperial-ratified institutional church. The empire's gravity operated in two formal steps. **Step One**, the military reset of 62–73 CE, removed the parent institution: the high-priestly aristocracy, the Sanhedrin, the Temple, the Jamesian Jerusalem community, and the institutional matrix within which the messianic-Jewish-Christian protest had grown across thirty years. The reset was conducted by the Roman state's documented operating procedures — the proxy escalation against James, the engineered escalation under Florus, the methodical reduction under Vespasian and Titus — applied to the eastern Mediterranean's most contested provincial space. The reset removed the parent and left, as surviving substrate, the Pauline network: distributed across imperial cities, demographically compatible with empire, theologically articulated for Gentile audiences, politically posture-trained by Romans 13. **Step Two**, the editorial operation conducted by the surviving network across approximately 70 CE to 367 CE, produced the proto-orthodox canon and the proto-orthodox synthesis. The operation deployed six handling strategies — write fresh, smooth, capture, admit at low canonical weight, marginalize, destroy or bury — applied across the full surviving textual corpus in exact correspondence with each text's compatibility with the imperial-compatible synthesis. The differentiation pattern is the operational signature of the operation. The signature is the diagnostic. **The Constantinian moment** of 313–367 CE is the formal ratification, by the empire, of the institutional vehicle the empire's gravitational field had produced across the prior 250 years.

This thesis has argued that the textual outputs of the Jesus movement across this period are not the unmanaged emergent products of an ordinary religious movement; they are the textual outputs of a movement that operated, across its entire institutional development, within the gravitational field of the Roman imperial interest. The differentiation pattern across the canonical, proto-canonical, marginalized, and destroyed-or-buried portions of the textual corpus is the empirical signature of this gravitational operation. The two-tiered evidentiary structure that the thesis applied — Tier 1 the documented Roman record, Tier 2 the textual-record reading — converges on the conclusion that the empire was the gravitational mass and the surviving network was the local actor. The thesis has not argued that Rome wrote the Gospels, edited the Pauline corpus, or composed the Pastorals. The thesis has argued that Rome did what Rome documentably did across two centuries — surveilled, managed, engineered, deployed, reorganized, rewarded, tolerated, suppressed — and that the textual outputs of the surviving network reflect operation of those instruments by local actors whose interests aligned with the imperial outcome.

The four canonical Gospels are four datapoints among approximately fifty primary witnesses in the textual corpus that this study has examined. They are not the subject; they are particularly visible signatures of the substrate. The substrate is Rome. The settlement was two-step. The brief was singular: produce a movement compatible with the empire's gravitational interest; remove the alternatives. The brief was successful. The cost was the loss of the Jamesian-Davidic-Jewish-Christian tradition that had been the movement's first generation. The cost is also the textual record we now read. Reading the record honestly requires reading the substrate.
