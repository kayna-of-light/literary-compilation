# **The Mirror of the Invisible: Swedenborgian Natural Philosophy and the Romantic Folklore of the Brothers Grimm**

## **I. Introduction: The Cartography of the Enchanted World**

The early nineteenth century in the German-speaking lands was defined by a radical cognitive shift, a pivot away from the cold, clockwork universe of the Enlightenment toward a cosmos pulsing with hidden vitality, spiritual immanence, and organic unity. This era, widely known as German Romanticism, was not merely a literary movement but a profound philosophical rebellion. It sought to re-enchant the world, to dissolve the Cartesian barrier between mind and matter, and to rediscover the "lost language" of nature. At the epicenter of this cultural earthquake stood the monumental project of Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, whose *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* (Children's and Household Tales) were ostensibly a collection of rustic folklore but functioned, in reality, as a repository of ancient metaphysical intuitions. While the Grimms are celebrated as philologists who captured the voice of the *Volk*, their work is embedded in the philosophical matrix of German Romanticism (Leerssen 2013, 20–21)—a matrix into which the theology of the Swedish scientist and mystic Emanuel Swedenborg had entered through Oetinger, Schelling and Goethe, and, in the brothers' own reading, through Jung-Stilling (Horn 1954; Morris 1902; Stecher 1913).

This report undertakes an exhaustive investigation into the structural, thematic, and philosophical connections between Swedenborg’s "Nature Philosophy"—specifically his Doctrine of Correspondences—and the narrative mechanics of the Grimms' fairy tales. The central hypothesis of this inquiry is that the "magic" found in these tales is neither arbitrary nor nonsensical. Rather, the talking animals, animate landscapes, and reactive environments of the fairy tale operate according to a consistent "theological physics" that mirrors Swedenborg’s assertion that the natural world is a "theatre representative" of the spiritual realm (*Heaven and Hell* §106; *Arcana Coelestia* §3483). Through the intellectual mediation of German Idealist philosophers like Friedrich Schelling, theosophists like Friedrich Oetinger, and literary titans like Goethe, Swedenborg’s vision of a universe where every natural object is the "ultimate effect" of a spiritual cause saturated the intellectual soil in which the Brothers Grimm labored.

In the Swedenborgian cosmos, nature is a living mirror of the human mind: to the man whose interiors are open, "the things which are of the light of the world become to him images and as it were mirrors in which he sees the things of the light of heaven" (*AC* §4104). Animals are not merely biological entities but embodiments of specific human affections—lions representing the good of love and its truth "in their power", foxes representing cunning, and lambs representing innocence (*AC* §§6367, 3994; *HH* §110). Similarly, landscapes function as moral topographies: mountains correspond to celestial love, valleys to the natural mind, and dark forests to the state of the unregenerate intellect lost in the bewildering data of the senses (*AC* §§795, 1723, 10644). When the Brothers Grimm recorded tales of princes cursed into frogs, simpletons aided by ants and ducks, or forests that open and close like living organs, they were preserving narrative structures that function as precise "moral mirrors."

This analysis demonstrates that the Grimms' use of "animate landscapes" and "talking animals" serves a specific heuristic function: to externalize the internal moral state of the protagonist. The environment in a Grimm fairy tale is never neutral; it is reactive. It judges, rewards, punishes, and guides, behaving exactly as the spiritual world does in Swedenborg’s theology. By tracing the transmission vectors from Swedenborg to Schelling and finally to the Grimms, and by conducting a rigorous exegetical analysis of key tales such as *The Queen Bee*, *The White Snake*, *The Frog King*, *The Goose Girl*, and *Hansel and Gretel*, this report reveals the extent to which these "nursery tales" function as complex allegories of spiritual regeneration and vastation.

## **II. The Philosophical Ecosystem: Swedenborg, Schelling, and the Romantics**

To understand the resonance between Swedenborgian thought and German Romantic folklore, it is essential to reconstruct the intellectual bridge that connected the Swedish mystic to the German philologists. This was not a direct, linear transmission but a complex diffusion of ideas through the conduit of *Naturphilosophie* (Nature Philosophy).

### **2.1 The Theological Physics of Emanuel Swedenborg**

Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772) began his career as a scientist and engineer, deeply engaged in the study of anatomy, metallurgy, and cosmology (Swedenborg 1734; Swedenborg 1740; Dunér 2013). When his interests turned toward the spiritual in the 1740s, he did not abandon his scientific rigor but applied it to the metaphysics of the soul. The result was a theology that functioned like a physics of the spirit.

#### **The Doctrine of Correspondences**

The cornerstone of Swedenborg’s system is the concept of *Correspondence*. He argues that the natural world has no independent existence; it exists only as a perpetual effect of a spiritual cause. "There is a correspondence of all things of heaven with all things of man," Swedenborg writes (*HH* §87), and by extension, with all things of the earth: "all things that have existence in nature, from the least to the greatest thereof, are correspondences" (*HH* §106). This relationship is causal and ontological. A physical object is the "ultimate" form of a spiritual essence.

* **Vertical Causality:** Reality is structured in discrete degrees. Divine Love and Wisdom (the Solar Fire of the spiritual world) flow down through the Spiritual World (the realm of causes) and terminate in the Natural World (the realm of effects).  
* **The Grand Man (*Maximus Homo*):** Swedenborg posits that the entire heaven is structured in the form of a single human being. Consequently, the universe itself is anthropomorphic in its essence. Nature speaks to man because nature is, in its spiritual origin, human. This provides the metaphysical justification for the anthropomorphism inherent in fairy tales (*HH* §59).  
* **The Spiritual World as Moral Mirror:** In the spiritual world, where souls go after death, the external environment is not fixed matter. "All things in heaven come forth from the Lord in correspondence with the interiors of the angels" (*HH* §173). Those in evil find themselves among caverns, dens and deserts (*HH* §586); the intelligent walk in "gardens and parks full of trees and flowers of every kind" (*HH* §176). The landscape is a projection of character.

#### **The Zoology of Affection**

In this system, living creatures are living symbols of the "Will." Swedenborg asserts that "the animals of the earth correspond in general to affection, mild and useful animals to good affections, fierce and useless ones to evil affections" (*Heaven and Hell* §110). A sheep does not merely *symbolize* charity; the spiritual affection of charity *causes* the form of the sheep to exist. Thus, a "talking animal" in a story is simply an "affection" made articulate.

### **2.2 The Schelling Connection: The Prism of Naturphilosophie**

The primary vector for Swedenborg’s influence on German Romanticism was Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (1775–1854). Schelling’s *Naturphilosophie* revolutionized German thought by rejecting the Newtonian mechanistic view of nature. Instead, Schelling argued that "Nature is visible Spirit; Spirit is invisible Nature" (Schelling 1797, LXIV). This *Naturphilosophie* grew from the theosophy of Schelling's Swabian homeland: the theosophical idea of the bodiliness of the spiritual, in its inversion as the spirituality of the bodily, had shaped his philosophy of nature "from the beginning" (Horn 1954, 15).

Schelling’s engagement with Swedenborg was profound and well-documented. The Swabian pietist Friedrich Oetinger, who corresponded with Swedenborg (Johnson 2002) and defended him in his heresy trial (Tilliette n.d.), integrated the Swede’s ideas into a German theosophical tradition, and Schelling was "an avid reader of Böhme and Oetinger" (Magee 2001). Oetinger may have served as the intermediary, but Schelling read Swedenborg for himself. In his dialogue *Clara* (c. 1810) he acknowledges "the Swedish spirit-seer" as one whose inner sight had been opened to look into the other world, and he has his narrator recall what "the northern spirit-seer" taught about why the Lord was born on this earth—"for the sake of the Word, because here alone it could be materially propagated"—in words that follow Swedenborg almost verbatim; he never wrote Swedenborg's name (Horn 1954, 13, 27–28). Friedemann Horn’s definitive study, *Schelling und Swedenborg*, traces this reception. Its emblem is "spirit-corporeality" (*Geistleiblichkeit*)—the idea that spirit has substance and form. Horn calls Swedenborg's doctrine of degrees "the highest systematization known to us of the idea of spirit-corporeality", and finds Schelling's three potencies so like Swedenborg's three degrees "that it seems almost impossible not to believe in a connection" (Horn 1954, 109).

Schelling’s philosophy provided the intellectual framework for the Romantics. He posited that the universe is an organism striving for self-consciousness, a view that sanctified nature and elevated mythology to the status of high truth. For Schelling, as for Swedenborg, the separation between the "real" and the "ideal" was a modern illusion; at the deepest level, nature and spirit were one, in "the absolute identity of the spirit in us and the nature outside us" (Schelling 1797, LXIV; Hösle 2017, 109–110, 113). Ernst Benz traced the Romantic reading of nature to this source: "From this Swedenborgian doctrine of analogy the Romantic idea of nature as hieroglyph is developed" (Benz 1941, 26, quoted in Horn 1954, 106).

### **2.3 The Brothers Grimm and the Romantic Circle**

Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm were not isolated scholars; they were active participants in the vibrant intellectual circle of Heidelberg Romanticism. They were students of Friedrich Carl von Savigny and close friends with Clemens Brentano and Achim von Arnim, the poets who compiled *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* (The Boy's Magic Horn) (Leerssen 2013, 20–21).

This circle lived in the mystical revival that looked to Jakob Böhme and Swedenborg as antidotes to Enlightenment materialism: its philosopher, Schelling, read Böhme, Oetinger and Swedenborg (Magee 2001; Horn 1954, 13, 27), and Goethe had built the spirit-scene of the *Faust* on Swedenborg's world of spirits (Morris 1902, 19–22). The brothers met Swedenborg in their own reading. In 1809 both read Johann Heinrich Jung-Stilling's *Theorie der Geister-Kunde* (1808), whose author knew *Heaven and Hell*, followed Swedenborg in his doctrine of the intermediate state, and gave whole sections to "the famous spirit-seer Swedenborg", "the most remarkable man of this kind", whose writings "contain uncommonly much that is beautiful, instructive and credible" (Jung-Stilling 1808, §§113, 118; Stecher 1913, 245, 252–253). Jacob wrote that its first part, on presentiments and prophecies, had moved him "infinitely more" than the second; Wilhelm judged: "Jung's book is thoroughly original and is surely right in the main" (Grimm and Grimm 1881, 90, 95). Arnim wrote a long admiring review of the book, Görres began one, and Brentano kept company with Stilling in Heidelberg (Stecher 1913, 260–264); the brothers took *Jorinde and Joringel* (KHM 69) from Stilling's autobiography (Grimm and Grimm 1822), and two legends of their *Deutsche Sagen* from the same book, finding almost nothing in his wording to improve (Stecher 1913, 259). The books themselves survive. The reconstructed catalogue of the brothers' library records both Jung-Stilling titles, the *Theorie der Geister-Kunde* (1808) and its *Apologie* (1809), together with a pamphlet attacking the *Theorie* and the last volume of Stilling's autobiography (Denecke and Teitge 1989, nos. 3111, 5280–5282). Beside them it records G. H. Schubert's *Die Symbolik des Traumes* (1814) and his *Altes und Neues aus dem Gebiet der innren Seelenkunde* (1817–1824), the latter bearing Jacob's ownership note (Denecke and Teitge 1989, nos. 6523–6524). Schubert's book, which reads dreams as a picture-language, names Swedenborg four times. It compares the common root of words for love and strife to the way, "according to Swedenborg", the fiercest hatred is born in the other world out of sensual love (Schubert 1814, 79); it recalls his saying that the bodily-sensual is represented in the other life by serpents (81); it quotes at length his account of the spirits of Mars who place heaven "in knowledge alone, and not in the life of love", learn to speak without affection, and come to resemble the hard skin, "stiffened to bone", that encloses the brain, for "only those have a spiritual life whose knowledge comes from heavenly love" (93–95; cf. *AC* §§7745–7750); and it cites "the Swedenborgian proposition" that lustful love turns in that world into a lust to murder (122). Schubert introduces the long quotation by calling Swedenborg's state "very like" the deeper degree of dreaming, differing from it only in being joined to waking consciousness (93). The catalogue records no work by Swedenborg himself, nothing by Oetinger, and no *Adrastea*, the Herder journal that carried his essay on Swedenborg, among the Herder holdings (Denecke and Teitge 1989, nos. 3114–3117, 4517). Its editors warn that losses from the library cannot all be identified by title (Denecke and Teitge 1989, 26), so that silence does not show the brothers never owned them. What the catalogue does establish is a route through books on their own shelves: Swedenborg reached the Grimms quoted, defended and argued over in Jung-Stilling and Schubert. Schubert's other book on their shelves does not add to it: the first volume of *Altes und Neues* (1817) never names Swedenborg, though it twice cites "der liebe Vater Stilling" (Schubert 1817, 98, 143). Arnim, the brothers' closest friend, knew Swedenborg as a figure. In *Die Ehenschmiede* his narrator, a naturalist dining alone in a Scottish inn, hears a conversation start up as if in his own room: "I thought of Swedenborg, who received his first heavenly revelations after a meal in an English inn", until the wonder resolves into the chimney flue of the next room (Arnim 1965, 533). The novella was first printed from Arnim's papers in 1839, in the second volume of his collected works, whose title page names Wilhelm Grimm as editor; by Otto Mallon's account Wilhelm wrote only the preface and left the choice of texts to Bettina von Arnim (Arnim 1965, 806–807). In Arnim's *Landhausleben* (1826) it is a sceptic who promises to guard a young man against "the swindles of this new mysticism", now that he has happily escaped "the old Swedenborgian" with a rich inheritance (Arnim 1965, 300). Both passages are ironic, and they show that Swedenborg and the story of his first vision were common knowledge in the brothers' circle, not that Arnim held his doctrine. The evidence therefore stands at two levels. The brothers' own reading is documented: they read Jung-Stilling's book in 1809, discussed it with each other, and kept it and Schubert's *Symbolik des Traumes* on their shelves. Their circle is the wider ground. Its members read and reviewed one another and wrote to the brothers constantly; Arnim and Görres reviewed Stilling's book, and Schelling, Schubert and Arnim wrote of Swedenborg, sometimes with admiration and sometimes with irony. What the circle shows is inferred rather than established for the brothers themselves, Herder's essay on Swedenborg in *Adrastea* among it, but these were people who shared their reading openly, and the Grimms took the measure of the spirit-seer in that company. The Grimms shared the Romantic conviction that folk tales were the *Naturpoesie* (natural poetry) of the *Volk*—the oral remains of an older, vanished system of supernatural belief (Leerssen 2013, 21), shattered fragments of an ancient, organic revelation that mirrored the workings of nature itself.

While the Grimms were ostensibly philologists, their worldview was shaped by the Romantic idea, which Jacob learned from Savigny's "historicist organicism" (Leerssen 2013, 20), that language and myth were organic growths of the human spirit, rooted in the natural world. Introducing the second edition in 1819, Wilhelm Grimm found preserved in the tales "thoughts about the divine and the spiritual", "old faith and doctrine", an all-animated nature in which the transformed stones, trees and plants "are properly spiritually alive", and good and evil set out as white and black: "the good, helping spirits are almost always white birds … the pure, gall-less doves; the evil and ill-boding are black ravens"; "the pious girl becomes white as the day, the godless one black as sin" (W. Grimm 1819). In 1850 he summed it up: common to all the tales are "the remnants of a belief reaching back to the oldest times, which expresses itself in a figurative grasp of supersensible things" (W. Grimm 1856, 409)—a view of ancient revelation surviving in figurative form that Swedenborg himself held in his description of the ancient people, to whom "the knowledge of correspondences was the chief of knowledges" (*HH* §87). Thus, when the Grimms collected tales of speaking trees and magical birds, they were preserving what they saw as the remnants of a primordial spiritual language—the very language of correspondence described by Swedenborg.

## **III. Animate Landscapes: The Topography of the Soul**

In the tales collected by the Brothers Grimm, the landscape is never a passive backdrop. It is an active participant in the moral drama, a "moral mirror" that reflects the internal state of the characters and the community. This aligns precisely with Swedenborg’s teaching that in the spiritual world, the environment is an externalization of the inhabitants' minds (*HH* §173). Wilhelm Grimm saw the same world in the tales: "as in a golden age, everything is still alive: sun, moon and stars are approachable and give gifts … the animals, birds (doves are the most beloved and helpful), plants, stones speak and know how to show their sympathy" (W. Grimm 1819). Swedenborg gave the golden age to the most ancient people, who "thought from correspondences themselves, the natural things of the world before their eyes serving them as means of thinking in this way" (*HH* §115).

### **3.1 The Forest (*Der Wald*): The Unregenerate Mind and Temptation**

The forest is the most ubiquitous setting in the Grimm corpus. In Swedenborgian correspondence, "forests" signify "the memory-knowledge which is of the natural man" (*AC* §10644)—the external mind, dense, entangled, and obscured from the direct light of the "spiritual sun". It is a place of knowledge (trees correspond to perceptions, *AC* §103), but it is uncultivated knowledge, often harboring "wild beasts"—the "fierce and useless" animals that correspond to evil affections (*HH* §110).

* **Hansel and Gretel (KHM 15):** The narrative arc of this tale is a journey into the "vastation" of the soul. The children, who live "hard by a great forest", are abandoned in it when the parental order (specifically the stepmother, representing a perverted natural affection) fails to sustain them.  
  * *The Swedenborgian Parallel:* The forest represents the state of temptation. The children are lost in the density of their own natural minds, cut off from spiritual guidance. The witch’s house, constructed of bread and sugar, is a precise correspondence for the "sensual" appetite—it offers sustenance but is a trap. The Witch herself personifies "Self-Love" that devours innocence.  
  * *The Crossing:* To escape, the children must not only kill the witch (destroy the evil affection) but also cross a body of water on a white duck. In Swedenborg, water corresponds to truth (*AC* §2702) and birds to rational and intellectual things (*AC* §40); the white duck that carries the children over is a natural affection for truth that purifies. Gretel will not let them ride together: the duck "shall take us across, one after the other" (Grimm and Grimm 1884)—the individual nature of the spiritual passage through truth.  
* **Little Red Riding Hood (Rotkäppchen, KHM 26):** The girl enters the wood and is seduced by the Wolf. Here, the forest is the realm of the "Sensual," where the innocence of the child is threatened by the Wolf, which in Swedenborg denotes "those who are against innocence" (*AC* §3994) and, in sheep's clothing, the one "who seizes and scatters" (*AC* §6441), hiding within the natural mind.

### **3.2 The Mountain and the Cave: The Vertical Axis of the Spirit**

Swedenborg posits that physical altitude corresponds to spiritual "degree." "Mountains" signify celestial love, "the good of love to the Lord", the highest state of the mind; "hills" the good of mutual love (*AC* §§795, 6435). "Valleys" signify the natural mind—"the external man is called a 'valley' from the fact that it is below" (*AC* §1723)—and "caves," dark dwellings, signify an obscure good (*AC* §2463), while the hells themselves appear as "caverns and dens in rocks" (*HH* §586).

* **The Seven Ravens (KHM 25):** The sister’s journey to rescue her brothers takes her to the "Glass Mountain." In folklore, the glass mountain often represents a barrier between worlds, but in Swedenborgian terms, "glass" corresponds to the clarity of Divine Truth: the holy city of "pure gold like unto pure glass" is "pellucid from the Divine wisdom" (*Apocalypse Revealed* §912). Her ascent represents the elevation of the mind to a state of spiritual perception.  
  * *Cosmological Negotiation:* Before reaching the mountain, she visits the Sun, Moon, and Stars. The Sun is "too hot and terrible, and devoured little children" (devouring self-love if approached without mediation), the Moon "far too cold, and also awful and malicious" (faith without charity), but the Stars (knowledge of good and truth) are "kind and good to her", and the morning star gives her the key (the drumstick of a chicken) to open the mountain (Grimm and Grimm 1884). In Swedenborg the sun is love, the moon faith, and the stars knowledges (*AC* §§31–32); for Wilhelm Grimm, "sun, moon and stars above all possess a spiritual nature" in the tales (W. Grimm 1819). This cosmology mirrors Swedenborg’s description of the spiritual spheres and the necessity of balancing Heat (Love) and Light (Wisdom).  
* **Simeliberg (KHM 142):** The mountain opens to reveal treasures when the correct name is spoken: "Semsi mountain, Semsi mountain, open" (Grimm and Grimm 1884). This mirrors the Swedenborgian idea that the "Internal Sense" of the Word (the treasure) is locked within the "External Sense" (the mountain) and can only be accessed by the "key" of correspondence.

### **3.3 The Briar Hedge: The Defense of Innocence**

The landscape in Grimm tales often actively defends or imprisons based on the moral timing of the narrative.

* **Sleeping Beauty / Briar Rose (KHM 50):** The princess is surrounded by a "hedge of thorns" that grows to conceal the castle completely (Grimm and Grimm 1884).  
  * *Swedenborgian Correspondence:* "Thorns," "briers," "brambles," and "thistles" "signify falsities of evil, from their stings" (*Apocalypse Revealed* §439), which choke the church or the mind. In the tale, the hedge acts as a lethal barrier to the "false suitors"—princes who attempt to force their way in through self-intelligence or "faith alone" before the state has come: "the thorns held fast together, as if they had hands," and the youths "died a miserable death" (Grimm and Grimm 1884). They are entangled and destroyed by the falsities of the age.  
  * *The Transformation:* The "right" Prince (representing the conjunction of Good and Truth) comes when "the hundred years had just passed, and the day had come when Briar-rose was to awake again": the hedge is "nothing but large and beautiful flowers, which parted from each other of their own accord, and let him pass unhurt" (Grimm and Grimm 1884). This "miraculous" change of the landscape from thorns to flowers is a precise correspondence of the *transformation* of the mind during regeneration, which cannot be forced and comes when its state is full. Approached by genuine Love in the fullness of time, the "thorns" of the literal sense or natural mind blossom into the "flowers" of wisdom. The landscape answers both the fullness of the time and the rightness of the one who comes; it never yields to force. The Grimms' own note reads the hedge so: the maiden sleeps in a castle walled with thorns "until the right king's son releases her, before whom the thorns give way"—the sleeping Brynhild of Norse saga, ringed by a wall of flame "that Sigurd alone can pass", the spindle being the sleep-thorn with which Odin pricked her (Grimm and Grimm 1822).

### **3.4 Weather and Elements: The Response of the Heavens**

* **Mother Holle (KHM 24):** This tale explicitly links human labor to meteorological phenomena. When the industrious girl shakes the feather bed in the underworld, "then there is snow on the earth" (Grimm and Grimm 1884).  
  * *Swedenborgian Insight:* This reflects the doctrine of the "Grand Man"—that the microcosm (human action) influences the macrocosm (nature). Snow corresponds to truth in ultimates—"'snow,' truth in ultimates" (*Apocalypse Revealed* §47)—truth that is cold and pure. The girl’s service to Mother Holle (a nature goddess figure representing the stewardship of the natural sphere) results in the blessing of the earth. Jacob Grimm described her as "a being of the sky, begirdling the earth: when it snows, she is making her bed", "a goddess of no mean rank" (J. Grimm 1882, 267–268). The landscape also judges the two sisters: the diligent one is covered in gold (celestial good), while the lazy one is covered in pitch (evil/falsity), which "stuck fast to her, and could not be got off as long as she lived" (Grimm and Grimm 1884)—a vivid image of the Swedenborgian "ruling love" which constitutes the spirit’s eternal nature and cannot be changed after death. The diligent girl's gold is the "celestial good" of the golden age (*HH* §115).

## **IV. The Menagerie of the Mind: Animals as Moral Mirrors**

In Grimm’s fairy tales, talking animals are the most direct application of Swedenborgian correspondence. They are not merely characters; they are externalized faculties of the protagonist’s own soul, or the "world soul" responding to the hero’s moral quality. Swedenborg states, "Man is like an animal so far as his natural man is concerned … if he is gentle he is called a sheep or lamb, if fierce a bear or wolf, if cunning a fox or serpent" (*HH* §110). The animals speak because, in the spiritual world, affections are articulate.

### **4.1 The Queen Bee (KHM 62): The Hierarchy of Affections**

This tale is the *locus classicus* for Swedenborgian animal symbolism in the Grimm collection. It tells of three brothers: the two eldest are "clever" but cruel, seeking to destroy ant hills, kill ducks, and smoke out bees. The youngest, named Simpleton (*Dummling*), protects the animals (Grimm and Grimm 1884).

* **The Animals as Correspondences:**  
  * **Ants:** Industry, and the gathering of memory-knowledge on the sensual plane. Swedenborg names ants, with bees and silk-worms, among the creatures whose inborn knowledge shows "a resemblance to what is spiritual" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §61). The eldest brothers want to disrupt this order for amusement (a disorderly intellect disrupting uses).  
  * **Ducks:** Waterfowl join the two correspondences of Genesis 1: birds signify "rational and intellectual things," and the creatures of the waters "the memory-knowledges [*scientifica*] which belong to the external man" (*AC* §40)—the understanding at home in the truths of the natural mind (water). Killing them signifies the destruction of natural truths by the perverse will.  
  * **Bees:** The rational principle acting from natural good, collecting the "honey" of wisdom: "a love for the sweetness of usefulness" (Worcester 1875, 265). Swedenborg explicitly links bees to the order of heaven: they "set over them a mistress as queen," and "from influx from the spiritual world" have "a form of government similar to that among men on earth, and even like that of angels in heaven" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §355).  
* **Simpleton as the Celestial Man:** Simpleton represents the Swedenborgian concept of "Innocence"—a state often mistaken for stupidity by the worldly wise. Wilhelm Grimm read him so: "through the Simpleton worldly cleverness is humbled, for he, because he is pure of heart, alone wins happiness" (W. Grimm 1819). Because he protects these "affections" (animals), they serve him.  
  * *The Tasks:* The ants collect the scattered pearls (recovering truths from the dust of the natural world). The ducks retrieve the key from the lake (accessing the spiritual sense hidden in the waters of the letter). The Queen Bee identifies the correct princess by tasting the honey on her lips (perception of the "sweetness" of genuine good) (Grimm and Grimm 1884).  
* **Synthesis:** The tale demonstrates that the "innocent" will (Simpleton) can command the affections of nature, whereas the "clever" intellect (brothers) destroys them and is ultimately turned to stone—a correspondence for spiritual death or the "hardened heart".

### **4.2 The Frog King (KHM 1): The Repugnance of the Literal**

The iconic image of the frog in *The Frog King* (KHM 1\) serves as a potent Swedenborgian symbol of the "Natural Man" prior to regeneration.

* **The Golden Ball:** Correspondentially, gold signifies celestial good, in which the most ancient people lived (*HH* §115), and the sphere a state of wholeness: the golden ball is innocence held whole. Losing it in the well signifies the loss of innocent perception into the depths of the natural mind (water).  
* **The Frog:** Frogs correspond to "reasonings from mere falsities" (*AC* §7351), the sensual degree of the mind that croaks in the mud. In the tale, the frog is an enchanted prince. This signifies that the "natural man" or the "literal sense" of the Word appears repulsive (a cold, wet frog) to the "spiritual affection" (the princess).  
* **The Transformation:** The princess’s revulsion mirrors the natural mind’s aversion to spiritual discipline. The frog demands entry into her "house" (her mind) and her "bed" (her will/love). The climax, where she throws the frog against the wall, is crucial. In the Grimm version, she does *not* kiss him; she "threw him with all her might against the wall" (Grimm and Grimm 1884), violently rejecting the form. This violence mirrors the Swedenborgian concept of "temptation" and the breaking of the "hardened" self (selfhood). The shattering of the frog form releases the Prince (Spiritual Truth). It illustrates that the natural form must be "broken" or vastated for the spiritual reality to emerge. The frog is the "repulsive" exterior that holds the "golden" interior truth. In a Hessian variant the Grimms recorded, the frog troubles the water of the well and offers the princess "clear, clear water" if she will be his; when she consents, the water is so clear "that the sun fairly sparkled in it for joy" (Grimm and Grimm 1822).

### **4.3 The White Snake (KHM 17): The Consumption of Wisdom**

In *The White Snake*, a servant eats a piece of a white snake meant for the King—a king "famed for his wisdom through all the land"—and gains the ability to understand the language of animals (Grimm and Grimm 1884).

* **The Serpent:** Generally, the serpent corresponds to the "sensual" principle: "as serpents live close to the earth, so sensuous things are those next the body" (*AC* §195). However, among the most ancient people "by the 'serpent' was signified circumspection" (*AC* §197), and the *White* Snake represents that circumspection, the wisdom that can be extracted from the sensual degree when purified (whiteness). The Romantic physician G. H. Schubert, whose *Symbolik des Traumes* (1814) read the "hieroglyphic" picture-language of dreams, noted that in that language the serpent still signifies sensuality, and added: "One is reminded here of Swedenborg's dream-spirit-world. 'The bodily-sensual,' he says somewhere, 'is represented in the other life by serpents'" (Schubert 1814, 81).  
* **The Grimms' Reading:** The brothers noted the same motif in the legend of the Seeburg, where eating a white snake teaches "the language of animals", and added: "equivalent to it is eating the heart of a dragon or a bird" (Grimm and Grimm 1822); in Wilhelm's words, "whoever eats the heart, the liver of a bird receives supernatural powers" (W. Grimm 1819).  
* **Universal Communication:** By integrating this "wisdom of the senses," the servant enters into communion with all of nature. He hears what the animals (affections) are saying. This mirrors the state of the "Most Ancient Church" described by Swedenborg, where humanity possessed an intuitive knowledge of correspondences and could perceive the spiritual causes (affections) behind the natural forms (animals): "the most ancient men on our earth, who were celestial men, thought from correspondences themselves" (*HH* §115).  
* **Reciprocity:** Like Simpleton, the servant helps fishes, ants, and ravens. In return, they help him. This illustrates the "Grand Man" concept of mutual use: every degree of creation (from fish to man) serves the others.

### **4.4 The Faithful Horse: Falada and the Intellect**

In *The Goose Girl* (KHM 89), the princess is accompanied by a talking horse named Falada (Grimm and Grimm 1884). The Grimms called it a tale of "the majesty of the royal figure standing upright even in a servant's form", who each morning under the dark gate has "the conversation with the horse that stays faithful even in death"; "in the severed head (as in Mimir's) speech lives on" (Grimm and Grimm 1822).

* **The Horse:** In Swedenborg, the horse corresponds to the *Intellect* or the *Understanding of the Word*. A white horse represents the pure understanding of truth: "the 'white horse' is the understanding of the Word as to its interiors" (*AC* §2761).  
* **Falada’s Fate:** The false bride (the personification of the deceptive self or "self-hood") has Falada killed and his head mounted on the gate. Yet, the head still speaks truth. This signifies that even when the "understanding" is severed or destroyed by falsity in the external world, the "remains" of truth—"goods and truths from the Lord stored up in the interior man" (*AC* §3654)—can still testify to the conscience (the true princess).  
* **The Wind:** The wind that blows the Goose Boy's hat away at the princess's word corresponds to the spiritual influx that disrupts the disorderly thoughts of the natural mind, protecting the princess's hair (her spiritual power/beauty). In Swedenborg the wind that passes over the waters signifies "the disposal of all things into their order," the dispersal of invading fantasies (*AC* §842).

### **4.5 The Golden Bird (KHM 57): The Fox as Prudence**

This tale features a Fox who guides the youngest son.

* **The Fox:** Usually a symbol of cunning and deceit (*HH* §110), here the Fox acts as a wise guide. Swedenborg acknowledges that animals can have dual correspondences depending on the context: "most things in the Word have also an opposite sense" (*AC* §2460). Here, the Fox represents "prudence" or the "sagacity" of the natural mind when it is aligned with the spiritual. The Fox repeatedly warns the hero not to judge by appearances (e.g., "Buy no gallows'-flesh," and "beware of taking the bird out of the common cage and putting it into the golden one") (Grimm and Grimm 1884).  
* **The Lesson:** The hero constantly fails when he follows his eyes (sensual preference for the golden cage/saddle) rather than the Fox’s voice (inner prudence). The Fox eventually asks to be killed (shot), and is "no other than the brother of the beautiful princess," freed from enchantment (Grimm and Grimm 1884). Again, the destruction of the animal form releases the human spirit, symbolizing the sublimation of natural instinct into spiritual humanity.

## **V. Syncretism and the "Lost Word": A Structural Isomorphism**

The convergence between Grimm’s folklore and Swedenborg’s theology extends beyond individual symbols to the very structural logic of the narratives. Both were engaged in a project of *recovery*.

### **5.1 The Moral Physics of the Fairy Tale**

In a Grimm fairy tale, the physical world operates according to moral laws, not Newtonian ones. If a character is good, the birds warn them; if they are wicked, the trees might trap them.

* **Swedenborgian Principle:** The spiritual world is where "all causes are there," and the natural world is that "where all things that appear are effects" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §119). Therefore, a change in the "cause" (the moral state of the hero) *must* produce a change in the "effect" (the physical environment). This is "simultaneous order"—where the spiritual state is instantly readable in the natural object, since "the outmost degree is the complex, containant and base of the prior degrees" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §§205, 209).  
* **Example:** In *The Two Brothers* (KHM 60), a knife stuck in a tree rusts to signal that the brother is in danger. Physical matter responds telepathically to the state of the life force because they are connected by correspondence (Grimm and Grimm 1884).

### **5.2 The Syncretic Project**

Swedenborg argued that the "Ancient Church" "was spread over much of the Asiatic world," that the gods and demigods of the ancients were originally correspondential personifications whose custom the sages took from that church (*AC* §§4442, 1238), and that its representative worship "was turned into idolatry" when the internal meaning was lost (*AC* §2722). The Grimms noticed that their tales blended Christian and Pagan elements (W. Grimm 1819). By Swedenborg’s logic, this is natural because both Christianity and genuine Pagan myth stem from the same spiritual source—the Ancient Word. The "Wise Women" in *Sleeping Beauty* (Grimm and Grimm 1884)—kin, as Jacob Grimm showed, to the Norns of Germanic myth and the fays of Romance tales, which share "a common nature with our idises and norns," Briar Rose's spindle being the spindle of all "the wise-women of antiquity" (J. Grimm 1882, 411, 419)—becoming "Fairies" or "Godmothers" illustrates this continuity of spiritual function across religious epochs.

## **VI. Conclusion: The Enduring Mirror**

The "Nature Philosophy" of the Brothers Grimm is inextricably bound to the "Theological Physics" of Emanuel Swedenborg. Through the conduit of German Romanticism—specifically via Schelling’s digestion of Oetinger and Swedenborg, and in their own reading via Jung-Stilling—the Grimms inherited a worldview where nature was a script to be read, the Romantic idea of nature as hieroglyph that Benz traced to Swedenborg's doctrine of correspondence: "What we speak of as nature is a poem lying pent in a mysterious and wonderful script" (Schelling 1800, quoted in Hösle 2017, 110).

This investigation reveals that the "animate landscapes" and "talking animals" in the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* are not decorative whimsy. They are functional components of a spiritual technology.

1. **Landscapes** are externalizations of the soul’s state—forests as confusion, mountains as elevation, thorns as falsity.  
2. **Animals** are externalizations of the will’s affections—bees as industry, frogs as sensuality, horses as intellect.

In the end, the "Magic" of the Grimm fairy tales is the "Science of Correspondences" in narrative form. It reminds the reader that the physical world is a mask for the spiritual, and that true power lies not in dominating nature, but in aligning one's internal moral state with the "Divine Influx" that animates the landscape. To read Grimm through the lens of Swedenborg is to see the forest not as a collection of trees, but as a cathedral of the mind, where every leaf and stone preaches the gospel of the invisible.
