# Source 027: The Septuagint — Making the Torah Readable by the Synthesis Community

**Status**: Gap D research.

## The Core Finding

The Septuagint translation (3rd century BCE, Alexandria) was not merely a practical accommodation for Greek-speaking Jews who had lost their Hebrew. It was a translation that made the Torah accessible to the entire Alexandrian intellectual landscape — the Greek philosophers, the Hermetic practitioners, the Therapeutae. Without the Septuagint, the correspondential synthesis could not have occurred, because the Greek-speaking communities could not read the Hebrew text that contained the correspondential content.

## Historical Facts

- Commissioned under Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285-246 BCE) — the same king who built up the Library of Alexandria
- The Letter of Aristeas (2nd c. BCE, likely pseudepigraphical) claims 72 translators from Jerusalem produced the translation in 72 days
- Scholarly consensus: the Pentateuch was translated in the early-to-mid 3rd century BCE; other books followed over the next two centuries
- The translators were described as having "not only acquired proficiency in Jewish literature, but had studied most carefully that of the Greeks as well" — BILINGUAL intellectuals
- Produced in Alexandria specifically, not elsewhere

## Why This Matters for the Thesis

The Septuagint is the ENABLING condition for the Alexandrian synthesis:

1. **Philo** read Scripture in Greek (the Septuagint). All his correspondential readings of Genesis — Adam = nous, Eve = aisthesis, the four rivers = four virtues — were produced from the Greek text. Without the Septuagint, Philo could not have done what he did.

2. **The Therapeutae** read Scripture in Greek. Their "books composed by the founders of their sect on the allegorical method" interpreted the Septuagint, not the Hebrew original.

3. **The Hermetic tradition** could recognize parallels between the Poimandres creation narrative and Genesis 1 BECAUSE Genesis 1 was available in Greek.

4. **The NHL communities** used Coptic translations derived from the Septuagint (via Greek). The Sethian texts (Apocryphon of John, Hypostasis of the Archons) engage with Genesis THROUGH its Greek form.

5. **Origen's Hexapla** — his six-column Bible placing the Septuagint alongside the Hebrew and other Greek versions — was the culmination of the Alexandrian tradition of working with the Greek text.

The Septuagint didn't just translate words. It translated the Torah into the intellectual medium of the synthesis community. It was the bridge between the Hebrew correspondential content and the Greek-speaking world that would work with it.

## The Ptolemaic Connection

Ptolemy II commissioned the Septuagint AND built up the Library. The same royal project that was consolidating all knowledge in Alexandria also made the Jewish sacred texts available in Greek. This is not coincidence — it's the same enterprise. The Ptolemies were building the universal repository, and the Torah was part of it.

The Letter of Aristeas — even if fictional — reveals the 2nd-century self-understanding: the Septuagint was seen as making the Torah's "universal wisdom" available to the world. The translators demonstrate Stoic moral philosophy at the royal banquet, showing that Jewish wisdom and Greek philosophy speak the same language.

## A Correspondential Reading of the Translation

The Septuagint translation was itself an act of correspondential transmission — the same spiritual content expressed in a new natural form (Greek instead of Hebrew). The content was constant; the form changed. This is what the library calls "re-expression" rather than "adaptation" — the translator perceives THROUGH the Hebrew form to the content and expresses it in Greek while preserving the correspondence.

The fact that the LXX sometimes DIFFERS from the Masoretic text in ways that open new correspondential readings (e.g., Isaiah 7:14 — almah → parthenos) shows that the translators were not merely converting words but making interpretive choices that could carry correspondential weight.
