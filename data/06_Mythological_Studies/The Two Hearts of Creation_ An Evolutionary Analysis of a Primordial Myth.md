## **I. The Primordial Matrix: Reconstructing a Common Ancestor Myth**

The creation stories of the Ancient Near East, from the Tigris to the Nile, were not born in isolation. They are distinct dialects of a single, ancient conversation about cosmic origins. While the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* and the Hebrew Genesis 1 present starkly different theological conclusions, they share a common narrative grammar—a structural template that points toward a shared ancestor, a proto-myth that predates them both (Heidel 1951, quoted in Cho 2018, 86). This hypothetical source narrative, reflecting the shared environment and fundamental questions of the region's earliest peoples, likely contained a few core, unadorned elements:

1. **A Primordial Watery State:** Before creation, there was only a formless, undifferentiated, watery chaos. This reflects a universal experience in a world dominated by great rivers and the sea.  
2. **The Act of Separation:** The first creative act is one of division. A divine force separates this chaos, creating distinct realms: light is separated from darkness, and the waters are separated to form the sky above and the sea below.  
3. **The Emergence of Land:** From the contained waters, dry land appears.  
4. **The Creation of Humanity:** Humans are fashioned, often from clay or earth, by a divine being.  
5. **Divine Rest:** After the work of ordering is complete, the divine force ceases its labor.

This simple sequence forms the primordial matrix. It is a story not of material creation from nothing, but of *functional* creation—the bringing of order to a pre-existing, non-functional chaos (Walton 2008). This proto-myth was the common inheritance, a narrative canvas onto which different cultures, guided by their own "hearts," would paint vastly different portraits of the divine and the human. The divergence between the *Enuma Elish* and Genesis 1 is the story of two such artistic traditions, each evolving this shared source material through small, incremental steps to reflect its own core values.

## **II. The Heart of Division: The Politicization of Myth in Mesopotamia**

The worldview of the *Enuma Elish*—one of conflict, violence, and hierarchy—is a direct reflection of the political heart of its creators: the imperial ambitions of Babylon. The evolution from the simple proto-myth to this complex political epic was not a single leap but a gradual process of adaptation, a bricolage where existing myths were repurposed to serve a new agenda.  
The "small step" that set this evolutionary path in motion was the fusion of the simple creation proto-myth with a separate, pre-existing, and very popular story type: the "chaos-combat" myth. Earlier Mesopotamian myths, such as the Sumerian tales of the god Ninurta or the Akkadian *Myth of Anzu*, already told stories of a heroic younger god battling a monstrous, chaotic entity to establish order and prove his supremacy. These were not originally creation stories; they were tales of divine heroism and the maintenance of cosmic stability (Walton 2008; Lambert 2013).  
The genius of the Babylonian scribes was to see that this chaos-combat motif "almost aligned" with the primordial chaos of the creation proto-myth. They performed an act of literary fusion: the formless watery abyss of the original story was personified as the chaos-dragon goddess, Tiamat. The act of separation was no longer a peaceful ordering but a violent dismemberment of Tiamat's corpse by the hero-god Marduk (*Enuma Elish* IV.137–140). This adaptation was not a radical invention but a clever recombination of familiar narrative elements.  
This fusion served a clear political purpose. By casting Marduk, the patron god of Babylon, in the role of the victorious hero-god, the myth provided a divine charter for Babylon's historical rise to power over other Mesopotamian city-states. The story no longer just explained the origin of the cosmos; it now explained and legitimized the political reality of the Babylonian empire. The creation of humanity as slaves from the blood of a rebel god was a logical extension of this worldview, reflecting a society built on hierarchy and servitude (*Enuma Elish* VI.29–34). The *Enuma Elish*, therefore, is the end product of an evolutionary path where the simple proto-myth was incrementally adapted and fused with conflict narratives to perfectly align with the political and imperialistic heart of its tellers.

## **III. The Heart of Unity: The Demythologization of Myth in Israel**

The authors of Genesis 1 were also *bricoleurs*, working with the same primordial matrix as their Mesopotamian neighbors. However, their "heart" was radically different, animated by an impulse toward unity, order, and the absolute transcendence of a single Creator. Their adaptation of the proto-myth was not a rejection of it, but a profound and deliberate purification of it. They did not invent a new story but took the shared story and systematically stripped it of the very elements the Babylonians had added.  
The "almost aligns" source for the Genesis authors was not the chaos-combat myth, but the ancient West Semitic tradition of the high god, El. In the Canaanite pantheon, El was the supreme deity, the "Creator of Creatures," a wise and patriarchal figure who ruled from a distance. This tradition provided a conceptual foundation for a creator who was sovereign and non-combative. The Israelite scribes, in a process of theological evolution, fused the attributes of this high creator god with their national god, Yahweh, and elevated the concept to one of absolute monotheism (Cross 1973; Smith 2002).  
Armed with this "heart of unity," they approached the shared proto-myth and performed a series of precise, polemical adaptations:

* **The Taming of Chaos:** They recognized the primordial water, the *tehom*, but deliberately refused to personify it. The linguistic link to Tiamat is retained, making the polemic direct and unmistakable, but the character is demoted from a divine rival to an inanimate substance under God's effortless control (Lam 2023).

The non-personal water is not a counter-narrative but preservation of proto-Semitic cosmology.  
* **The Subjugation of the Gods:** They took the elements that other cultures deified—the sun, the moon, the stars, the great sea creatures—and included them in the story, but pointedly re-categorized them as mere "creations." The sun and moon are not even given their proper names, lest they be confused with deities; they are simply "lights" created on the fourth day to serve a function (Gen 1:14–18).  
* **Creation by Word, Not War:** The creative mechanism is not theomachy (divine combat) but divine fiat. The effortless, orderly sequence of "And God said..." is a direct refutation of the violent, chaotic narrative of Marduk's battle.

This was not the creation of a new story, but a masterful re-reading of the old one. The authors of Genesis saw the polytheistic and violent elements of myths like the *Enuma Elish* not as the original story, but as heretical additions—corruptions of a purer, simpler truth. Their work was an act of restoration, adapting the shared myth to align perfectly with their monotheistic heart. The result is a narrative that is both recognizable within its ancient context and a radical departure from it.

## **IV. The Two Hearts of Cognition: A Re-evaluation of the Cognitive Science of Religion**

The Cognitive Science of Religion (CSR) invites a fundamental critique. The standard CSR model, which posits that religion is a byproduct of cognitive mechanisms like the Hypersensitive Agency Detection Device (HADD), operates from an unstated philosophical premise of metaphysical materialism. This assumption—that only the material world is real—forces the conclusion that any perception of a non-material, spiritual reality must be a cognitive error or illusion. This is not a scientific finding but a philosophical belief system in itself (Lee 2021; Nikkel 2025).  
When we examine CSR's core mechanism, HADD, we see that it provides a powerful cognitive explanation for the "heart of division." HADD is a cognitive default that perceives agency everywhere, naturally generating a world populated by multiple, localized spirits and gods—the foundation of animism and polytheism (Barrett 2004). From a purely CSR perspective, polytheism is the path of least cognitive resistance; it is what the human brain does naturally.  
This makes monotheism a profound cognitive anomaly. The "heart of unity" requires a high-resistance cognitive override. It demands the suppression of the default HADD impulse and a sophisticated act of abstraction to unify all causal power into a single, transcendent source. The standard CSR model struggles to explain this leap, often resorting to later socio-political explanations, because its own foundational mechanism predicts the opposite outcome (Costello 2016).  
This is where a factual paradox becomes critical. The ethnographic evidence collected by anthropologists like Andrew Lang and Wilhelm Schmidt for "primordial monotheism" (*Urmonotheismus*) directly challenges the CSR model. Their research documented that many of the world's most technologically simple societies hold a belief in a single, moral, creator High God (Lang 1900; Schmidt 1931; Cox 2017). This widespread phenomenon cannot be easily explained if the human cognitive default is purely polytheistic.  
This suggests a more complex cognitive reality. Human consciousness may not have a single religious default, but rather two competing, primordial impulses:

1. A **Fragmenting, Agent-Detecting Impulse (HADD):** An evolutionarily pragmatic tool that perceives multiple, immanent agents in the world. This is the cognitive engine of the "heart of division."  
2. A **Unifying, Philosophical Impulse:** An innate capacity for abstract causal reasoning that seeks a single, ultimate cause or "maker of things" (Lang 1900, preface). This is the cognitive engine of the "heart of unity."

The history of religion is not a simple, linear evolution from one to the other, but the perpetual tension and interplay between these two fundamental modes of human thought.

## **Conclusion: An Evolution of the Heart**

This inquiry leads to a more nuanced and powerful solution. The vast difference between the *Enuma Elish* and Genesis 1 is not an unbridgeable gap but the result of a long, divergent evolution from a common proto-mythical ancestor. These two paths were not arbitrary; they were guided by the "hearts" of their respective cultures, which in turn were expressions of two fundamental and competing cognitive impulses within the human spirit.  
The Mesopotamian tradition, reflecting a "heart of division," embraced the cognitively simple path of agency detection. Its storytellers, acting as *bricoleurs*, fused the proto-myth with pre-existing conflict narratives, creating a politically useful epic that mirrored their hierarchical and imperial worldview.  
The Israelite tradition, reflecting a "heart of unity," took the more cognitively demanding path of abstraction and unification. Its *bricoleurs* drew upon the West Semitic tradition of a high creator god and systematically purified the proto-myth of its polytheistic and combative elements, creating a radical theological statement that aligned with their core values of order, goodness, and the sovereignty of a single Creator.  
The stories we tell are indeed reflections of what is in our hearts. The parallel creation myths of the Ancient Near East are the ultimate proof of this principle, revealing two distinct human hearts, both drawing from the same ancient wellspring of myth to tell the story of the world as they understood it to be.
