# The Format That Cannot Say When: Recognition Without Tense in the Memory Sciences

## Demonstrating the Structural Identity of Déjà Vu and the Uncontextualised Sensory Representation

---

> **Abstract.** This thesis argues that a déjà vu and a sensory representation firing without its contextual counterpart are one event described in two vocabularies. The claim is not a prediction and cannot be cited: no one in either literature has proposed it, and dual representation theory has never been extended to déjà vu. What is available is coherence, and coherence is defensible only by itemisation. Fourteen properties are accounted for; eleven match. Two of the eleven could not have been arranged after the fact — Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph defined their second memory system in 1996 as the one "automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues," a statement of déjà vu's trigger condition written by authors with no interest in it; and Cleary's finding that déjà vu can be produced by parahippocampal configural resemblance meets Meyer's finding that efficiency at exactly that encoding predicts perceptual intrusion rate, in two literatures that do not cite one another. Three properties fail and are reported with the rest. The identification has a consequence that falls on the framework proposing it. A representation without a contextual index carries no tense, so felt direction is assigned downstream and a report that an experience felt like foreknowledge is inadmissible as evidence that it was. Every reportable property of the experience is then fixed by the format and constant across instances, including two of the five structural signatures the parent thesis identified in near-death temporal preview. One variable survives: what laid the representation down. Three origins are live — dreaming, unretrieved waking exposure, and constructive simulation — and nothing currently decides among them. They are the routes for which a mechanism has been published, not an enumeration of the sources that are possible, and no part of the argument depends on which is correct or on the correct one being among them. The framework's own account of boundary states is not a fourth entry in that list, and §6.4 sets out why an origin claim of that kind is unavailable to it: the framework makes no claim about which physical process laid anything down, so it neither requires a gap in the physical account nor is entitled to propose one. The same subsection sets out why the inverse inference is equally unavailable — an account complete at the level it works on settles nothing about whether that level is exhaustive — so that nothing here counts for or against the framework in either direction.

> **Keywords.** déjà vu, dual representation theory, S-rep, C-rep, contextual index, familiarity without recollection, entorhinal cortex, electrical brain stimulation, configural encoding, temporal preview, state-perception, coherence, correspondence

## 1. Introduction

The companion thesis *[The Future That Cannot Be Remembered Until It Arrives](The%20Future%20That%20Cannot%20Be%20Remembered%20Until%20It%20Arrives_%20Temporal%20Preview%20in%20Near-Death%20Experience%20as%20State-Perception.md)* analysed 6,753 structured near-death accounts and identified five structural signatures in the reports of temporal preview. Two of those signatures were properties not of the content but of its *form*: the received material could not be retrieved by ordinary recall until the corresponding moment arrived, and when the moment arrived it was met by recognition rather than by memory. That thesis proposed, in a single subsection near its end, that these two properties are not peculiar to preview at all — that they are the ordinary behaviour of a known representational format, and that a déjà vu and an uncontextualised sensory representation firing without its contextual counterpart are one event described in two vocabularies. It then discounted its own proposal, correctly, on the ground that the identification was argued rather than cited and inherited no empirical support from either literature.

That subsection is the origin of the present thesis, and the reason for a separate treatment is that the proposal was mis-weighted where it stood. It was introduced as a supporting step, brought in to discharge a debt the parent argument owed elsewhere. But an identification of this kind is not a supporting step. If it holds, it is the more general claim, and the preview case is a special instance of it. The parent thesis leaned its evidentiary weight on a corpus of retrospective self-reports whose central weakness it acknowledged in detail, while treating as a minor auxiliary the one claim in it that engages a mechanism established by direct experimental production. The present thesis reverses that arrangement and takes the identification as its subject.

The question it asks is therefore narrow and prior to any question about preview: **what form of claim can be made about the relation between déjà vu and dual representation theory, and does the evidence support a claim of that form?** The answer defended here is that the available claim is neither prediction nor citation but **coherence** — that every property the déjà vu literature attributes to the experience meets a specification the trauma-memory literature wrote down independently, in detail, and without reference to déjà vu at all — and that coherence at the observed density is a real result, of a kind that cannot be arranged after the fact.

The central finding is the accounting itself, set out in §4. Fourteen properties are considered. Eleven of them — drawn from the déjà vu literature's own descriptions of the experience — meet specifications that the trauma-memory literature wrote down independently for the sensory-bound representation. Two of the eleven are convergences of a kind that neither research programme was in a position to produce deliberately, and they carry the argument. The first is verbal. Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph, writing in 1996 about post-traumatic intrusion, defined their second memory system as the one "automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues" — which is not a phrase merely *consistent with* déjà vu's trigger condition but a statement of it, written by authors with no interest in the phenomenon. The second is quantitative. Cleary's laboratory established that déjà vu can be produced by configural resemblance between a present scene and a previously encountered spatial layout; Meyer and colleagues, testing dual representation theory with the contextual cuing paradigm, established that individual efficiency at precisely that form of configural learning predicts the rate of perceptual intrusion (*r* = −.33, *p* < .01). Two programmes, one parahippocampal variable, opposite ends of a single relationship — and the two literatures do not cite one another. The remaining three properties do not match, and they are reported at full strength in §4.3 rather than absorbed: the affective mismatch between aversive intrusion and neutral déjà vu, an untested interaction between representational production and contextual binding, and a straightforward absence where dual representation theory makes no statement about duration.

The mechanism on the trauma side is a standard model with tested predictions. The mechanism on the déjà vu side is established by production rather than by correlation, which is a stronger evidential position than most of the phenomenology of memory occupies: Bancaud and colleagues evoked the experience by direct electrical stimulation on forty-three occasions in fourteen patients, and Bartolomei and colleagues, across 280 stimulations in twenty-four patients, separated the bare familiarity signal from the retrieval of remembered content by anatomical site. §3.4 argues that a widespread gloss on this literature — that what the stimulation demonstrates is a brain misfiring — is a classificatory judgement laid over the finding rather than the finding itself. What the stimulation demonstrates is which structures generate the experience and that the familiarity signal is dissociable from remembered content. Whether a signal so produced is spurious is a separate question, decided elsewhere and on other grounds.

The central argument is that the identification, once granted, changes what has to be explained. If a déjà vu is a sensory representation firing without its context, then a representation carrying no contextual index carries **no tense**, because a contextual index is exactly what supplies one. A vehicle of this kind cannot deliver direction, and any direction the experience is reported with was assigned downstream by the system that had to file it. This has an immediate consequence for the reading of Cleary and Claxton's finding that experiencers report heightened feelings of prediction during déjà vu while displaying no actual predictive ability. Read as a result about foresight, it is a debunking. Read from the format, it is what the format entails: familiarity and premonition are two interpretations laid over one tenseless signal, and both should scale with signal strength, which is what was observed. §5.2 develops this and marks it explicitly as a reading rather than a result.

The larger consequence is structural. If the two are one event, then the properties that made temporal preview look anomalous — content that cannot be reported until the moment arrives, and arrival met by recognition — are not special properties of preview at all. They are the ordinary behaviour of the format. What then remains distinctive about a preview case is exactly one thing: **what laid the representation down.** The entire burden relocates from form to origin, and §6 sets out the three candidate origins that are live — nightly dreaming, unretrieved waking exposure, and constructive simulation of anticipated scenes — together with what would discriminate among them. Nothing currently does. That is stated as the open question it is, and §8.4 records why the temptation to close it prematurely is the specific hazard this framework has to guard against. Section 6.4 sets out separately why the framework's own account of boundary states does not belong in that list, which is a constraint the framework imposes on itself rather than one imposed by the memory literature.

What this thesis does not claim should be stated before the argument begins rather than after it, and there is more of it than there is of the claim. It does not claim that dual representation theory predicts déjà vu; the theory was built for intrusive imagery in psychopathology and has never been extended to it, and the identification inherits none of its empirical support. And it does not claim that the identification establishes a referent for the familiarity signal. Removing an unwarranted premise reopens a question; it does not answer one. The parent thesis's verification deficit — 40 verified and 66 partially verified claims out of 435 — is untouched by anything here, and §7.3 states precisely why prospective registration, the one methodological reform that would improve that literature, cannot reach the claim this thesis makes in either direction.

Three further limits fix the scope of the claim, and they are easier to lose than the claim itself. The first is that this is an argument about **format and not about source.** To identify the representational form in which déjà vu content is carried is to say what kind of vehicle it travels in — how it is held, why it cannot be retrieved on demand, why it arrives when a situation arrives, why it carries no tense. It is not to say where the content came from. Section 5.3 makes that separation the thesis's central structural result: the format is silent on its own provenance, which is the reason origin has to be treated as a separate question at all.

The second is that this is an argument about **one class of experience and not about memory.** Dual representation theory posits two systems; the identification concerns one of them, in the specific condition of firing without the other. Nothing here is a claim about how a contextualised representation forms, about what happens when content becomes ordinary conscious recollection, or about how the two systems stand to one another in the general case. Those questions are open, are not addressed, and do not need to be answered for anything in §§4–7 to stand.

The third concerns §6 and is the limit most easily overread. Section 6 examines three routes by which the representation could have been laid down, and it does so because the identification makes origin the only remaining variable — not because the thesis is in any position to say which route operated. The three are the routes for which published mechanisms exist. They are **not an enumeration of the sources that are possible**, and no such enumeration is available to anyone: that a source can be pinpointed in a laboratory says nothing about whether other sources exist, and a survey of demonstrated routes cannot close a field it did not survey. Nothing in the argument depends on which candidate is correct, or on the correct one being among them. What §6 establishes is narrower, and is all that is required of it — that ordinary routes exist, and that they are sufficient to produce the phenomenon, so that nothing here *has to be* explained by an extraordinary one. Sufficiency removes a necessity. It does not remove a possibility, and §6.4 and §8.4 set out why this thesis is not entitled to convert the one into the other in either direction.

The thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 sets out the method: correspondential reading as functional identity (§2.1); the logic of a coherence claim and the conditions under which such a claim carries weight (§2.2); how the two literatures were read and what was excluded (§2.3); and a distinction between specification and voluntary access on which a later step depends (§2.4). Section 3 presents both literatures as they stand, including the stimulation evidence and the separation of that evidence from the interpretation habitually attached to it. Section 4 presents the accounting, the two convergences, the three failures, and the scale deficit that remains. Section 5 derives tenselessness and its consequences. Section 6 sets out the candidate origins. Section 7 states what all of this does to the parent thesis, including where it weakens it. Section 8 discusses and records limitations. Section 9 concludes. The appendix consolidates the eleven properties with their sources and their status.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 Correspondential Reading: Function, Not Resemblance

Correspondence in this framework is functional identity across discrete degrees, not resemblance, allegory, symbol, or metaphor. A natural object corresponds to a spiritual reality when it performs, at the natural degree, the office that the spiritual reality performs at its own — the relation is one of *use*, not of likeness, and it runs in one direction only, from inner to outer. The outer is the inner expressed in ultimates. It does not represent the inner to an observer; there is no observer for whom the representation is staged, and nothing is being encoded.

This matters for the present thesis chiefly as a constraint on what may be said, because the great bulk of the argument below is conducted at the natural plane and makes no correspondential claim at all. The accounting in §4 is a comparison of two bodies of published empirical work. The derivation in §5.1 is an analytic consequence of a definition. The survey of candidate origins in §6 is a review of competing natural-plane explanations, three of which are entirely mundane. A reader who grants none of the framework's premises can follow all of it and is invited to.

The correspondential reading enters at two points and is confined to them. The first is §8.3, where the natural-plane finding — that a representation without a contextual index has no tense, and that succession is supplied by the receiving system rather than carried by the content — is read against the framework's prior commitment that extension and succession are properties of the natural degree rather than of consciousness as such. The second is the reading of recognition itself: on the framework's account, what a state's arrival in ultimates produces is identification rather than retrieval, and identification of a condition one already occupies produces alreadyness necessarily. Both readings are marked where they occur, and neither is used to license any empirical conclusion in §§3–7.

The discipline this imposes is worth stating explicitly, because the failure mode it guards against is the one this framework is most exposed to. A correspondential reading may be offered where it illuminates, but it may never be back-filled into a gap the natural-plane evidence has left open, and it may never be used to make a thin empirical result look prescient. Where the corpus of framework documents is silent on a question, the correct report is that it is silent. §8.4 returns to this at length, because the identification argued here is precisely the kind of finding that invites the error.

### 2.2 What a Coherence Claim Is, and What It Is Not

The claim this thesis defends is of an unusual type, and misidentifying the type is the most likely way to misread what follows. It is therefore set out here in advance, together with the conditions under which such a claim carries weight and the conditions under which it would fail.

Three kinds of claim are available when one body of work is brought to bear on another. The first is **prediction**: theory A entails observation X, X had not been observed, X was then observed. This is the strongest form and it is not available here. Dual representation theory entails nothing about déjà vu, because it was never pointed at déjà vu; no one has derived a novel déjà vu observation from it and gone looking. The second is **citation**: the identification of A with B is established in the literature and can simply be invoked. This is not available either, and its unavailability is total. Brewin's framework was built for intrusive imagery in psychopathology. It has never been extended to déjà vu by anyone. The identification proposed here is this thesis's own, and it inherits no empirical support whatever from either research programme.

The third is **coherence**, and it is what remains. The form of a coherence claim is this: two descriptions were produced independently, by different people, for different purposes, without reference to one another; when laid side by side they are found to specify the same object in detail; and the detail is dense enough, and specific enough, that the correspondence is not plausibly an artefact of how the comparison was constructed. A coherence claim asserts that two vocabularies are describing one thing. It does not assert that either literature has demonstrated this, and it does not add a single new observation to the world.

Coherence is weaker than prediction and it is not nothing. Its evidential value comes from a single feature: **it cannot be arranged retroactively.** Both literatures are published and fixed. Brewin's 1996 formulation cannot be adjusted now to fit déjà vu's trigger condition more closely; Meyer's correlation cannot be re-run to align better with Cleary's variable. If the descriptions match in detail, they matched before anyone looked, and the only two available explanations are that the match is coincidental or that the two are describing the same object. The strength of the claim is therefore a direct function of how much detail the match survives — which is why §4 proceeds by itemisation rather than by argument, and why the items that fail are reported in the same table as the items that hold. An accounting that reported only successes would forfeit exactly the property that gives the method its force.

Four conditions have to be met for the coherence to count, and each is checkable:

1. **Independence.** The two literatures must not have influenced one another. If either cites the other on the point at issue, the match may be inheritance rather than convergence.
2. **Specificity.** The matched properties must be detailed rather than schematic. Any two accounts of memory will agree that memory involves retrieval; that is not a convergence. A shared commitment to configural spatial encoding in the parahippocampus is.
3. **Density.** A small number of matches invites coincidence. The claim requires that the correspondence hold across the full set of properties either literature considers characteristic.
4. **Disclosure of failures.** Properties that do not match must be counted and reported, since a match rate is meaningless without its denominator.

What would defeat the claim is correspondingly definite. If the properties dual representation theory entails were found not to follow in déjà vu — if, for instance, déjà vu proved to be driven by verbal rather than configural resemblance, or if its generation proved anatomically unrelated to the structures implicated in sensory-bound representation — the identification would fail. If systematic scoring of the déjà vu survey literature against the specification (§4.4) returned a phenomenology substantially unlike the one described here, it would fail. And if the failures in §4.3 proved not to be gaps but contradictions — if aversiveness turned out to be constitutive of the format rather than of its typical content — it would fail. None of these is remote or contrived. They are the ordinary ways an identification of this kind comes apart.

One further clarification, because it bears on how §5 should be read. Establishing that two descriptions pick out one object says nothing about what produced the object in any given instance. The coherence claim is about **format**. It is silent on **origin**, and §6 treats origin as fully open. Any argument that slid from "this is the format" to "therefore this is where it came from" would be a non sequitur, and the fact that one of the candidate origins is the one this framework has an interest in makes the slide more tempting rather than less.

### 2.3 The Two Literatures and How They Were Read

Every empirical source used in §§3–6 was read at its published abstract or full text rather than taken from a secondary summary or a reference list, and each is cited with the journal, volume, and page range recorded at the source. This is stated because the parent thesis contained at least one citation that had been carried from a reference list without verification, and the error it produced was not trivial: a set of percentages from Bancaud and colleagues (1994) was reproduced as though it ranked generative sites, when the figures count a structure as involved whether it was stimulated or merely received the spread of discharge. Read that way the numbers appear to support neocortical primacy, which inverts the paper's own conclusion. The corrected reading is given in §3.3.

The retrieval strategy was deliberately asymmetric. For the trauma-memory side the relevant sources are few and canonical, and the two statements of the theory (Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph 1996; Brewin, Gregory, Lipton, and Burgess 2010) together with one direct empirical test of it (Meyer et al. 2013) carry the whole of what is needed. For the déjà vu side the literature is larger and more heterogeneous, and sources were selected to cover four distinct evidential categories: prevalence and survey phenomenology (Brown 2003; Adachi et al. 2003; Hadzic and Andersson 2024), experimental induction (Cleary et al. 2012; Cleary and Claxton 2018; Cleary et al. 2021; Urquhart et al. 2021), electrical stimulation in epilepsy monitoring (Bancaud et al. 1994; Bartolomei et al. 2004; Curot et al. 2018), and results bearing adversely on the identification (Adachi et al. 2008; O'Connor and Moulin 2013). Sources in the fourth category were sought deliberately, and §4.3 and §8.5 report what they cost.

Three exclusions were applied, and each removes material that would otherwise inflate the apparent match.

**Ictal déjà vu is not treated as continuous with the non-ictal experience.** The stimulation studies are used for one purpose only — to establish which structures generate the experience, and that the familiarity signal is dissociable from remembered content — and not as evidence about the prevalence, character, or origin of déjà vu in the general population. A seizure-associated experience in a patient with temporal lobe epilepsy and a spontaneous experience in a healthy adult are treated as sharing a generator, not as being the same event. The 2024 meta-analysis reports non-ictal and ictal prevalence separately, and only the non-ictal figure is used.

**Déjà rêve is not folded into déjà vu.** Curot and colleagues (2018) demonstrate that the two are distinct entities: across their own database and a review of the literature they collected 42 stimulation-induced déjà rêve episodes, resolved the term into three separate phenomena, and localised the episodic-like and familiarity-like variants principally to the medial temporal lobes. The distinction is consequential for §6.1, where the dream route is assessed, because a substantial part of the published correlation between dreaming and déjà vu is in fact a correlation with déjà rêve — the experiencer's *attribution* of a present familiarity to a remembered dream — which is a different variable and cannot be substituted for the one at issue.

**Attributional data are not treated as origin data.** When a survey records that an experiencer traced a déjà vu to a prior dream, that is a datum about what the experiencer concluded, not about what laid the representation down. The distinction is maintained throughout §6, where it does substantial work, and its neglect is the principal reason the dream-origin question has remained unresolved despite a large literature.

One source is used but was not verified at first hand and is flagged accordingly. Brown's 2004 monograph *The Déjà Vu Experience* is a book rather than an indexed article, and the figures attributed to it in the parent thesis — that roughly a fifth of déjà vu experiences are attributed by the experiencer to a prior dream, and about two fifths to dreams and waking life together — could not be checked against the source in preparing this thesis. They are reported here as attributed rather than as confirmed. Nothing in the argument turns on their exact values; §6.1 would be unchanged if they were somewhat different, since the exclusion above already restricts what attributional figures of this kind can establish.

### 2.4 Specification and Voluntary Access: A Distinction the Argument Requires

One distinction has to be fixed before the accounting begins, because a later step depends on it and because collapsing it produces a specific and attractive error.

A sensory-bound representation lacks a contextual index and cannot be retrieved voluntarily. It does **not** lack detail. This is not a refinement of the theory but a plain feature of the clinical phenomenon it was built to explain: a flashback is among the most highly specified experiences a person ever has, and its specificity is precisely what makes it clinically consequential. Nobody describes an intrusive traumatic memory as vague. The absent element is the index — the binding to a time, a place, and a position in a narrative — together with the voluntary access that an index makes possible.

**Specification and voluntary access are therefore two axes, not one.** Content may be highly detailed and wholly involuntary; that combination is the ordinary case in the trauma literature. Content may be poorly detailed and freely retrievable; that is much of ordinary autobiographical memory. Treating the two axes as a single dimension running from *vague and uncontrollable* to *detailed and retrievable* licenses an inference that does not follow: that material which arrives fully specified, or which its recipient can subsequently narrate in order, cannot have been carried by a sensory-bound representation. It can.

Two consequences follow, and both are used later.

The first concerns cue-delivered content that is acted upon. If a situation arrives and the representation fires with its content intact, the recipient has access to that content at that moment — richly, immediately, and without having chosen it. Acting on such content is not evidence against the format; it is a description of the format operating. What the recipient cannot do is summon the content beforehand, or locate its source, or produce it on request. The involuntariness is in the addressing, not in the fidelity.

The second concerns subsequent narratability, and it is the more important. Contextualisation is not a property that a representation either possesses permanently or permanently lacks. In dual representation theory it is a **process that acts upon a sensory representation**, and it is the process on which the entire therapeutic model rests: treatment for post-traumatic stress works, on this account, by constructing contextual representations for material that was laid down without them. It follows directly that a representation may be formed without an index and acquire one afterwards. Whether it does so depends on what happens next — and deliberate narration, performed soon enough after the material arrives and before it disperses, is that process carried out by hand.

The practical upshot is a rule for reading cases. That an account is now narratable in sequence, and has remained stable, establishes that a contextual representation exists **now**. It establishes nothing about whether one existed at formation. The inference from present narratability to original contextualisation is invalid, and any argument that sorts cases into *sensory-bound* and *contextual* on the basis of how well they can be told today is sorting on the wrong variable. §7.1 applies this rule; §8.5 records that it also cuts against a conclusion drawn in the parent thesis.

## 3. The Two Literatures

### 3.1 Dual Representation Theory: C-Reps and S-Reps

Dual representation theory was proposed by Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph in *Psychological Review* in 1996 to account for a specific clinical puzzle: why the memory disturbance in post-traumatic stress takes two apparently contradictory forms at once. Patients cannot reliably narrate what happened to them, and simultaneously cannot stop it from returning. Deliberate recall is impoverished; involuntary recall is overwhelming. A single-store account of memory has difficulty producing both symptoms from one mechanism, and the theory's proposal was that two systems are operating rather than one. The original formulation distinguished a *verbally accessible* system from a *situationally accessible* system, and characterised the second — in the phrase that will matter throughout §4 — as being **"automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues."**

Brewin, Gregory, Lipton, and Burgess restated the theory in the same journal in 2010, embedding it in a neural-systems account of ordinary memory and imagery rather than treating it as a special theory of pathology, and supplying the terminology used here. Their statement is exact: the evidence "implies distinct neural bases to abstract, flexible, contextualized representations (C-reps) and to inflexible, sensory-bound representations (S-reps)." The revision also specifies how the two interact, via visuospatial working memory, which is the basis of the intervention literature that has grown up around the model.

The two representational types differ on a small number of properties, and it is worth listing them plainly because §4 checks each one:

A **C-rep** is contextualised. It is bound to a time, a place, and a position within a narrative sequence; it is abstract and flexible, in the sense that it can be re-described, summarised, reordered, and integrated with other knowledge; and it can be retrieved voluntarily, by deliberate search, in the absence of any external prompt. This is the representational form of ordinary autobiographical memory, and it is what allows a person to answer the question "what happened to you in 2019?"

An **S-rep** is none of these. It is sensory-bound rather than abstract — perceptual in character, carrying the qualities of the original perceptual episode rather than a summary of it. It is inflexible: it does not re-describe, reorder, or integrate. It is cue-triggered rather than voluntarily retrievable, addressed only by the arrival of a matching situation. And, decisively for everything that follows, it carries **no contextual index**: no binding to a time, a place, or a narrative position.

The clinical consequence of the missing index is the flashback, and its defining oddity is not its vividness but its **tense**. The content of a flashback is not experienced as a memory of something that happened. It is experienced as happening, now. This is not a failure of the patient's reasoning — patients typically know perfectly well that the event is over — but a property of what the vehicle delivers. The representation contains no marker specifying when, and so supplies none, and the receiving system defaults to the present. §5.1 takes this consequence and runs it in the other direction.

Two features of the theory's standing should be recorded, since a coherence claim is worth no more than the mechanism it coheres with. First, this is a mainstream model rather than a marginal one: it was published twice in *Psychological Review*, it has structured a substantial treatment literature, and its central distinction is not seriously contested within the trauma field even where particular formulations are. Second, it has generated tested predictions of its own. Meyer, Smeets, Giesbrecht, Quaedflieg, Girardelli, Mackay, and Merckelbach (2013) derived a specific one and tested it: if intrusions occur because contextual representations failed to form during the adverse experience, then individual efficiency at the kind of encoding that builds them should predict individual intrusion rates. They measured that efficiency with the contextual cuing paradigm — a task assessing spatial configuration learning, believed to depend on associative encoding in the parahippocampus — in 82 participants, showed them a trauma film, and recorded intrusive experiences over the following week. Better configuration learning was associated with fewer perceptual intrusions, *r* = −.33, *p* < .01. The effect was specific: it did not extend to physiological reactivity to trauma reminders, to intrusion-related distress, or to overall symptom scores.

That result is a modest one within its own literature and does a great deal of work within this one, for a reason that has nothing to do with trauma. The variable Meyer and colleagues measured — configural spatial encoding in the parahippocampus — is, as §3.2 and §4.2 show, the same variable that Cleary's laboratory independently identified as the driver of déjà vu.

### 3.2 Déjà Vu: Settled Phenomenology, Contested Explanation

The déjà vu literature has an unusual shape. Its descriptive layer is stable, replicated across languages and instruments, and largely uncontroversial. Its explanatory layer is not settled at all. Brown's 2003 review in *Psychological Bulletin* remains the standard synthesis and is explicit about the division: he catalogues a well-characterised phenomenon and then sorts the attempts to account for it into four families, none of which commands the field. This section takes the two layers in turn, because the argument in §4 draws on the first and deliberately does not adjudicate the second.

**The descriptive layer.** Brown reports a lifetime prevalence of roughly 60 per cent, declining with age, elevated under stress and fatigue, and positively associated with socioeconomic status, education, and travel. Hadzic and Andersson (2024) conducted the first systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence literature — registered as PROSPERO CRD42023394239 and pooling 46 studies — and returned a non-ictal lifetime prevalence of 0.74, 95% CI [0.67, 0.79]. The precise figure varies with instrument and sampling; the fact it establishes is that déjà vu is a majority experience in the general population and not a clinical rarity.

The phenomenological description is consistent across sources and can be stated compactly. A person encounters a situation and is struck by a sense of familiarity with it. The familiarity is not accompanied by any retrievable episode: no prior occasion can be brought to mind, and the experiencer typically cannot say when or where the supposed earlier encounter occurred. Crucially, the sense of familiarity coexists with the simultaneous knowledge that the situation is in fact new — the experience is characteristically described as a conflict rather than as a mistaken belief, and this doubled quality is part of what makes it striking rather than merely confusing. It cannot be produced on demand. It arrives when a situation arrives. It is perceptual and scene-like rather than verbal or propositional. And it is brief, usually a matter of seconds.

Two further descriptive findings bear directly on §4 and are less widely known.

The first concerns what can produce it. Cleary and colleagues (2012), using virtual environments, demonstrated that déjà vu in healthy participants can be produced by **configural resemblance**: a novel scene whose spatial layout matches the layout of a previously viewed but unrecalled scene elicits déjà vu at an elevated rate, and the strength of the elicited experience is graded by the degree of feature match. The mechanism is Gestalt familiarity — resemblance at the level of the arrangement, not of the items arranged. This is an experimental induction result rather than a survey finding, and it is the most methodologically secure item in the descriptive layer.

Its scope belongs with it, because the numbers are narrower than the way the result is ordinarily summarised. A replication using the identical stimulus set (McNeely-White and Cleary 2023, Experiment 1) reports déjà vu on 27 per cent of retrieval-failure trials overall: 31 per cent where the test scene carried the exact spatial layout of an unrecalled study scene, and 26 per cent where it bore no relation to anything studied. The difference is reliable, *t*(69) = 3.15, *p* = .002, and it is small, *d* = 0.38. What the paradigm establishes is that configural resemblance is **sufficient** to raise the rate of déjà vu, not that it is what déjà vu consists in. Most of the déjà vu occurring inside an experiment built to elicit it occurs on trials where no configural match was installed. That residue is not mysterious and should not be treated as one: participants arrive having lived in the world, and §6.2 sets out how ordinarily a configuration is acquired without becoming retrievable.

Nor is the restriction to spatial layout a finding about déjà vu. It is a property of the instrument. A virtual tour is the apparatus that permits arrangement to be varied while content is held constant, and arrangement in a walkable environment is spatial by construction. This matters because the same laboratory has since crossed the modality. McNeely-White and Cleary (2023, Experiment 2) induced *déjà entendu* — the auditory analogue, the sense of having heard something before while knowing it is new — using tonal sequences: the notes of a familiar song in their original order, detached from their rhythm and reattached to an arbitrary one. Reports rose with exposure, from 34 per cent for sequences with no studied counterpart to 46 per cent after a single exposure and 56 per cent after three. An earlier attempt with differently constructed materials had not raised the rate (McNeely-White and Cleary 2019), which is worth recording alongside the success. The structure preserved in the 2023 materials is ordered relation over time rather than layout in space. Two things follow. Configuration is the operative term and space is one of its instances; and the induction evidence has reached a second modality, which narrows the distance between a spatial-only demonstration and the sensory representation dual representation theory specifies, which was never spatial. What has not been done is an induction of déjà vu proper by non-spatial material, and §8.5 records that.

The second concerns what it is not. Adachi and colleagues (2003), administering the Inventory of Déjà vu Experiences Assessment to 386 respondents and recovering a déjà vu rate of 76.2 per cent, subjected the responses to factor analysis. Déjà vu did not load with dissociative measures, and did not load with paranormal belief or with travel. It loaded with **precognitive dreams and with remembering dreams**, and the authors report it as "associated with good memory function." A subsequent study by the same group (2008), with 227 participants and déjà vu in 71.4 per cent, tested the dissociation question directly: in multiple regression, déjà vu dropped out of the model predicting Dissociative Experiences Scale scores, which was best predicted by age, jamais vu, depersonalization, and precognitive dreams. Neither the DES taxon nor a DES cutoff of 30 was associated with déjà vu. The finding is that déjà vu is an ordinary experience of an intact memory system, not a marker of pathology or of dissociative process.

**The explanatory layer.** Brown sorts the accounts into four families. *Dual processing* accounts hold that two normally synchronised cognitive processes become momentarily desynchronised, so that a percept is registered twice or a familiarity signal is generated separately from the encoding it should accompany. *Neurological* accounts locate the experience in aberrant medial temporal activity, and take the epilepsy evidence as their model case. *Memory* accounts attribute it to implicit familiarity: a genuine prior encounter, encoded but not recollectable, whose resemblance to the present scene produces familiarity without a source. *Attentional* accounts hold that a brief lapse of attention results in a scene being processed twice, the first processing unattended, so that the second registers as a repetition.

The reading advanced in this thesis belongs to the third family. It is a memory account, and specifically a claim about the representational format in which the implicit trace is held. That placement should be stated with its cost attached: it makes the identification **one live account among four rather than the settled position of the field**, and none of the four has been eliminated. The most direct experimental support for a rival comes from Urquhart, Sivakumaran, Macfarlane, and O'Connor (2021), whose fMRI study found that déjà vu induced under a modified false-memory procedure activated anterior cingulate, medial prefrontal, and parietal cortex — a conflict-monitoring signature rather than a memory-retrieval one — and who read this as support for the dual-processing family. §8.5 discusses the weight that study can carry.

The two layers should be kept apart when reading §4. The accounting there is conducted entirely against the descriptive layer, which is what the déjà vu literature has actually established. It does not require, and does not assume, that any of Brown's four explanatory families is correct.

### 3.3 Production Rather Than Correlation: The Stimulation Evidence

Most of what is known about the phenomenology of memory is known by correlation. A person reports an experience and something is measured alongside it, and the inference from the measurement to the mechanism has to cross the gap that all correlational evidence has to cross. Déjà vu is a partial exception, and the exception is the strongest card in the present argument. In patients undergoing intracranial monitoring for epilepsy surgery, the experience can be **produced on demand** by delivering current to specific structures. This is intervention rather than observation, and it establishes generative involvement in a way that no amount of correlated imaging can.

Bancaud, Brunet-Bourgin, Chauvel, and Halgren reported the classic series in *Brain* in 1994. Across 16 patients with temporal lobe epilepsy studied with depth electrodes, they recorded 9 spontaneous "dreamy states" in 6 patients — the collective term in that literature for déjà vu, reminiscence, and scene hallucination — against **43 evoked by electrical stimulation in 14 patients**, plus 5 further episodes induced chemically in 3. The evoked episodes were produced from the temporal neocortex on 15 occasions, the anterior hippocampus on 17, and the amygdala on 10.

The paper also reports involvement figures — anterior hippocampus 83 per cent, amygdala 73 per cent, temporal neocortex 88 per cent — and these require care, because they do not mean what their arrangement suggests. A structure is counted as involved if it was stimulated **or** if it was a site to which ictal or after-discharge activity spread. A structure that receives propagated activity is therefore counted equally with a structure whose stimulation produced the experience, and the percentages consequently measure participation in the episode rather than capacity to generate it. Read as a generative ranking they place the neocortex first, which inverts the authors' own conclusion. What Bancaud and colleagues concluded was that "the proportion of medial temporal electrodes where dreamy states could be evoked was much higher than in the neocortex." The generative primacy is medial temporal. The parent thesis reproduced the percentages without this qualifier, and the correction is recorded here rather than in a footnote because the misreading is easy to make and has propagated.

One further observation in that paper carries more weight for the present argument than the localisation does. Bancaud and colleagues report that "déjà vu and hallucinations of scenes were often evoked by different stimulations of the same electrode in the same patient." The bare familiarity signal and the delivery of remembered content are separable at a single site, in a single brain, minutes apart. Whatever produces familiarity is not simply a weak or partial version of whatever produces recollection.

Bartolomei and colleagues confirmed and localised that dissociation in *Neurology* in 2004, with a larger and more systematic series: 280 stimulations in 24 patients, using stereoelectroencephalography. Déjà vu was elicited from the entorhinal cortex more readily than from the amygdala or the hippocampus. And the dissociation resolved anatomically: **"déjà vu was associated with stimulation of the EC and reminiscence of memories with PC stimulation"** — entorhinal cortex for the familiarity signal, perirhinal cortex for retrieved content. Two adjacent rhinal structures, two different products.

Three consequences follow, and they are the whole of what this thesis takes from the stimulation literature.

First, the generator is identified and it is medial temporal, in the rhinal cortex specifically. Second, the familiarity signal is **dissociable by anatomy from remembered content**, which is precisely the structural claim dual representation theory makes about S-reps and C-reps: distinct neural bases, not a single representation in two strengths. Third, and methodologically, the experience is producible, which means the claim that these structures generate déjà vu is not an inference from correlation.

Two limits on that inheritance should be stated in the same breath. These are patients with temporal lobe epilepsy, and §2.3 recorded the decision not to treat ictal and non-ictal déjà vu as continuous; what transfers is the identification of a generator, not any claim about how the experience arises spontaneously in healthy adults. And the déjà rêve literature demonstrates how easily this evidence can be over-read: Curot and colleagues (2018) found stimulation-induced déjà rêve at a prevalence of 0.3 per mille of all electrical brain stimulations, resolved the term into three distinct phenomena, and localised the episodic-like and familiarity-like variants principally to the medial temporal lobes — while establishing that déjà rêve is a heterogeneous entity **distinct from déjà vu**. The structures overlap. The experiences do not collapse into one another.

### 3.4 Separating the Finding From the Misfire Gloss

The stimulation literature is habitually summarised in a single sentence: déjà vu is what happens when the brain misfires. The summary is not confined to popular accounts; it appears in the framing paragraphs of technical work as well, and it is worth separating from the results it purports to summarise, because it is doing something the results do not do.

What the experiments establish is a list of three items, and it was given at the end of §3.3: which structures generate the experience; that the familiarity signal is anatomically dissociable from remembered content; and that the experience is producible on demand by stimulating those structures. Every one of these is a statement about mechanism. None of them contains a verdict.

"Misfire" adds the verdict. It classifies the output as spurious — as a signal that should not have been generated, corresponding to nothing, an error of the machinery. That classification does not follow from any of the three findings, and this is easiest to see by noticing what the same evidence would look like elsewhere. Electrical stimulation of the primary visual cortex produces phosphenes; nobody concludes from this that vision is a misfire. Stimulation of the motor cortex produces movement; nobody concludes that voluntary movement is a malfunction. That a system can be driven artificially at its generator establishes that the generator is the generator. It establishes nothing whatever about the status of the signal when the system runs on its own inputs. The inference from *producible by stimulation* to *spurious in ordinary operation* is not available for any other faculty, and it is not available here either.

The gloss is also, on inspection, an inference from population rather than from mechanism. It rests on the fact that the paradigm cases were studied in patients with epilepsy, and on the reasonable observation that in those patients the episodes are seizure-associated. But the transfer from ictal to non-ictal is exactly the move §2.3 declined, and the prevalence data make the cost of it plain: with a pooled non-ictal lifetime prevalence of 0.74 [0.67, 0.79], the experience is the norm rather than the exception in people who do not have epilepsy at all. A dysfunction account has to explain why three quarters of the healthy population exhibit the dysfunction, and Adachi's finding that déjà vu is associated with *good* memory function and drops out of any model predicting dissociation makes that explanatory burden heavier rather than lighter.

None of this shows that the gloss is wrong. It may be right. There may be no referent behind the familiarity signal in the ordinary spontaneous case, and if there is not, then calling the signal an error is fair. The point is narrower and entirely procedural: **the gloss is a classificatory judgement laid over the finding, not a component of it,** and it was not established by the experiments it is habitually attached to. It belongs to a separate argument, and that argument is conducted on other grounds — grounds having to do with origin, which is the subject of §6.

The reason to insist on the separation is that the gloss, left in place, forecloses the question this thesis asks. If the experience is by definition an error, then asking what laid the representation down is asking what caused the malfunction, and the answer is given in advance. Removing the gloss does not supply a referent; §2.2 was explicit that removing an unwarranted premise reopens a question rather than answering one. It restores the question to the condition the evidence actually leaves it in, which is open.

## 4. The Accounting

### 4.1 Eleven Properties, Item by Item

The accounting proceeds as follows. Every property the déjà vu literature treats as characteristic of the experience is stated in the left column, in that literature's own terms. The middle column states what dual representation theory specifies for the sensory-bound representation on the same point, in the trauma literature's terms. The right column records where each statement comes from. Properties on which the two do not agree are not shown here; they are counted and discussed in §4.3, and the reader should hold in mind that the denominator is fourteen, not eleven.

| # | Property attributed to déjà vu | Corresponding S-rep specification | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Familiarity with no retrievable episode | Content present without a contextual index | Brown 2003; Brewin et al. 2010 |
| 2 | Sourceless — the experiencer cannot say when or where | No binding to time, place, or narrative position | Brown 2003; Brewin et al. 2010 |
| 3 | Accompanied by simultaneous knowledge that the scene is novel | The format carries no claim about its own origin; any verdict on origin is downstream of it | Urquhart et al. 2021; Brewin et al. 2010 |
| 4 | Cannot be produced voluntarily or on demand | Involuntary by definition; not addressable by deliberate search | Brown 2003; Brewin et al. 2010 |
| 5 | Arises on encountering a situation | "Automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues" | Brewin et al. 1996 |
| 6 | Perceptual and scene-like rather than verbal or propositional | "Inflexible, sensory-bound representations" | Brewin et al. 2010; Cleary et al. 2012 |
| 7 | Graded by configural resemblance between present scene and prior layout | Configural encoding in parahippocampus governs intrusion rate (*r* = −.33, *p* < .01) | Cleary et al. 2012; Meyer et al. 2013 |
| 8 | Generated from rhinal cortex, and dissociable there from remembered content | S-rep and C-rep have distinct neural bases | Bartolomei et al. 2004; Bancaud et al. 1994 |
| 9 | Not a dissociative symptom and not a marker of pathology | S-rep formation is ordinary encoding, not damage | Adachi et al. 2008; Brewin et al. 2010 |
| 10 | Unrelated to recognition-memory parameters measured with word lists | S-reps are not verbal representations, and would not be expected to appear in such parameters | O'Connor and Moulin 2013 |
| 11 | Carries a feeling of prediction that scales with the strength of the signal | No temporal index, therefore no tense the vehicle could supply | Cleary and Claxton 2018; Cleary et al. 2021 |

Several rows are unremarkable and are included for completeness rather than for force. Rows 1, 2, and 4 are the definitional core of both descriptions, and their agreement is close to trivial: any account of déjà vu will say that the familiarity has no retrievable source and cannot be summoned, and any account of the sensory-bound representation will say that it lacks an index and is not addressable by deliberate search. These rows establish that the two descriptions are about the same *kind* of thing. They do not by themselves establish that they are about the same thing, and §2.2 was explicit that schematic agreement of this sort carries no weight on its own.

Rows 3 and 6 are stronger. Row 3 concerns the doubled quality that makes déjà vu striking: the familiarity arrives alongside the perfectly intact knowledge that the situation is new, and the experience is characteristically reported as a conflict rather than as a false belief. Dual representation theory accounts for this without needing to say anything special, because on its terms the vehicle carries no claim about its own origin. An S-rep delivers content; it does not deliver a verdict on where the content came from. Any such verdict is supplied afterwards by the contextual system, and where the contextual system has no matching representation to supply, the result is exactly what is reported — a signal, and a simultaneous recognition that nothing accounts for it. Row 6 records that the déjà vu literature's insistence on the perceptual, scene-like character of the experience matches the trauma literature's formulation almost word for word: "inflexible, sensory-bound representations."

Rows 8, 9, and 10 are the ones that would most easily have failed, and each is worth stating as a risk that was run. Row 8 asks whether the two descriptions agree on neuroanatomy, which is the place where an identification of this kind normally comes apart. They do, and the agreement is not merely regional. Bartolomei and colleagues did not simply localise déjà vu to the medial temporal lobe; they showed that the bare familiarity signal and the retrieval of remembered content dissociate across two adjacent rhinal structures — entorhinal for the former, perirhinal for the latter. That is the structural claim Brewin's group makes about C-reps and S-reps, stated as an anatomical result by researchers who were not testing it. Row 9 asks whether déjà vu might be a dissociative phenomenon, which would place it in a quite different explanatory category; Adachi's regression result answers that it is not, and the identification survives a test it need not have. Row 10 records what looks at first like an adverse finding and is not: O'Connor and Moulin (2013), in a sample of 206, found no relation between déjà vu frequency and recognition-memory parameters. Their stimuli were word lists. A representational format defined as non-verbal would not be expected to show up in parameters estimated from verbal recognition, and the null is what the identification predicts rather than a strike against it. That reading is available only because the format claim was made in advance of looking at the result, which is the condition under which such a reading is legitimate.

Row 11 is different in kind from the rest and should not be read as a match of the same sort. The left column states an empirical finding — Cleary and Claxton (2018) report heightened feelings of prediction during déjà vu unaccompanied by any actual predictive ability, and Cleary and colleagues (2021) report such feelings in just over half of déjà vu states, associated with greater familiarity intensity. The right column does not state a corresponding empirical finding from the trauma literature. It states an **entailment**: a representation with no temporal index has no tense to deliver. The row therefore records a place where the identification, if granted, explains a finding that the déjà vu literature reports without explaining. That is a different and weaker kind of support than rows 5 and 7 provide, and §5.2 develops it under an explicit label as a reading rather than a result.

Rows 5 and 7 are the two that carry the argument, and they are set out separately in §4.2 because what makes them count is not the closeness of the match but the circumstances under which the match was produced.

### 4.2 Two Convergences Neither Literature Could Have Arranged

A match between two descriptions is worth something only if the descriptions were produced independently, and worth a great deal if they were produced independently *and* agree on something specific. Two of the eleven rows meet both conditions, and they are the reason this thesis exists. Neither was arranged. Neither could have been.

**Row 5: the trigger condition, stated verbatim by authors with no interest in the phenomenon.**

The single most distinctive fact about déjà vu, and the one every survey instrument records, is how it starts. It does not arise from reflection, from deliberate search, or from being asked a question. It arises on encountering a situation. A person walks into a room, or turns a corner, or sits down at a table, and the experience is delivered by the arrival of that configuration. This is why it cannot be studied by asking people to produce one — a point Brown makes and every experimentalist in the field works around — and why Cleary's laboratory had to build virtual environments in order to get at it: the only way to elicit the experience is to construct the situation and wait.

Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph, writing in 1996 about intrusive memory in post-traumatic stress, defined their second memory system as the one **"automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues."**

Set the two statements beside each other and the relationship is not that the theory's definition is *compatible with* déjà vu's trigger condition, or *could be extended to accommodate* it. The definition is a statement of the trigger condition, in different words, written for a different phenomenon. Both terms of the phrase carry: *automatically*, which is the involuntariness, and *situational cues*, which is the delivery mechanism. Brewin and colleagues were not thinking about déjà vu. There is no evidence they had ever considered it, and their paper does not mention it. They arrived at that formulation because it was what the trauma data required — because patients could not summon the material and could not stop situations from summoning it — and it is the same formulation the déjà vu literature arrived at from a completely different body of evidence.

This is what a convergence looks like when it is genuine rather than constructed. Neither party was aiming at the other. The phrase is fixed in a 1996 publication and cannot now be adjusted.

**Row 7: one parahippocampal variable, approached from opposite ends by two programmes that do not cite each other.**

The second convergence is quantitative, and it is the stronger of the two.

Cleary and colleagues (2012) demonstrated that déjà vu can be produced by **configural resemblance** — Gestalt-level similarity in the arrangement of a scene, not similarity in its contents — and that its intensity is graded by the degree of that match. Participants viewed scenes in a virtual environment and were later shown novel scenes matched to earlier ones in spatial layout. Configural spatial encoding is a parahippocampal function. Cleary's programme is a memory-psychology programme with no connection to trauma research.

Meyer, Smeets, Giesbrecht, Quaedflieg, Girardelli, Mackay, and Merckelbach (2013) were testing dual representation theory. Their hypothesis was that if intrusions arise because contextual representations fail to form, then individual differences in the capacity to build such representations should predict intrusion frequency. They operationalised that capacity with the contextual cuing paradigm, chosen precisely because spatial configuration learning is understood to depend on associative encoding in the parahippocampus, measured it in 82 participants, exposed them to a trauma film, and logged intrusive experiences for a week. Better configuration learning predicted **fewer** perceptual intrusions, *r* = −.33, *p* < .01, with the effect specific to perceptual intrusions and absent for physiological reactivity, distress, and overall symptoms.

The two results are opposite ends of a single relationship. Cleary shows that when configural resemblance is high, an uncontextualised familiarity signal is produced. Meyer shows that when configural encoding is efficient, uncontextualised perceptual intrusions are fewer. One variable, in one structure, governing both the generation of déjà vu and the rate of S-rep intrusion — measured in two laboratories, in two fields, for two unrelated purposes.

One limit is shared by both and belongs with the convergence rather than after it. Each programme operationalised configuration spatially — Cleary because a virtual tour is what permits arrangement to be varied independently of content, Meyer because the contextual cuing paradigm was chosen for its parahippocampal specificity. The convergence is therefore demonstrated in a single modality on both sides. §3.2 gives the reason to think the term generalises, the same laboratory having since produced the auditory analogue from sequential rather than spatial structure. The row as it stands is a match on the spatial case, and it should be read as one.

**And the two literatures do not cite one another.** Meyer's paper is not in Cleary's reference lists; Cleary's is not in Meyer's. Neither group was aware of standing at the other end of the same finding. This is the property §2.2 identified as the whole source of a coherence claim's evidential force: it cannot be arranged afterwards. Both papers are published and fixed. If the correspondence is real, it was real before anyone put the two side by side, and the only remaining options are coincidence or identity.

The reason to weight these two rows above the other nine is not that the matches are closer. It is that the other nine could in principle have arisen from the two literatures describing similar-seeming phenomena in similar-seeming language, since both are describing something involuntary, unindexed, and perceptual, and a certain amount of verbal overlap between such descriptions is unsurprising. Rows 5 and 7 are not verbal overlap. One is a definition written for one phenomenon that turns out to state the entry condition of another; the other is a specific neuroanatomical variable identified independently by two programmes as the governing parameter of what each was studying. Coincidence remains formally available as an explanation of both. It is not, at this level of specificity, the more economical one.

### 4.3 Three Items That Do Not Check

An accounting that reports only its successes has no denominator, and a match rate without a denominator is not a result. Three properties were considered and did not check. They are set out here at full strength, and the responses available to each are given without the pretence that any of them is a demonstration. Two of the three are open questions; one is a straightforward absence.

**First: affect.** Sensory-bound representations come to attention in the clinical literature because they are aversive. The intrusive flashback is distressing, often severely; that is why the format was theorised at all. Déjà vu is not aversive. It is characteristically neutral or mildly pleasant, sometimes interesting, occasionally unsettling in a way that stops well short of distress. Two experiences that differ this sharply in affective tone are not obviously the same kind of event.

The available response is that **aversiveness belongs to the content, not to the format.** A representation of a car crash is distressing because a car crash is distressing; a representation of a hotel lobby is not, because a hotel lobby is not. On this reading, S-reps became visible to psychology in trauma research for a reason that has nothing to do with what they are — namely, that trauma is where they hurt enough for someone to seek treatment. The everyday, affectively neutral S-rep, having no clinical consequence, does not present anywhere and so has no literature.

This response should be labelled accurately. It is an **auxiliary claim**, not something dual representation theory entails. Brewin's group have not asserted that S-reps form routinely from non-traumatic material, and the theory does not require it. The claim is testable in principle — one would look for cue-triggered, unindexed, perceptual intrusions of ordinary content in non-clinical populations, which is a study nobody has a reason to run — and it is untested. It is a coherent way of absorbing the mismatch. It is not evidence, and the mismatch is not thereby removed.

**Second: production versus binding.** Adachi and colleagues (2003) found that déjà vu loads with vivid dream recall and is associated with *good* memory function; Meyer and colleagues (2013) found that intrusions are associated with *poorer* configural binding. Taken as though they concerned one variable, these point in opposite directions, and the identification would be in trouble.

Dual representation theory holds that they are two variables. The production of sensory representations and the formation of contextual representations for them are separate processes that vary independently, and a person could in principle be a strong producer and a strong binder at once, or weak at both. On that reading Adachi's result concerns the rate at which vivid sensory material is generated and Meyer's concerns the rate at which it is contextualised, and no contradiction arises.

The reading is legitimate but it is not free, because it requires that the interaction between the two rates behave in a particular way, and **the interaction has not been tested.** Nobody has measured production and binding in the same participants and looked at how they combine to predict either déjà vu frequency or intrusion frequency. The theory permits the response; the data do not yet support it. This is the item most likely to decide the identification one way or the other, and §4.4 and §8.5 return to it.

**Third: duration.** Déjà vu is brief. The literature is consistent that episodes last seconds and terminate spontaneously. Dual representation theory says nothing about how long an S-rep intrusion persists. There is no specification to check the observation against.

This is not a mismatch and should not be counted as one, but it should not be counted as a match either. It is an **absence**: a property of the phenomenon on which the theory is silent, so that the accounting simply has no entry. It is recorded here because a set of properties chosen to be checkable would have quietly omitted it, and the omission would have improved the apparent match rate without improving the argument.

The tally, then, is eleven of fourteen. Two of the eleven are convergences of the kind §4.2 describes; one, row 11, is an entailment rather than an independent match; three fail, of which one is an absence, one is answerable only by an untested auxiliary claim, and one turns on an interaction nobody has measured. That is the result, and it is stated in that form because a coherence claim reported without its failures forfeits the property that gives it force.

### 4.4 What Has Not Been Done: The Scale Deficit

The accounting in §4.1 has a limitation that is easy to overlook because the table looks quantitative and is not. Every property in the left column was taken from the déjà vu literature's **own descriptions** of the experience — from Brown's synthesis of what surveys and case reports report, from Cleary's characterisation of what her paradigm induces, from Adachi's inventory items. None of it comes from scoring a corpus of first-person accounts against the specification directly.

This matters because of what the claim's strength depends on. §2.2 identified density as one of four conditions: a coherence claim gets its force from the number of independent points at which two descriptions agree, and from the specificity of each. The present accounting satisfies specificity but samples density at second hand. It establishes that the déjà vu literature's summary description of the experience matches the S-rep specification at eleven points. It does not establish that individual reported déjà vus, taken in bulk and scored blind, exhibit those eleven properties at the rates the summary implies. Summaries smooth. A property present in sixty per cent of accounts and a property present in ninety-five per cent both appear in a review as characteristic, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between a strong identification and a weak one.

The material for doing this properly exists and is substantial. Brown's 2003 review synthesises a survey literature reaching back decades. The Inventory of Déjà vu Experiences Assessment has been administered in more than one language and yields item-level data. Hadzic and Andersson's 2024 meta-analysis aggregates 46 studies, which means 46 sampled populations with published methods. And the great majority of published first-person descriptions do read in the way the specification requires — sourceless, situation-triggered, perceptual, brief, accompanied by intact knowledge of novelty. That impression is what makes the identification worth pursuing. It is not a measurement.

The work that would convert impression into measurement is well defined and unglamorous. It requires a coding frame stating what counts as an instance of each of the eleven properties and what counts as its absence; independent coders scoring published accounts against the frame without knowledge of the hypothesis; an inter-rater reliability statistic; and a report of the proportion of accounts satisfying each property, with the properties that fail reported alongside those that hold. **This is a reading task on already-published material rather than a new data collection**, which means it requires no participants, no ethics approval, and no instrumentation — only labour and a pre-registered frame.

It has not been done here, and the argument is weaker for it in a specific and statable way: the identification currently rests on the fit between two *characterisations*, and a characterisation is an author's compression of a corpus. Until the corpus itself is scored, the possibility remains that the fit is partly an artefact of how review authors chose to summarise, and there is no way to bound that possibility from the inside.

One further caution applies to any such exercise, and it is the reason the coding frame has to be fixed before scoring begins. §2.3 excluded attributional data from counting as origin data, and the same discipline is required here in a different form: the accounts to be scored describe experiences, and a scorer who knows the hypothesis will read ambiguous descriptions in its favour without intending to. The identification is attractive, which is precisely why the procedure has to be built to resist it.

## 5. Tenselessness and What Follows From It

### 5.1 No Index, No Tense

Everything in this section follows from a single step, and the step is analytic rather than empirical. It does not require any new observation, and it does not require the identification argued in §4 to be correct — it holds of the sensory-bound representation as dual representation theory defines it, whatever else turns out to be an instance of that format.

The step is this. A contextual index is what binds a representation to a position in time. That is not one of its incidental features; it is a substantial part of what "contextual" means in the theory. To say that a representation is contextualised is to say, among other things, that it is filed *when* — placed relative to the events around it, given a location in the narrative sequence that lets a person answer the question "when was that?" A representation defined as lacking a contextual index therefore lacks the very structure that could specify when its content belongs. **It carries no tense.** Not the wrong tense, and not an ambiguous one. It has no place to put one.

This is worth holding still for a moment, because the ordinary intuition about memory resists it. Introspectively, remembering feels like receiving something stamped *past*, as though pastness were a quality of the recalled content in the way that colour is a quality of a seen object. Dual representation theory implies that this is not so. Pastness is not carried by the content; it is supplied by the file the content sits in. Where the file is missing, the stamp is missing, and no substitute arrives with the content itself.

The clinical evidence for this is direct and was given in §3.1. The flashback's defining oddity is not that it is vivid but that it arrives in the wrong tense — the traumatic content is not experienced as something that happened but as something happening, and it is experienced that way by patients who know quite well that the event is over. Their factual knowledge is intact; what fails is the tense of the delivery. That is the missing index showing itself. Read in reverse, the flashback is a demonstration that a representation without an index does not default to the past, which is what one would expect if pastness were intrinsic to memory content. It defaults to whatever the receiving system does with untensed material, and in the flashback case the receiving system reads it as present.

The general principle is therefore that **direction is assigned downstream.** A representation arrives; the system that receives it has to file it somewhere; and where the representation supplies no temporal information, the filing is done by inference from whatever else is available — the situation the person is in, the affective tone, the content's plausibility as something remembered, the person's expectations. The resulting verdict is a property of the interpretation, not of the vehicle. It can be right, and it can be wrong, and in neither case does the vehicle bear any of the responsibility, because the vehicle never made a claim.

Two consequences follow, and they are pursued in the next two subsections. The first is that a tenseless signal can be read as pastness or as futurity with equal warrant from the signal itself, which bears directly on the most striking finding in the recent déjà vu literature (§5.2). The second is more general: if the form of the experience is fixed by the format, and the format is the same in every instance, then the form of an experience cannot distinguish one instance from another. Everything distinguishing must lie elsewhere, and there is only one place left for it to lie (§5.3).

### 5.2 The Illusion of Prediction, Read From the Format

Cleary and Claxton published a paper in *Psychological Science* in 2018 titled "Déjà Vu: An Illusion of Prediction." Using the virtual-environment paradigm described in §3.2, they found that participants in a déjà vu state reported markedly heightened feelings of being able to predict what came next — which way the path would turn, what would be around the corner — while displaying **no predictive accuracy above chance**. The asymmetry is the finding, and it is stark in both directions: the bias effects are very large, *dz* = 1.33 and 1.54, and the accuracy effect is absent. Cleary and colleagues (2021) extended it: such feelings occur in just over half of déjà vu states, and their intensity is associated with greater intensity of the familiarity signal itself.

One property of the design has to be carried forward with the result. The paths ran through environments built for the experiment, and which way a given corridor turned was settled by whoever laid the tour out; nothing in the scene bore on it. A test of whether a memory signal delivers the next turn in such an environment is a test of retrieval, and a good one. It is not a test of whether anticipation of any kind can operate on the structure of an unfolding situation, because the paradigm contains no structure of that sort to operate on. The authors record the corresponding limit from the other side: in a later study using the same materials they note being "unable to collect the dynamically unfolding mental events occurring as the participant processed a test stimulus," participants being prompted only once the tour had finished (McNeely-White and Cleary 2023). What the 2018 result establishes is what it says — that in an environment whose continuations are stipulated, the feeling of knowing the next turn is uncorrelated with the next turn. §8.5 records what is left untested, and why the untested part licenses nothing.

Read in the ordinary way, this is a debunking, and a clean one. People in a déjà vu state feel as though they know what is coming; they do not know what is coming; the feeling is an illusion. The title says so.

Read from the format, the same data describe something else, and the difference is worth setting out carefully because it changes nothing about the result and a good deal about what the result is a result *about*.

If a déjà vu is a sensory representation firing without its context, then by §5.1 the signal reaching awareness carries no temporal index and therefore no tense. It is not a past-flavoured signal that some participants mistakenly read as future-flavoured. It is **tenseless**, and it arrives in a person who must nonetheless do something with it. Two readings are available to that person, and both are interpretations laid over the same untensed material. One reads it as pastness: *I have been here before* — the classic déjà vu report. The other reads it as futurity: *I know what comes next* — the prediction feeling. Neither reading is licensed by the signal, because the signal contains nothing that could license either.

This makes an otherwise curious feature of the 2021 result expected rather than anomalous. If the prediction feeling were a distinct phenomenon riding alongside déjà vu, there would be no particular reason for its intensity to track the intensity of the familiarity signal. If both are interpretations of one signal, the association is required: a stronger signal is a stronger thing to interpret, and both interpretations of it should strengthen together. That is what Cleary and colleagues found.

It also relocates what the 2018 finding refutes. What the finding establishes is that in that environment the prediction feeling did not track the continuation. It does not demonstrate that the participants were confused about what a memory is, or that a normally reliable past-signal was being misread. On the format reading there was no past-signal to misread. Both readings are equally unfounded on the evidence of the vehicle, and the past reading is the more common one only because it is the more available — people have a category for having been somewhere before, and it fits the circumstance.

This reading should be labelled precisely, because the temptation to over-claim it is real. **It is a reading, not a result.** Cleary and Claxton did not test it and their data cannot distinguish it from the ordinary interpretation, since both accounts predict exactly what was observed. It is offered because it removes an oddity — the ordinary reading has to explain why a feeling of *prediction* should arise from a *memory* signal at all, and the format reading has no such explanatory debt — and because it follows from a step (§5.1) taken on independent grounds. That is a reason to prefer it. It is not evidence for it.

One consequence has to be stated even though it is unwelcome, because it falls squarely on the parent thesis and on the corpus that thesis analysed. If the felt quality of a tenseless signal carries no information about its direction, then **a report that an experience "felt like foreknowledge" is formally inadmissible as evidence that it was foreknowledge.** The feeling is an interpretation, and interpretations of untensed signals are unconstrained by the signal. This disqualifies, at a stroke, the great majority of what the near-death corpus offers on temporal preview, which consists precisely of experiencers reporting that they knew something was coming. Their reports remain accurate descriptions of what they experienced. They cease to be evidence about direction.

The ground of that disqualification is §5.1 and not Cleary and Claxton. The format argument entails it whatever the 2018 experiment had returned; the experiment supplies one setting in which an unfounded directional reading can be watched failing, which is an illustration and not a premise. The distinction matters practically, because a conclusion resting on a single experiment falls when that experiment's scope is questioned, and §8.5 questions its scope. The conclusion does not move.

What it does not disqualify is a narrower and much rarer class: cases in which content was **specified and recorded before** the corresponding event, so that the direction is established by the record rather than by the feeling. The parent thesis identified 435 veridical claims in its corpus, of which 40 were verified and 66 partially verified — a yield that was already the weakest part of that argument, and that the present section leaves as the only part of it still standing. §7 works out what that costs.

### 5.3 Origin as the Only Remaining Variable

The argument to this point has removed a great deal from the table, and it is worth stating plainly what is left, because the reduction is the thesis's principal structural result and it cuts in a direction that is not obviously favourable to the framework advancing it.

If the identification holds, then the *form* of the experience is fixed by the format and is therefore constant across every instance. Sourcelessness is constant: no instance carries a retrievable origin, because no S-rep does. Situational triggering is constant. Perceptual character is constant. Involuntariness is constant. Tenselessness, and with it the availability of both a past reading and a future reading, is constant. Brevity, whatever its explanation, is constant. None of these properties can distinguish one instance from another, because none of them varies.

The consequence is that **every property by which one might hope to tell a significant instance from an ordinary one has been absorbed into the format.** The vividness cannot do it; §2.4 established that specification and voluntary access are separate axes and that high specification is the norm for this format, not a mark of distinction. The uncanniness cannot do it; the sense of alreadyness is what an unindexed familiarity signal feels like from the inside, in every instance. The conviction cannot do it; §5.2 established that the felt direction is an interpretation, and interpretations of a tenseless signal are unconstrained. The subsequent narratability cannot do it, since contextualisation is a process that can act on a representation after formation.

Exactly one variable survives: **what laid the representation down.**

This is the whole of what separates a déjà vu produced by an unretrieved hotel lobby from a déjà vu produced by anything else. Two experiences identical in form, identical in feel, identical in every reportable property, may differ entirely in origin — and nothing in the experience itself will indicate which is which. The format is silent on its own provenance. That silence is not a limitation of introspection or a defect in self-report; it is a structural property of a representation that carries no contextual index, since an index is exactly what would say where the content came from.

Two things follow, one methodological and one that bears on the framework.

The methodological consequence is that **any argument from the character of an experience to its origin is invalid on this account, including arguments the present framework would like to make.** A person who reports that a preview was unlike an ordinary déjà vu — more vivid, more insistent, more certain — is reporting on signal strength and on their own interpretation, and §5.2 showed that both interpretations scale with signal strength together. The report cannot discriminate. This closes off the most natural line of defence available to any claim about extraordinary content, and it closes it off from the inside rather than by external objection.

The consequence for the framework is that the question it cares about has become both narrower and harder. Narrower, because the form is no longer at issue: there is nothing anomalous to explain in *how* preview material behaves, since it behaves the way this format always behaves, and the parent thesis's two formal signatures cease to be evidence of anything unusual (§7.1). Harder, because the remaining question — what laid it down — is the one question the experience cannot answer about itself, and must therefore be answered from outside it.

Section 6 sets out the candidates. Three are live, all of them entirely mundane, and two things should be said before they are examined. Nothing in the present argument favours any of them; the reduction performed here is not a clearing of the ground in preparation for a preferred conclusion, but a demonstration that the ground was never as populated as it appeared. And the three are not a closed field. They are the routes for which a mechanism has been published and tested, which is a fact about what has been studied rather than about what is possible. A demonstrated route establishes that a route exists; it establishes nothing about whether it is the only one. Section 6 is therefore subordinate to the argument rather than part of its foundation — §§4, 5, and 7 stand whichever route operated, and stand equally if none of the three did. Section 6.4 sets out why the framework's own account of boundary states is not a fourth entry in that list.

## 6. Candidate Origins

### 6.1 The Dream Route

The first candidate is that the representation was laid down during sleep. It has the most survey support of the three, the most direct physiological rationale, and the most treacherous evidence base, and those three things should be kept apart.

**The survey finding.** Adachi and colleagues (2003) factor-analysed responses from 386 participants and found that déjà vu loaded with precognitive dreams and with remembering dreams, and not with dissociation, paranormal belief, or travel. The 2008 study reproduced the association from the other direction: in a regression predicting Dissociative Experiences Scale scores, precognitive dreams survived as a predictor while déjà vu dropped out. Whatever else is true, the variable that travels with déjà vu across two samples and two analyses is dream-related.

**The physiological rationale**, which is the stronger part of the case, does not depend on surveys at all. Braun and colleagues (1997), using positron emission tomography across the sleep-wake cycle, reported that during REM sleep the post-rolandic sensory cortices are selectively activated while frontoparietal association cortices remain depressed, and that limbic and paralimbic structures — including the **mesial temporal** regions — are reactivated. Set beside the specification in §3.1, this describes a state in which perceptual representation runs at full strength, the machinery that binds perceptual content to time, place, and narrative sequence runs at reduced strength, and the structures identified in §3.3 as the generators of the familiarity signal are active.

The qualifier in that sentence is load-bearing, and the stronger version has to be refused explicitly because it is the version the physiology invites. **Contextual binding during REM is impaired, not absent.** If dream content were laid down purely as sensory representations, no dream would ever be narratable — and dreams are narrated every morning, in sequence, with places and persons and a reported order of events. Whatever survives frontoparietal depression is evidently sufficient to contextualise a substantial part of what is generated. The same correction applies to the trauma case, where the hormonal disruption of contextual encoding is a degradation rather than an abolition, which is why patients retain some narrative of the event and not none. What the physiology supports is a gradient, and any reading of it as a switch — here or anywhere in what follows — is an over-claim.

The asymmetry is the part that carries the route, and it survives the correction intact. The two systems do not degrade together. Sensory representation is not reported to fail under either condition; contextualisation is what fails, and it fails while capture continues — which is why material from both conditions remains reachable by cue-driven routes that do not run through voluntary retrieval. One system is compromised and the other is not. That is a weaker claim than the physiology is often made to carry and a better-supported one, and it is all the route requires: not that sleep manufactures sensory representations exclusively, but that it is a condition in which the proportion laid down without usable context is elevated.

Even so stated, the inference is a reading of the imaging rather than a result reported by its authors, and it is stated as such. A neurochemical layer is often added to it — the suppression of noradrenergic activity in REM, and the cholinergic profile associated with encoding failure — which would strengthen the case considerably if it holds. **That layer was not verified at first hand for this thesis** and is therefore not relied upon; it is flagged in §8.5 as an outstanding verification.

**The treacherous part is the survey evidence, and it has to be discounted heavily.** Section 2.3 excluded attributional data from counting as origin data, and the exclusion bites hardest here. When a respondent reports that a déjà vu corresponded to a remembered dream, that is a record of what the respondent concluded, not of what laid the representation down — and the conclusion is precisely the sort a person reaches when confronted with a sourceless familiarity signal and searching for something to attribute it to. Curot and colleagues (2018) make the problem concrete by showing that déjà rêve, the attribution of a present familiarity to a prior dream, is a heterogeneous entity distinct from déjà vu, resolvable into three separate phenomena. A substantial portion of the published dream–déjà vu correlation is therefore a correlation with the *attribution*, which cannot be substituted for the variable of interest. Brown's 2004 monograph is often cited for figures of roughly a fifth of déjà vu experiences attributed to a prior dream and about two fifths to dreams and waking life together; those figures were not verified at source for this thesis, and in any case they are attributional and subject to the same discount.

One further finding bears on the route and is easy to misread in the opposite direction. Pace-Schott and colleagues (2001), studying 14 participants across seven days of baseline, nineteen days on an SSRI, and five days of discontinuation, found that dream **recall frequency decreased** while subjective **intensity increased**, with report length and bizarreness rising during discontinuation. Recall and production are dissociable: a person can be generating vivid dream content and reporting less of it. This matters because it removes an objection — that a dream route would predict déjà vu to track dream recall, and that people with poor recall should have less — by showing that recall is a poor proxy for what was generated. It cuts the other way too, and honestly: it means the surveys measuring recall are not measuring the variable the route requires.

The dream route is therefore well motivated physiologically, poorly evidenced empirically, and currently indistinguishable from the alternatives on any measurement anyone has taken.

### 6.2 Unretrieved Waking Exposure

The second candidate is the one the memory literature already favours, and it is the strongest of the three on present evidence. On this account the representation was laid down during ordinary waking life, from a real prior encounter, and simply failed to be contextualised — or was contextualised and the context was subsequently lost. Later, a scene resembling the original in spatial configuration arrives, addresses the representation through the situational-cue route, and produces familiarity with no accompanying source, because the source was never available for retrieval.

This is Cleary's account, and it has something none of the others has: **it has been demonstrated experimentally.** The 2012 virtual-environment studies do not infer this mechanism from correlations; they build it. Participants view scenes, the scenes are not subsequently recallable, and novel scenes matched in configuration to the earlier ones elicit déjà vu at rates graded by the degree of configural match. The laboratory produces the experience on demand from a known and recorded source. In evidential terms this is the same class of result as the stimulation work in §3.3 — production rather than correlation — and it is the reason any competing account of origin starts at a disadvantage. What has been demonstrated is that the route works, not that it is the route most déjà vu takes; §3.2 gives both the size of the induced effect and the size of what the induction leaves unaccounted for. The advantage is an advantage in mechanism rather than in established prevalence, and the last paragraph of this subsection is why that distinction does not much help the rival candidates.

It should be noted explicitly that this candidate is not a rival to the identification argued in §4. It is a rival about **origin only**, and it is fully compatible with — indeed it exemplifies — the format claim. What Cleary's paradigm produces is precisely a sensory representation, laid down without a usable context, retrieved by configural situational cue, delivering familiarity without a source. If one wanted a controlled demonstration that S-reps can be formed from affectively neutral, non-traumatic material and later fire on cue, this is it, and §4.3's auxiliary claim about affect gains some support from that fact even though nobody set out to provide it.

The route also has scope that is easily underestimated. The material need not have been consciously attended. A configuration seen peripherally, in a photograph, in a film, through a car window, or in a room laid out like another room years earlier, is enough — the paradigm's own manipulation is layout resemblance, not identity, and the resemblance need only be configural. Given the number of spatial configurations a person passes through in a life, the supply of unretrieved candidate sources is effectively unbounded. This is why the route is difficult to rule out in any individual case: for almost any déjà vu, some prior exposure could in principle have been the source, and the fact that none can be recovered is exactly what the account predicts.

That last point is also the route's principal weakness as a scientific proposal, and it should be stated rather than passed over. An explanation that predicts its own unfalsifiability in the individual case is doing less work than it appears to. What rescues it from vacuity is the laboratory result: the mechanism is not merely possible, it has been shown to operate, and an account that has been shown to operate is entitled to be the default for cases where nothing distinguishes them. It is not entitled to be the default for cases where something does, and §6.5 sets out what that something would have to be.

One feature of this candidate is decisive for the discrimination problem and is developed there. On the unretrieved-exposure account, **the source is recoverable in principle.** It existed, in the world, at a time and place. It could in principle be found — by a witness, by a photograph, by a record of where the person was. That the source has not been recovered is a contingent fact about the difficulty of the search, not a structural fact about the account. The same holds, with different search costs, of the other two: a dream occurred on a night, and an assembly event was built from documented anticipations. That all three posit a locatable source is what makes any test of them a test of records rather than of experiences.

### 6.3 Constructive Simulation

The third candidate is the one most likely to be overlooked, and it is the one that most directly threatens the framework's preferred reading, which is a reason to state it at full strength rather than in passing.

On this account the representation was laid down from a scene that **was never perceived at all**, because it was constructed. Wamsley (2022) collected 469 dreams from 48 participants and traced their content to waking sources. The headline figures are that 53.5 per cent of dreams contained material traceable to a past episodic source and 43.9 per cent drew on multiple waking sources — but the figure that matters here is the third: **25.7 per cent contained material traceable to an anticipated *future* event.** Not to something that had happened, but to something the dreamer expected, was preparing for, or was concerned about. Wamsley reads this as evidence that dreaming is in substantial part constructive episodic simulation — the assembly of scenes that have not occurred, out of elements that have.

Follow that through the machinery already established and the consequence is immediate. A constructed scene is perceptual content. Generated during REM, under the conditions Braun and colleagues describe, it is generated by the same sensory cortices with the same contextualising machinery offline. It is therefore laid down as a sensory-bound representation exactly as a perceived scene would be, and the representation carries no marker distinguishing it from one — since §5.1 established that the format carries no information about its own origin. Later, an actual situation resembling the simulated one in configuration arrives, addresses the representation through the situational-cue route, and produces recognition.

The result is a person encountering a genuinely novel event and recognising it, on the basis of a scene that was never perceived and never existed until the moment it was assembled. **No anomalous access to the future is required, and no coincidence is required either**, because the simulation was built out of the person's own anticipations — it resembles what actually happened for the same reason that a well-informed expectation resembles what actually happens.

This candidate deserves particular weight for three reasons. It is empirically grounded, in a published quantitative study with a stated method rather than in a theoretical possibility. It requires nothing that is not already established elsewhere in this thesis — Wamsley's simulation finding, Braun's REM physiology, Cleary's configural cue mechanism, and Brewin's format, each independently supported. And it produces, without strain, the exact phenomenology that motivates preview claims in the first place: a scene that is recognised on arrival, that could not have been reported beforehand, and whose source cannot be located because it was never in the world.

It should also be noticed how well this candidate accounts for a finding that the dream route (§6.1) can only partly absorb. Adachi's factor analysis placed déjà vu with *precognitive dreams* specifically, alongside dream recall. On the constructive-simulation account that association is not a puzzle and not evidence of foresight: dreams containing anticipated future material are common, they will sometimes resemble what follows, and a person who notices the resemblance will report a precognitive dream. The same population variable — how much constructive dream content a person generates and retains — would drive both the déjà vu rate and the precognitive-dream rate, producing exactly the loading Adachi observed.

Nothing in this thesis counts against this candidate, and nothing in the framework competes with it. Any argument that would set it aside in a particular case has to be an argument about records rather than about experiences, which is the subject of §6.5.

### 6.4 Why the Framework's Claim Is Not a Fourth Candidate

There is an obvious fourth entry to add to the three, and it should not be added. The candidate would be that in some cases the representation was laid down during a boundary state — the near-death episode itself, or a comparable condition — and that its content originates neither in prior perception nor in constructive assembly. It is withheld for a reason that has nothing to do with how it would score if it were entered. Nothing in this subsection, and nothing anywhere in §6, is a finding against it.

Sections 6.1 to 6.3 answer one question: **which physical process laid this representation down?** Every entry names a process — a night's REM cycle, an unattended glimpse, an act of constructive assembly — and the question is which was operating. The framework's claim is not a rival answer, because it is not a claim about processes. What the parent thesis argues is that what is received in a boundary state is better described as **state-perception** than as foreknowledge: not a preview of events in sequence but the apprehension of a condition, met later as the events themselves. That says nothing about which encoding event occurred, when, or in which structure, and it neither predicts nor forbids any answer to the question §6 asks. A claim of that kind cannot be entered in this column because it is not the kind of thing the column holds. The framework also has its own reason for expecting the physical account to be complete; it is stated in the framework's own terms in §8.3, and nothing in this subsection depends on granting it.

Three things follow, and two of them are costly to the framework.

The first is that the framework has no entry in the origin column and gains nothing from an absence in it. If prospective work establishes that constructive simulation accounts for most or all of ordinary déjà vu (§6.5), that is not a defeat, because there is nothing there to defeat. Equally, if a residue remains after every searchable source has been eliminated, that residue is not an opening. A column the framework does not occupy cannot be won by the difficulties of its occupants.

The second is that **this forbids an entire class of argument, including arguments the framework would like to make.** Any inference of the form *the physical account cannot reach X, therefore something else must* is unavailable — permanently, by construction, rather than pending better evidence. It is an argument from ignorance, and it does not improve by being made about consciousness rather than about anything else. An account with a hole in it is not better evidence of something beyond it. It is a worse account.

The third is that the inverse is unavailable on the same grounds, and because that half costs the framework nothing it is the half most likely to go unsaid. *The physical account reaches X, therefore there is nothing else* has the identical form. An account that is complete at the level it operates on settles nothing about whether that level is exhaustive: a full description of a machine in terms of voltages is a full description, and says nothing at all about what the machine is computing. Completeness at one level of description is compatible with a great many positions about others, which is exactly why it discriminates among none of them. This thesis produces a good deal of material inviting that second inference, §6.3 in particular. It licenses it no more than it licenses the first, and §8.4 names the error in both directions.

Taken together, these mean that no result reported in this thesis, and none that could be designed on its model, bears on the framework's claim in either direction. That is a real limit on what the present work can do for the framework, and it is not to be softened by pointing at the residue in §6.5.

What the identification does supply should be stated precisely, because it is smaller than it looks and is easy to inflate. It removes an anomaly. The parent thesis treats preview material's non-retrievability and its recognition-on-arrival as puzzling behaviour requiring a special mechanism; the format supplies both without one (§7.1). That is the subtraction of something the framework had to explain, not the addition of something it can claim. It applies equally to every origin in this column and discriminates nothing.

Whether the *form* of the physical account is itself informative — whether a representational system that holds content without a place in sequence and delivers it when the situation arrives is a shape worth noticing — is a separate question, and not an origin question. Section 8.3 takes it up, marked as a reading and licensing nothing empirical.

### 6.5 What Would Discriminate Among Them

**Nothing currently decides among the three candidates.** No measurement anyone has taken distinguishes them, and none of the arguments in §§6.1–6.3 was offered as a decision. This should be said in exactly those words, because a survey of candidates ending in a preference the evidence does not support would be the failure this thesis is most exposed to. A second thing has to be said in the same breath, because the first sentence invites the assumption that the answer is one of the three. **Nothing establishes that it is.** The three are the routes with a published mechanism behind them, and a survey of what has been demonstrated is not a census of what is possible. What follows concerns discrimination among the three; it does not concern, and cannot settle, whether the source of a given instance lies among them.

The reason the question is hard is structural rather than practical. Section 5.3 established that the format is silent on its own provenance: the experience carries no marker of where its content came from, and every reportable property is constant across origins. It follows that **no report can discriminate**, however detailed or however sincere. Asking experiencers is not merely unreliable here; it is asking a question the vehicle cannot answer.

Discrimination therefore has to come from outside the experience, and what makes an outside test possible is a feature the three candidates share:

**Each posits a source that existed in the world.**

Unretrieved waking exposure posits a real prior encounter, which had a time and a place and could in principle be located by a witness, a photograph, or a record of the person's movements. The dream route posits a real dream, which occurred on a night, and which — if reported, recorded, or observed under laboratory conditions — leaves a trace. Constructive simulation posits a real assembly event, built from the person's own anticipations, whose ingredients are in principle traceable, which is precisely what Wamsley's method does. In all three the failure to recover a source is contingent: a fact about the difficulty of searching, not about the account. Each is therefore answerable to records in a way that none of them is answerable to testimony.

That yields one workable test, and it is not a test of the experience.

If content is specified in a durable, timestamped form before the corresponding event occurs, and the specification is detailed enough that the match cannot be accounted for by expectation, then all three candidates are constrained in a way they are not otherwise. Unretrieved exposure has to produce a prior encounter that contained the specified detail. The dream route has to produce a dream that did. Constructive simulation has to account for detail exceeding what the person's anticipations could supply — which is the crux, since §6.3's whole strength is that anticipation-built scenes resemble outcomes for ordinary reasons. None of the three is refuted by a single case, and none would be; but all become answerable to something, which they are not when the only evidence is a report given afterwards.

This sets the requirements for evidence of any weight, and they are demanding. The record must **precede** the event, and its precedence must be established by something better than the recorder's memory of when it was written. It must be **specific**, since general content is satisfied by too many outcomes. It must be **independent of expectation**, which rules out most content concerning a person's own foreseeable circumstances. And it must be **collected without knowing the outcome**, since a corpus assembled after the fact selects for hits by construction. The parent thesis's yield of 40 verified and 66 partially verified claims from 435 was drawn from retrospective accounts and fails the last requirement structurally, whatever the merits of individual cases within it.

What such a test cannot produce should be stated alongside what it can. A well-run series can eliminate searchable sources and leave a residue: cases in which specification preceded event, expectation was excluded, and no prior source was found. That residue is a description of what a search did not find. It is not a fourth candidate arriving by elimination, and §6.4 sets out why there is no fourth candidate for it to arrive as. Its value is that it would bound how much of the phenomenon the three routes account for — which is worth knowing on its own terms, and worth knowing whichever way it comes out.

Three subsidiary lines of work would also help, none of them decisive.

The first is the **production–binding interaction** left open in §4.3: measuring sensory-representation production and contextual binding in the same participants, and observing how they combine to predict déjà vu frequency. This does not discriminate among origins, but it is the outstanding test of the identification on which the whole framing rests, and it should be settled before more weight is placed on the framing.

The second is **prospective dream collection with configural matching**. Wamsley's method traces dream content to waking sources; run forward, with dreams recorded on the night and configural resemblance to subsequently encountered scenes assessed blind, it would establish how much ordinary déjà vu the dream and simulation routes actually account for. It is the single most informative study available now, and it should be run whether or not its result is convenient.

The third is the **scale scoring** described in §4.4, which bears on the identification rather than on origin but conditions everything built on it.

The honest summary is that all three candidates are unfalsified and variously supported, that nothing decides among them, that nothing establishes the field to be closed at three, and that the only route to deciding anything here runs through prospectively recorded specification rather than through anything an experiencer can report. §7.3 states why even that route has a limit.

## 7. Consequence for the Parent Thesis

### 7.1 Signatures 3 and 5 Cease to Be Special

The parent thesis identified five structural signatures in reports of temporal preview and argued that each is incompatible with the preview being a recording of a fixed future. Three of the five concern content: the material does not arrive in chronological order, it arrives with explicit conditionality, and it is selected by function rather than by sequence. Those three are untouched by anything in this thesis, and nothing below bears on them.

The remaining two concern **form**, and they are the ones this thesis dissolves.

Signature 3 is **non-retrievability**: the received content cannot be recalled deliberately before the corresponding moment arrives. The parent thesis treated this as the strongest single structural item in its corpus, on the ground that there is no version of a storage-and-playback model that produces a stored record its owner cannot consult. That reasoning is sound against a storage model. It is not sound against the format described in §3.1, in which a representation lacking a contextual index is by definition not addressable by deliberate search. Non-retrievability is not an anomaly requiring explanation; it is the defining property of a well-attested representational form, exhibited by every instance of it, in trauma patients and in people encountering a hotel lobby.

Signature 5 is **recognition rather than recall**: when the moment arrives it is met with a sense of having already lived it, not with the retrieval of a stored preview. The parent thesis called this the alreadyness debt and set it as a requirement any rival must discharge — an account of why matching a stored record should produce alreadyness rather than recognition of a record. The debt is discharged by the format without further assumption. An unindexed familiarity signal, delivered by situational cue, produces exactly alreadyness; that is what déjà vu is, and roughly three quarters of the population have had one.

The consequence is unambiguous and unfavourable to the parent argument as it was stated. **Two of its five signatures are not evidence of anything unusual.** They are evidence that the material is carried in a particular representational format — a format that is ordinary, common, experimentally producible, and entirely compatible with every mundane origin in §6. A reader who accepts §§3–5 should reduce the parent thesis's evidential claim accordingly, and the reduction is not marginal: signature 3 was named as its strongest structural item.

Two clarifications keep this from over-correcting.

First, dissolving a signature is not refuting a thesis. The parent thesis's central positive claim is that what is received is a state rather than a coordinate sequence, and the three content signatures — non-chronological arrival, conditionality, functional selection — are what support it. Those are claims about what the material *is*, and they survive intact. What has been removed is a pair of claims about how the material *behaves*, which turn out to be claims about the vehicle rather than about the cargo.

Second, the same move that removes the two signatures also removes an objection the parent thesis had to work to answer. If preview material is carried as a sensory-bound representation, the framework no longer needs a special mechanism for it. It needs no explanation of why a stored future cannot be consulted, and none of why arrival produces alreadyness, because both are supplied by a mechanism established elsewhere for other reasons. The parent thesis paid for those properties in special pleading; the identification pays for them out of the memory sciences. That is a real gain, and it is the second half of a trade whose first half was the loss of the two signatures. Whether the trade is favourable depends on what one wanted the thesis to do, and §7.2 takes that up.

One caution recorded in §2.4 applies here and cuts against the parent thesis in a further place. That thesis, in treating certain accounts as more evidentially weighty because they were coherent, sequential, and stably narratable, was sorting on a variable that does not indicate what it was taken to indicate. Present narratability establishes that a contextual representation exists now. It says nothing about whether one existed at formation, because contextualisation is a process that acts on sensory representations after the fact — and a person who writes an account out in sequence shortly after waking has performed that process by hand. Cases sorted this way were sorted on the wrong axis.

### 7.2 The Format Arm and the Corpus Arm

The parent thesis has two arms, and the present analysis changes their relative weight substantially. Since the change is not the one the framework would have chosen, it is worth setting out in a form that does not disguise it.

**The corpus arm** is the near-death dataset: 6,753 structured accounts, from which the thesis drew a prevalence of 15.3 per cent for knowledge of one's personal future, 3.8 per cent for world events, 1.3 per cent for a full life preview, 6.4 per cent making a verifiable claim, and 9.4 per cent reporting a precognitive aftereffect. It reported an extraction validated at κ = 0.672 with 100 per cent sensitivity, a co-occurrence between preview and mission-type return of 50.9 per cent against 16.7 per cent (χ² = 598.2, φ = 0.298), a déjà vu aftereffect at 20.6 per cent against 7.4 per cent (χ² = 179.3), a null result on the clinical-versus-non-clinical comparison (9.7 per cent against 13.5 per cent, χ² = 2.08, *p* = 0.150), and an age gradient running from 21.0 per cent in the youngest band to 2.6 per cent in the oldest. Of 435 veridical claims, 40 were verified and 66 partially verified.

**The format arm** is the identification: that preview material behaves as it does because it is carried in a known representational form, and that the form is the same one déjà vu instantiates.

The parent thesis leaned on the corpus arm and treated the format arm as a supporting step near its end. The present analysis inverts that, on two grounds.

The first is that **the corpus arm is the weaker of the two, and §5.2 removed most of what remained of it.** The great majority of the corpus's testimony about temporal preview consists of experiencers reporting that they knew something was coming — that the experience carried a sense of foreknowledge. Sections 5.1 and 5.2 established that a report of felt direction is formally inadmissible as evidence of actual direction, because the vehicle carries no tense and the directional reading strengthens with the strength of the signal rather than with anything about direction. This is not a complaint about the honesty or accuracy of the reports. They are accurate reports of what the experiencers experienced. They simply cannot bear on the question of what produced it. What survives is the recorded-specification subset — 40 verified and 66 partially verified claims out of 435 — and §6.5 noted that even this was collected retrospectively and therefore selects for hits by construction.

The second is that **the format arm engages a mechanism established by direct experimental production**, in two independent ways: the experience can be evoked by electrical stimulation of identified structures (§3.3), and it can be induced in healthy participants from a controlled source (§6.2). Nothing in the corpus arm has that standing, and nothing in a retrospective self-report corpus ever could.

The resulting structure of the combined argument is therefore this. The strongest thing the framework has on this subject is a **coherence claim about format**, resting on eleven of fourteen properties matched, two of them convergences that could not have been arranged (§4.2). The weakest thing it has is the **veridical corpus**, which fails at its own stated requirement and which the format analysis further undermines. And an origin claim — that some instances of the format were laid down in a boundary state — is not available to it at all, being a claim of the wrong kind (§6.4). That last point is not a concession extracted by the memory literature. It follows from the framework's own account of how degrees relate, applied consistently rather than selectively.

It should also be recorded that the direction of this correction is the framework's own methodological commitment operating on its own material. The parent thesis argued that a hypothesis is worth following when it organises data better than the alternatives, and the present analysis is what that standard produces when applied honestly: the parts of the parent argument that organised data have been retained, the parts that rested on the felt quality of testimony have been withdrawn, and the withdrawal was not optional once §5.1 was granted. A framework that reports only the corrections that favour it is not following data.

### 7.3 What Prospective Registration Can and Cannot Reach

Prospective registration is the one methodological reform that would improve the veridical literature, and §6.5 set out its requirements: content specified in durable timestamped form before the event, specific enough to exclude expectation, and collected without knowledge of outcome. It is worth doing. It should be said clearly, however, what it can and cannot decide, because the two are easily conflated and the conflation would produce a test that appears to settle the question and does not.

Prospective registration tests **coordinate access**. Its logic is that a person specified detail *d* at time *t*₁, event *E* containing *d* occurred at *t*₂, and no source available before *t*₁ contained *d*. A positive finding constrains the mundane origins in a definite way: unretrieved waking exposure must produce a prior encounter containing *d*, and constructive simulation must account for detail exceeding what the person's anticipations could supply. A negative finding across a well-designed series would be genuinely damaging to any claim of anomalous access.

What it cannot reach is the claim this thesis makes. The identification in §4 is a claim about **format** — that a déjà vu and an S-rep firing without its C-rep are one event under two descriptions. That claim is settled or unsettled by the evidence in §§3–4: the anatomical dissociation, the configural convergence, the trigger condition, the production–binding interaction that §4.3 left open, and the corpus scoring §4.4 called for. A registration study bears on none of these. Whether registered specifications succeed or fail, the format claim stands exactly where it stood, because the format claim is silent on origin and registration studies address nothing else.

The independence runs in both directions, and the second direction is the one that matters for how this thesis should be used. A framework that took a positive registration result as confirmation of the identification would be confirming a claim the study did not test. And a framework that treated a negative result as leaving the identification unharmed while treating a positive result as supporting it would be helping itself to an asymmetry it has not earned. **Neither outcome touches it.** The correct posture is to say so in advance, which is what this subsection is for.

There is a further limit, and it follows directly from §5.3 rather than from any practical difficulty. Suppose the strongest possible result: a registered specification, verified, with no available prior source. What has been established is that the content did not come from anywhere the search could reach. What has not been established is where it did come from, and no refinement of the method will supply that, because the experience carries no marker of its provenance and the record only certifies timing. A registration study can therefore **exclude** origins; it cannot **identify** one. Positive results narrow the field to whatever remains after the searchable sources are eliminated, and what remains after that is a residue defined by absence rather than a candidate established by evidence.

This is a real constraint on how much any such programme could ever deliver, and it should temper the expectations attached to it. The most a well-run registration series can produce is a well-characterised residue — cases in which specification preceded event, expectation was excluded, and no prior source was found. That is worth having, and it is what §6.5 recommends pursuing. It demonstrates nothing the framework claims, since the framework has no entry in the origin column for a residue to occupy (§6.4), and it would remain a residue however large the series and however clean the individual cases. Section 8.4 records why the temptation to read a residue as a positive finding is the specific hazard this framework has to guard against, and why the guarding has to be done before the results are in rather than afterwards.

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 What the Déjà Vu Literature Contributes

Taken on its own, the déjà vu literature contributes three things to the present argument, and they are of quite different evidential weight. Distinguishing them matters, because the field's own summaries tend to run them together and the strongest of the three is the one least often foregrounded.

**A stable descriptive layer.** The phenomenology has been recorded consistently across decades, instruments, and languages, and is now supported by a registered meta-analysis: a non-ictal lifetime prevalence of 0.74, 95% CI [0.67, 0.79], pooled across 46 studies (Hadzic and Andersson 2024). This is what supplies the left-hand column of §4.1, and its reliability is what makes an itemised comparison possible at all. Its limitation was stated in §4.4: it is a characterisation of a corpus rather than a scoring of one, and characterisations compress.

**A generator, established by production.** The stimulation studies are the field's strongest evidential asset and are not principally about déjà vu at all — they are about what happens when current is delivered to the rhinal cortex. Bancaud and colleagues evoked 43 dreamy states in 14 patients, against 9 spontaneous episodes in 6, and concluded that medial temporal electrodes evoked them at a far higher rate than neocortical ones. Bartolomei and colleagues, across 280 stimulations in 24 patients, localised the familiarity signal to the entorhinal cortex and remembered content to the perirhinal. This is intervention, and it establishes generative involvement in a way correlational imaging cannot. Its transfer to the healthy population is limited by exactly the consideration §2.3 imposed, and what transfers is the identification of a generator rather than any claim about spontaneous occurrence.

**A dissociation that was not sought and that the identification requires.** This is the contribution that carries the most weight and receives the least attention in the field's own framing. Bancaud reported that déjà vu and scene hallucination were "often evoked by different stimulations of the same electrode in the same patient"; Bartolomei resolved that dissociation anatomically across two adjacent rhinal structures. Neither group was testing dual representation theory or had any reason to. What they demonstrated is that a bare familiarity signal and the delivery of remembered content have distinct neural substrates — which is the structural claim Brewin's group makes about S-reps and C-reps, arrived at from stimulation data by researchers working on epilepsy.

Alongside these, the field contributes something the identification has to absorb rather than benefit from: a genuinely contested explanatory layer, in which the reading advanced here is one of four families and not the leading one. Section 3.2 recorded that placement, and §8.5 records what the principal rival costs.

Two of the field's findings are worth restating as contributions precisely because they look at first like obstacles. Adachi's demonstration that déjà vu is not a dissociative phenomenon and is associated with good memory function removes the experience from the pathology category, which the identification requires — S-rep formation on this account is ordinary encoding, not damage. And O'Connor and Moulin's null result across 206 participants, showing no relation between déjà vu and recognition-memory parameters, is what a non-verbal representational format predicts when the parameters are estimated from word lists. Both are cases where a finding read as adverse within the field is what the identification expects, and in both the reading is legitimate only because the format claim specifies non-verbal, non-pathological encoding in advance of the result rather than after it.

### 8.2 What Dual Representation Theory Contributes

Dual representation theory contributes the specification — the right-hand column of §4.1 — and its value lies almost entirely in the circumstances under which that specification was written.

**A format defined before the comparison, for another purpose.** Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph in 1996, and Brewin, Gregory, Lipton, and Burgess in 2010, wrote down a set of properties for a representational form: sensory-bound rather than abstract, inflexible rather than re-describable, cue-triggered rather than voluntarily retrievable, without contextual index, with a neural basis distinct from that of contextualised representation. They wrote them down to account for intrusive imagery in post-traumatic stress. Not one of the properties was chosen with déjà vu in view, and the specification is fixed in two published papers that cannot now be adjusted. This is what makes the comparison in §4 a test rather than an exercise in redescription: the second column was written first, by people with no stake in the outcome, and the first column had to meet it or fail.

**A mechanism with independent standing.** The theory is mainstream within its own field, published twice in *Psychological Review*, and structurally uncontested even where particular formulations are debated. It has a treatment literature built on it. It has generated at least one tested prediction of its own — Meyer and colleagues' derivation and confirmation that configural encoding efficiency predicts perceptual intrusion rate. The identification is therefore not a coupling of one poorly evidenced phenomenon to another; it couples a common experience to a model that carries its own empirical weight. Whether that weight transfers is the question §2.2 answered in the negative: it does not. Coherence with a well-supported mechanism is not inheritance of its support. But coherence with a *well-supported* mechanism is worth more than coherence with a speculative one, because the specification being matched has itself been tested against data of its own.

**The two consequences that do the analytic work.** Beyond supplying the specification, the theory supplies two things the argument could not have derived elsewhere. The first is the missing-index account of the flashback's tense, which §5.1 runs in reverse to obtain tenselessness — the step on which the whole of §5 and §6 depends, and which is an analytic consequence of the theory's own definition rather than an addition to it. The second is the distinction developed in §2.4: that contextualisation is a *process acting on* a sensory representation rather than a permanent property, which is the mechanism the entire therapeutic model rests on, and which invalidates the inference from present narratability to original contextualisation.

The limits of the contribution should be stated with equal precision, since they are absolute. **Dual representation theory says nothing about déjà vu.** It does not mention it, has never been extended to it, and entails nothing about it. It says nothing about how long an S-rep intrusion persists (§4.3). It does not assert that S-reps form routinely from affectively neutral material, which the identification requires and which remains an untested auxiliary claim. And it has not been tested on the one interaction — production against binding — that would most directly bear on whether the identification holds. The theory supplies a specification and a pair of analytic consequences. It supplies no evidence that déjà vu satisfies them; that had to be assembled from the other literature, item by item, which is what §4 does.

### 8.3 Implications for the Framework Hypothesis

Section 2.1 confined the correspondential reading to two places and undertook to mark it where it occurs. This is the first, and it is offered as a reading rather than asserted as a finding. Nothing in §§3–7 depends on it, and a reader who declines it loses none of the argument. It should also be said at the outset what this subsection cannot be: it is not an argument that would move a reader who does not already hold the framework, and no reading of natural-degree evidence could be. Section 6.4 gave the reason. A claim that never competes at the natural degree cannot be carried by natural-degree evidence in either direction.

The natural-plane result of §5.1 is that a representation without a contextual index carries no tense, and that succession is supplied by the receiving system rather than by the content. This is stated there as an analytic consequence of a definition in a trauma-memory model, and it stands on that ground alone.

Read correspondentially, it meets a commitment the framework holds on independent grounds and did not derive from memory research: that extension and succession are properties of the natural degree rather than of consciousness as such — Swedenborg's position, published in 1758, that in the spiritual there is no space and no time but state, and that what appears as distance and duration there is the appearance of state to those in it. On that account tense is not intrinsic to what consciousness receives. It is what happens to what consciousness receives when it is filed by a system operating in extension and succession.

The convergence, if it is one, is this. Dual representation theory arrives at a representational form that carries content without carrying time, and locates the supply of temporal position in a separate contextualising system. The framework holds that time is not a property of the received but of the receiver's degree. Two descriptions, one from clinical psychology in 1996 and one from a theological corpus in 1758, place the temporal index in the same relation to the content: outside it, added by whatever does the filing.

That correspondence concerns a single structural feature. Section 6.4 leaves a wider question: what the framework is able to say here at all, given that it has no entry in the origin column. This is the place to answer it.

What is available is a claim about **form**, and the form of the claim matters more than its content. It is emphatically not that the natural account leaves something out. Section 6.4 makes the opposite a requirement: the natural degree has its own complete expression of whatever manifests at a higher one, and an account with a hole in it would not be better evidence of a higher degree but a worse account. A vessel is entirely explicable — its material, its shape, how it was made, why the wall is thick where it is — and none of that explicability bears on whether it is made to hold anything. What can be said of it is that its shape is a holding shape, and a shape is visible whether or not anything is in it.

Applied here, the observation is that the natural-degree account of recognition has a particular structure. It stores content in a form carrying no position in sequence; it keeps that content outside the reach of the system that would go looking for it; and it delivers it when a situation arrives that matches it. Held without an index, delivered at the point where it belongs. That is what a representational system would have to look like in order to carry something apprehended non-successively through a process that runs successively — and it is also, entirely and without remainder, what the trauma-memory literature describes for reasons of its own that have nothing to do with any of this.

Both halves of that sentence have to be kept together. There are good functional reasons for a nervous system to encode sensory material quickly and bind it slowly, and the literature supplies several. The observation is not that the structure is unexplained; the moment it becomes an argument from inexplicability it has turned back into the gap argument §6.4 forbids. The observation is about shape, and it is offered on those terms and no others.

Three cautions attach, and they are not formalities.

The first is that this is **not a prediction and not a confirmation.** The framework did not predict dual representation theory, and dual representation theory does not support the framework. The relation is the same coherence relation §2.2 defined, one degree further out, and it is weaker here than in §4 because the two descriptions are not describing the same object at the same level — one concerns a representational format in a brain, the other concerns the structure of a degree of reality. Reading the first as an instance of the second is a framework move, available only to someone already standing in the framework.

The second is that **the reading licenses nothing empirical.** It does not favour any candidate in §6, the framework having no candidate there (§6.4); it does not raise the probability of any registered specification succeeding; and it is not a route back into the origin column by another door. A form observation that began to function as an origin argument would have become the error §8.4 names, and should be checked against that subsection whenever it is cited.

The third is the one §2.1 flagged as the discipline this framework most needs. The observation that a memory model and a theological corpus place the temporal index in the same structural position is interesting. It becomes an error the moment it is used to make the framework look prescient — to suggest that the framework anticipated the memory sciences, or that the memory sciences have caught up with it. Neither is true. The framework has no machinery for S-reps, said nothing about representational format before this thesis, and gains no retrospective credit from a correspondence noticed after the fact by someone looking for one. Recording that plainly is the price of being allowed to notice the correspondence at all.

### 8.4 The Inflation Hazard

This thesis has produced a gap, and a framework stands beside it. The gap is the one §5.3 opened: after the format absorbs every reportable property, origin is the only remaining variable, and §6.5 concluded that nothing currently decides it. A framework holding a preferred account of such matters, standing next to an undecided question, is the configuration in which the specific error this project scrutinises elsewhere gets made — **back-filling an undecoded absence with a flattering source.** Section 6.4 closes the most direct route to that error: there is no entry in the origin column to which an absence could be credited. It does not close the pull, which operates whether or not there is a formal entry to attach it to.

The error has a recognisable shape. Something is not accounted for; a source is available that would account for it; the availability is treated as a reason; and what was a gap is quietly reported as a finding. It does not require dishonesty and it rarely announces itself. It happens because a gap is uncomfortable and a candidate is at hand, and because the intermediate state — knowing that something is unexplained and declining to explain it — is harder to hold than either certainty.

Five specific forms of the error are live in the present material, and each is named here so that it can be checked against later.

**Reading the residue as a finding.** Section 7.3 established that a registration study can exclude origins but cannot identify one, so that the best possible outcome is a well-characterised residue: cases where specification preceded event, expectation was excluded, and no prior source was found. A residue is defined by what was not found. Reporting it as evidence for the boundary-state origin converts an absence into a presence, and the conversion is invalid however clean the individual cases and however large the series.

**Taking the format as though it were the origin.** The identification supplies a mechanism, and a mechanism is satisfying in a way that makes it easy to mistake for an answer. It is not one. The format is constant across every origin in §6 and therefore discriminates none of them; what the identification gives the framework is the removal of an anomaly, not the acquisition of evidence (§6.4). Any later use of this thesis that cites the identification as support for a boundary-state origin will have made this mistake, and will additionally have reinstated a claim §6.4 rules out.

**Claiming the framework anticipated something.** Section 8.3 stated it: the framework has no machinery for representational format, said nothing about it before this thesis, and gains no retrospective credit for a correspondence noticed by someone who went looking for one. The temptation here is specific and strong, because the correspondence in §8.3 is genuinely striking, and striking correspondences invite the claim that they were foreseen.

**Looking for the framework's support in the physical evidence.** The three errors above are errors of overreach. This one wears the costume of restraint, which is what makes it the hardest to see. It concedes that most of the evidence is inconclusive, and then locates the framework's real support in whatever narrow class the ordinary accounts reach least well — the verified veridical claims, the unexplained residue, the cases where the natural story is thinnest. The concession makes the move look disciplined. It is the same error in a smaller compass, because it still expects physical evidence to carry a claim that §6.4 established cannot be carried that way. If the framework's support lived in the residue, the framework would be a proposal about physical causation after all, competing badly.

**Reading sufficiency as exhaustion.** The four errors above all run in the framework's favour, and a subsection listing only those would be guarding one side of a road. The inverse error is live in exactly the same material, and it is the easier of the two to commit, because it is the reading any compression of this thesis will reach for by default. It runs: §6 exhibits ordinary physical routes that produce the phenomenon without remainder; therefore the phenomenon is nothing but those routes; therefore nothing further is in question here or anywhere. Each step is a jump. Showing that a route is **sufficient** does not show that it operated. Showing that it operated in a laboratory does not show that other routes do not exist, and §6.5 states that the field is not established to be closed at three. And an account complete at the level it works on settles nothing about whether that level is exhaustive, which §6.4 sets out as a point of general logic requiring no premise of this framework's. Two specific extensions are the ones to watch, because they are the ones the material invites: from a claim about **one class of experience** to a claim about memory in general, and from a claim about how content is **carried** to a claim about where content comes from. Section 1 states that neither extension is made here. Neither is available from anything here either.

The guard against all five is procedural rather than attitudinal, which is why it has been built into the structure of this thesis rather than left as a resolution. The failures in §4.3 are reported in the same place as the successes, with the denominator stated. The scale deficit in §4.4 is named as work not done rather than as a limitation in passing. Section 6.3 sets out the candidate most damaging to the framework at greater length than the framework's own. Section 6.5 says in the required words that nothing currently decides. Section 7.1 states that two of the parent thesis's five signatures cease to be evidence of anything unusual, including the one that thesis named as its strongest. Section 7.2 concedes that the corpus arm was the weaker and that §5.2 removed most of what remained of it.

The standard being applied is the framework's own, and it is worth stating in its own terms. A hypothesis is worth following when it organises data better than the alternatives do. It is not worth following when it is used to fill in what the data have left blank, because a hypothesis that can fill any blank has stopped organising anything. **The blank in §6 is to be left blank.** Any future document in this corpus that closes it is to be checked against this subsection first.

### 8.5 Limitations and Lacunae

**The identification is argued, not cited.** No one in either literature has proposed that déjà vu and the uncontextualised sensory representation are the same event. Dual representation theory has never been extended to déjà vu; déjà vu research does not cite Brewin. The claim in §4 is this thesis's own, and it inherits no empirical support from either programme. It is a coherence claim of the type §2.2 specified, and it should be weighted as one: stronger than an analogy, weaker than a prediction, and defeasible in the specific ways listed there.

**It is one explanatory account among four, and not the field's leading one.** Brown's taxonomy places it in the memory family, alongside dual-processing, neurological, and attentional accounts, none of which has been eliminated. The most direct experimental support for a rival is Urquhart and colleagues (2021), whose fMRI study found anterior cingulate, medial prefrontal, and parietal activation during induced déjà vu — a conflict-monitoring signature rather than a memory one. Three features of that study limit what it can carry: the modified false-memory procedure engineers the very memory conflict it then detects, the stimuli are verbal in a comparison where the format claim specifies non-verbal encoding, and *n* = 21 with 16 reporting déjà vu. Those are real limitations and they do not dispose of the result. It is a positive finding from a controlled paradigm, and the identification has nothing of equivalent directness to set against it.

**The scale scoring has not been done.** Section 4.4 sets out the deficit in full: the eleven properties come from the déjà vu literature's own characterisations rather than from blind scoring of first-person accounts against a pre-registered coding frame. The material exists, the work is a reading task on published sources, and it has not been performed here. Until it is, the claim rests on the fit between two compressions, and the possibility that the fit is partly an artefact of how review authors summarised cannot be bounded from the inside.

**The affect mismatch requires an untested auxiliary claim.** Sensory-bound representations are aversive in every population where they have been studied; déjà vu is not. The response in §4.3 — that aversiveness belongs to content rather than format, and that S-reps are visible in trauma research only because that is where they hurt enough to reach a clinic — is coherent, is not entailed by dual representation theory, has not been asserted by its authors, and has not been tested. Cleary's paradigm provides incidental support by producing neutral-content S-reps in the laboratory, but that support was not designed and should not be leaned on.

**The production–binding interaction is unmeasured.** Adachi's association of déjà vu with good memory function and vivid dream recall and Meyer's association of intrusions with poorer configural binding are reconciled by treating production and binding as independent variables. Dual representation theory permits this. Nobody has measured both in the same participants. Section 6.5 names it as the outstanding test of the identification itself, and it should be settled before further weight is placed on the framing.

**No duration prediction exists.** Déjà vu lasts seconds; dual representation theory says nothing about S-rep duration. This is an absence rather than a mismatch, and it is recorded because omitting it would have improved the apparent match rate without improving the argument.

**Two sources were not verified at first hand.** The figures attributed to Brown's 2004 monograph — approximately one fifth of déjà vu experiences attributed by experiencers to prior dreams, approximately two fifths to dreams and waking life together — are carried from the parent thesis and could not be checked at source, the work being a book rather than an indexed article. Nothing in §6.1 turns on their values, since §2.3 already discounts attributional data. Separately, the neurochemical layer often added to the REM argument — noradrenergic suppression and the cholinergic profile associated with encoding failure — was identified but not read at source, and is therefore not relied upon anywhere in §6.1.

**The tenselessness inversion in §5.2 is a reading, not a result.** Cleary and Claxton did not test it, and their data cannot distinguish it from the ordinary interpretation, because both accounts predict what was observed. It is preferred because it removes an explanatory debt the ordinary reading carries and because it follows from a step taken on independent grounds. That is a reason for preference, not evidence.

**The induction evidence is narrower than the descriptive claim it supports, in two directions at once.** The configural result establishes sufficiency: a matched spatial layout raises the rate of déjà vu from 26 to 31 per cent of retrieval-failure trials, which is reliable and small, and most of the déjà vu occurring inside the paradigm occurs where no match was installed. Whether configural resemblance is also the *usual* origin outside the laboratory is not established by it. Separately, every induction of déjà vu proper has used spatial layout, because that is what a virtual tour can vary while holding content fixed; the auditory analogue has been induced from sequential structure (§3.2), but déjà vu itself has not been induced by non-spatial material. Both gaps are gaps in what has been attempted. Neither is a finding, and neither may be read as one: an untested class is not a reservoir of unexplained cases, and treating it as one would be the §6.4 error committed with a methodological rather than a metaphysical premise.

**The prediction result was obtained in an environment with nothing to anticipate.** Cleary and Claxton's continuations were laid out by the experimenters and were unrelated to anything in the scene, so the study tests whether a memory signal delivers an arbitrary next turn. It does not. Whether the same feeling behaves the same way where a situation has structure that constrains how it continues has not been tested, and the authors record from their own side that the paradigm cannot observe the trial as it unfolds (§5.2). Two things must be held together here. The untested case is genuinely untested, and nothing in this thesis rests on it: the inadmissibility conclusion in §5.2 is entailed by the format argument in §5.1 independently of what any prediction experiment returns. Noticing that an experiment did not reach a case is not evidence that anything is recoverable in the case it did not reach.

**Cleary and Claxton (2018) could not be read in full at source.** The article is closed-access and not deposited in PubMed Central; the abstract, the effect sizes, and the stimulus description were obtained at source, the latter two by way of a later open-access study using the identical materials (McNeely-White and Cleary 2023). One design feature therefore remains unconfirmed: whether the study included a positive control demonstrating above-chance turn prediction on trials where the studied path *was* recalled. Its presence would strengthen the result and its absence would weaken it, and this thesis does not know which obtains.

**The origin candidates are not an enumeration, and the thesis does not know the origin of anything.** Section 6 examines three routes because three have published mechanisms behind them. Nothing here establishes that the list is complete, and no work of this kind could: demonstrating that a route exists shows that it exists, not that it is the only one, and a source that can be pinpointed in a laboratory says nothing about the sources that cannot. Nor does the thesis identify which route produced any actual instance; §6.5 states in the required words that nothing decides. Both halves matter, because the two available misreadings pull in opposite directions — a preference among the three, which §6.5 refuses, and a demonstration that the three are all there is, which §8.4 names as the fifth error. The whole of §§4, 5, and 7 stands without either.

**The correspondential reading in §8.3 is offered, not asserted**, and licenses nothing empirical. It is available only from inside the framework and is not put forward as support for it.

**How consciousness moves between degrees is unexplained, and the gap touches more of this thesis than its placement here suggests.** The position taken in §6.4 and §8.3 concerns how degrees stand in relation to one another. It says nothing about how experience moves between them, and that movement is the load-bearing unexplained thing in this area. Reports from boundary states describe experience rising and then dropping back, its character changing completely at each level to whatever that level admits, as though consciousness were present on several degrees at once while having to take the form of whichever one it is prominently expressing on. Four features of that are unaccounted for. Simultaneous presence on several degrees does not by itself entail a single seat of experience, so what produces prominence is unexplained. Prominence is difficult to leave, and stickiness does not follow from prominence. Whether extremity raises attention to a higher degree or merely removes what was holding it at the lower one is not decided by any description available, and those are very different mechanisms. And the transit itself is never reported: every account is composed at one end of it, in the vocabulary of the degree it describes a departure from, so that endpoints accumulate in a corpus and the movement does not. The last of these means the deficit is structural rather than a matter of collecting more accounts.

**A related corpus is not treated here.** The reincarnation-case literature bears on the same unexplained mechanism from another direction, reporting states that appear to take form not only in memory but in tissue — birthmarks corresponding to recorded wounds. A mark is not a record but a shape, which makes such cases awkward for any account in which what carries across is information. No argument in this thesis rests on that literature and none is built from it; it is noted because a reader following the mechanism above will find it there as well.

**The gradient across REM, trauma, and boundary states is a resemblance, not a measurement.** Section 6.1 records that contextual binding is impaired during REM and impaired again under the hormonal conditions of extreme trauma, and the near-death literature describes something more extensive. Placing the three on a single continuum of increasing impairment is a natural reading and an unsupported one. Nobody has measured them as one variable, and three literatures describing similar-looking degradations are not thereby describing one.

**Nothing here establishes a referent.** Removing the misfire gloss (§3.4) reopens the question of what the familiarity signal corresponds to; it does not answer it. Removing two of the parent thesis's five signatures (§7.1) reduces that thesis's evidential claim; it does not refute its positive argument. And the reduction in §5.3 leaves origin fully open, with §6.5 stating that nothing currently decides among three candidates, all of them entirely mundane, one of which — constructive simulation — is sufficient on its own to produce the whole phenomenology of déjà vu. Sufficiency is not actuality and three studied routes are not a closed field, and the sentence should not be read as saying otherwise (§8.4).

## 9. Conclusion: Recognition Without Tense

The claim defended here is narrower than the one the parent thesis was reaching for, and it is on firmer ground. It is not that déjà vu proves anything about the future, and not that the memory sciences have been shown to support a framework they know nothing about. It is that two literatures, working on unrelated problems and not citing one another, wrote down the same object.

The evidence for that is the accounting. Fourteen properties were considered; eleven matched. Two of the eleven are of a kind that could not have been arranged after the fact. Brewin, Dalgleish, and Joseph defined a memory system in 1996 as the one "automatically accessible through appropriate situational cues," writing about post-traumatic intrusion and thinking about déjà vu not at all — and the phrase is not compatible with déjà vu's trigger condition, it is a statement of it. Cleary's laboratory established that déjà vu is driven by configural resemblance in the parahippocampus; Meyer and colleagues established that efficiency at exactly that encoding predicts perceptual intrusion rate. One variable, one structure, two ends of a single relationship, found by two groups who did not know the other end existed. Three properties failed and were reported with the rest, because a match rate without a denominator is not a result.

That is a coherence claim and nothing more. It cannot be cited into existence; it can only be itemised, which is what §4 does. Its whole evidential force comes from a single feature: **it could not have been arranged.** Both literatures are published and fixed. If the descriptions match at this level of detail, they matched before anyone looked, and coincidence has to carry the weight instead.

What follows from the identification is a subtraction, and the subtraction falls on the framework that proposed it.

If a déjà vu is a sensory representation firing without its context, then the representation carries no contextual index, and an index is what supplies tense. The vehicle is tenseless. It does not deliver pastness and it does not deliver futurity; both are readings laid over it by a system that has to file untensed material somewhere, and both should strengthen together as the signal strengthens — which is what Cleary and colleagues found. A report that an experience *felt* like foreknowledge therefore says nothing about whether it was, and that disqualifies most of what a corpus of retrospective accounts can offer, including the corpus the parent thesis analysed.

The subtraction continues. Sourcelessness, situational triggering, perceptual character, involuntariness, brevity, the sense of alreadyness, the impossibility of deliberate recall before the moment — every one is fixed by the format and constant across instances. None can distinguish one instance from another. Two of the parent thesis's five structural signatures, including the one it named as its strongest, cease to be evidence of anything unusual and become evidence that the material travels in a common representational form.

What is left is one variable: what laid the representation down. And on that question the honest report is that **nothing currently decides.** Three candidates are live, and all three are ordinary — a dream, an unretrieved glimpse, a scene the mind assembled out of its own expectations — and one of them, constructive simulation, accounts for the whole phenomenology of déjà vu without strain. That is a statement about sufficiency, made of three routes that happen to have been studied. It is not a statement that the source of any given instance is among them, and nothing here establishes that the field is closed at three.

The framework that proposed the identification does not add a fourth. It cannot, and the reason is its own: it makes no claim about which physical process laid anything down, so there is no gap in the physical account for it to occupy and no version of it that needs one. That closes a whole class of argument, including arguments the framework would like to have. What it leaves is not an entry in the origin column but an observation about form — that a system storing content without a place in sequence, unreachable by the faculty that would search for it, and delivered when the situation arrives, is shaped the way a vessel is shaped. A vessel proves nothing about what it holds. Nor does a complete account of the vessel prove it empty; an account complete at the level it works on settles what that level is doing and settles nothing about whether that level is all there is. Its explanation is complete without reference to any contents, and that completeness is not a defect in the argument but a condition of it. Its explanation is complete without reference to any contents, and that completeness is not a defect in the argument but a condition of it.

That is not the result the framework would have chosen, and reporting it is the point. A hypothesis earns its standing by organising data better than the alternatives, not by filling in what the data left blank. The blank here is real and is to be left blank. What the argument has produced is not an answer but a properly stated question — asked of the one thing the experience cannot say about itself, by a vehicle that carries content faithfully and provenance not at all.

There is a discipline in that, and it is the same one the déjà vu literature has been practising without quite saying so. A person is walking through a room they have never entered and something in them says *already*. The saying is real; the room is new; and the honest position is that we know what kind of thing said it, and we do not know where it had been.

Third, prospective dream collection with blind configural matching (§6.5), which would bound how much ordinary déjà vu the dream and simulation routes account for. Fourth, verification at source of the two items flagged in §8.5.
