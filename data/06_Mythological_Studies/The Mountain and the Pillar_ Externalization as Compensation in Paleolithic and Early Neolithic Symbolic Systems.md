# The Mountain and the Pillar: Externalization as Compensation
## Correspondential Readings of Paleolithic and Early Neolithic Symbolic Systems

---

> **Abstract.** Between the Upper Paleolithic and the fourth millennium BCE, the relationship between a mark and the thing that carries it is transformed. Marks are first placed on found surfaces — cave walls whose natural contours are incorporated into the image. Then, at Göbekli Tepe, limestone is quarried, carved, transported and raised: the object bearing the meaning is now manufactured. Then the symbolic installation is fixed inside built and inhabited rooms at Çatalhöyük. Then, at Uruk, writing detaches the sign from its support altogether. At every step the support is more made and less found, the meaning more constructed and less indicated. This thesis sets out that trajectory from the archaeological record, states what the natural sciences can and cannot supply in explaining it, and then reads it through the doctrine of correspondences. The doctrinal sequence used is *Arcana Coelestia* §920, which names four stages of **worship** in order — upon mountains, in groves among trees, at pillars or statues in the open air, and at last at altars — with the hinge between found things and made things falling exactly where the archaeology places it, and which was published in 1749, before Paleolithic art was accepted as authentic and two and a half centuries before Göbekli Tepe was excavated. Because the stages are stages of worship, the administrative tablet is held outside the doctrinal mapping entirely, and the fourth stage is treated as open rather than terminal: altar worship continues for millennia after Uruk. Two carved pillars are read in detail: Pillar 18 as the human form rendered in built stone, and Pillar 43 as a legible sequence of scorpion, headless figure and disk-bearing vulture. Both readings work from the carved surface alone and depend on no astronomical, chronological or positional claim. Throughout, a correspondential reading is presented as what it is: a demonstration of what emerges when the doctrine is applied, never an assertion about what the makers intended.

> **Keywords.** Upper Paleolithic parietal art; geometric signs; Geneviève von Petzinger; Göbekli Tepe; Pillar 43; Pillar 18; doctrine of correspondences; externalization; *Arcana Coelestia* 920; significative and representative; neurovisual resonance theory; entoptic phenomena; stylistic dating; Ancient Word; Çatalhöyük; Uruk archaic tablets

## 1. Introduction

Somewhere between the painted caves of Ice Age Europe and the temple accounts of fourth-millennium Uruk, the relationship between a mark and the thing that bears it is inverted. At the beginning the surface is given: a cave existed, its walls had contours, and the marks were placed into a topography nobody designed. The horses at Lascaux stand with their hooves on a natural rock ledge. A tectiform at El Castillo sits beneath a natural crevice. The image works *with* what was found there. At the end the surface is a product: clay levigated, shaped into a tablet, impressed with a standardised stylus, dried, filed in a room, and read by scribes trained in schools to convert the marks into quantities of barley. Nothing in that object was found. Every part of it was manufactured to carry the sign.

Between those two conditions stands Göbekli Tepe. Limestone was quarried from bedrock terraces, dressed into T-shaped monoliths several metres tall and weighing up to ten tonnes, moved several hundred metres, and raised in circular enclosures in the open air. The pillars carry reliefs — foxes, snakes, scorpions, cranes, boars, a vulture with a disk on its wing, a headless human figure. The carving is confident and systematic. But the fact that matters most for the argument here is prior to any reading of the carvings: **the thing bearing the meaning has been made.** A cave is found. A pillar is built.

This thesis takes that trajectory as its subject. It sets out the archaeological sequence from marking found surfaces, through erecting made monuments, through fixing symbolic installations inside built rooms, to detaching the sign from its support altogether. It states, with care, what the natural sciences can supply in explaining that sequence and where their own practitioners locate the limit of what they can supply. And it then reads the sequence through the doctrine of correspondences — declaring, before the reading begins, exactly what such a reading claims and what it does not.

The doctrinal sequence used is a short passage in *Arcana Coelestia* §920. Describing the worship of the Most Ancient Church, Swedenborg writes that when they saw a high mountain "they perceived an idea, not of a mountain, but of elevation, and from elevation, of heaven and the Lord... and that afterwards the worship of the Lord was held on mountains." When perception failed, what remained was the collected significations, and from these arose "their worship upon mountains, and in groves in the midst of trees, and also of their pillars or statues in the open air, and at last of the altars and burnt-offerings which afterwards became the principal things of all worship" (*AC* §920[4]). Four stages, in order. And the hinge falls between the second and the third: **mountains and groves are found; pillars and altars are made.** Göbekli Tepe is manufactured pillars standing in the open air, which is stage three of a sequence published in 1749 — before Paleolithic parietal art was accepted as authentic, and two and a half centuries before the site was excavated.

The comparison inverts the usual reading of the material. On the standard account, monumental construction marks an advance: the moment symbolic capacity becomes powerful enough to organise labour and raise stone. On the doctrinal account the arrow runs the other way. Codification increases as direct perception declines, and externalization is compensation rather than achievement. Swedenborg is explicit. Of the Most Ancient Church he writes that "the Word was not, because the men of that church had the Word inscribed on their hearts" (*AC* §3432[2]) — notation is absent where perception is present, because there is nothing for notation to do. Of the transition to spoken language: "when such determination of the ideas of thought took place, that is to say, into spoken words, they could no longer be instructed... through the internal man, but through the external" (*AC* §608). Of the whole descent: "wisdom retired from inmost things to outermost; and... man had removed himself from heaven, and had at last descended even to the dust of the earth, wherein he now places wisdom" (*AC* §3432[3]).

This is not a declinist thesis, and §6.7 argues the point in full. The same passage that describes the closing of the internal way ends: "if the Lord had not brought the human race into this genius, or into this state, no man could have been saved" (*AC* §608). The externalization is a mercy. The pillar is not a monument to what was lost; it is what was provided when the mountain could no longer be read.

Two carved pillars are read in detail, in §6.3 and §6.4. Pillar 18 is read as the human form built — a T-shaped monolith bearing a belt, a buckle and long arms whose hands meet at the abdomen, which is to say a body standing where a body once did not need to be depicted. Pillar 43 is read as a legible sequence: a scorpion, a headless ithyphallic figure, and a vulture upholding a disk. Both readings work from the carved surface alone. Neither depends on the pillar's astronomical alignment, its original position, its date, or on any identification of the animal reliefs with constellations. §6.5 states that independence explicitly, because it is the feature that determines what kind of claim the reading is making.

The thesis proceeds as follows. §2 sets out the method: what a correspondential reading is, what it claims and does not claim, how the two tiers are kept separate, the resolution limits of the parietal record, and the doctrinal principle applied. §3 describes the sign corpus as it is documented, at its actual size and scope. §4 states what entoptic and neurovisual accounts supply, what a generative model of natural-scene statistics supplies, and where the discipline's own practitioners place the boundary of inference. §5 presents the trajectory from indicating to constructing. §6 gives the correspondential reading, including the two pillars. §7 discusses what each tier contributes, the status of a reading, and the limitations of the whole. §8 concludes. Two appendices give the full sequence mapping and the correspondential keys used.

## 2. Methodological Framework

The two-tiered method used throughout this library holds that rigorous secular inquiry produces an accurate description of observable phenomena — the "body" of effects — and that correspondential reading supplies the interior account of cause and meaning. The two tiers do different work and answer different questions, and the value of the method depends on keeping them genuinely separate: the archaeological description must be arrived at without reference to the reading it will later be compared against, or the comparison is not a comparison.

This section states the framework in the order it will be used. §2.1 sets out what a correspondential reading is and how it is performed. §2.2 states what such a reading claims and what it does not — the most important paragraph in the document, because everything in §6 is governed by it. §2.3 states how the two tiers are kept apart. §2.4 reports the resolution limits of the parietal record, which constrain what any chronological argument may say. §2.5 sets out the doctrinal principle applied.

### 2.1 What a Correspondential Reading Is

Correspondence, in the sense used here, is not allegory, not symbol, and not metaphor. Allegory is a code assigned by an author: the writer decides that the lion shall stand for courage, and the equation holds because it was stipulated. Symbol is looser still — a resonance felt by a community, revisable, culturally contingent. Metaphor is a comparison between two things that remain two things. Correspondence claims something different and considerably stronger: that the natural object *is* the spiritual reality, expressed at the natural degree. The relation is not similarity but identity across degrees. Swedenborg's own example is the mountain. The men of the Most Ancient Church, he writes, "when they saw a high mountain... perceived an idea, not of a mountain, but of elevation, and from elevation, of heaven and the Lord" (*AC* §920). They were not decoding a symbol. They were seeing what the mountain is.

It follows that **correspondential reading is not dictionary lookup.** A reading is not performed by matching each element of a composition against a table of equivalents and stringing the results together. That procedure will always produce an output, and the output will be as arbitrary as the table. The procedure used here has four steps and each of them constrains the next.

**Step one: identify the governing principle of the whole composition before reading any part of it.** A composition is read as a statement, not as a list. What is the scene doing — what is its subject, its action, its direction? Elements are then read as contributions to that statement. This is what prevents a reading from becoming a concatenation of independent lookups.

**Step two: read body parts as receptors.** In the correspondential anatomy — Swedenborg's *Maximus Homo*, the Grand Man — the parts of the human body correspond to functions of the mind and spirit by what they do. The scale used here places the head as highest and inmost, the upper chest as the reception and circulation of spiritual life, the loins as generative life, and the extremities as ultimates. This is not a claim that each large body region contains only one degree. Swedenborg says that the celestial kingdom of the heart and spiritual kingdom of the lungs combine "within each member and interior organ" (*AC* §4931); the degrees recur within the body as well as ordering it.

The head is not the understanding. In the brain rest the will and everything that arises from the will, and the will is what the brain is organised around: the understanding is the clothing that clothes the will, not the thing clothed. The brain arranges itself around a person's will the way the universe arranges itself around gravity — not one occupant among others, but the principle by which the whole takes its order. What belongs to the head is therefore celestial love, love to the Lord, seated in the will, with the understanding as its garment.

The upper chest below it carries the spiritual reception of what comes from above, and it carries it as a dependency rather than as two parallel organs. The lungs are the spiritual understanding — what Swedenborg calls faith. Nothing in this says the lungs perform a brain's work; the reading comes from what lungs actually do. They receive, hold and provide oxygen, and they make none of it. To breathe in is to receive influx, and what is received comes in aligned with the will, which is why what is seen and received on the spiritual degree is always aligned with the higher faculties. Oxygen is understanding that is not one's own. Swedenborg directly joins the lungs with the truth of faith and respiration with thought (*AC* §§3883–3886, 4931); the oxygen formulation is the functional reading applied here, not a phrase quoted from him.

The heart depends on that supply and can give life to nothing until the lungs have provided it. What the heart then does is carry it to every part of the body without exception. That circuit is the correspondence used here: the heart is spiritual love, which is love of the neighbour, and love of the neighbour does not originate in itself — it distributes through the whole what has been received from above. *AC* §4931 supplies the governing heart–lungs relation: truth of faith leads into the good of love, after which the lungs come under the governance of the heart.

The loins and generative organs supply the natural expression of generative life in the body scale used here. Swedenborg treats them as the province of genuine conjugial love and generation (*AC* §5050); their use below for natural life persisting and propagating is the application made by this thesis. These are not name-associations. The vocabulary in which they are stated is Swedenborg's, and carries the doctrinal and Christian idiom of his period; what is used here is the structure that vocabulary describes, not the idiom.

**Step three: read a creature from what the creature actually does.** The affection or faculty at issue must be established for the creature in context, not assigned by a universal rule about animals or by genus alone. Where a creature is read as an affection, and the corpus gives several readings of it, those are contextual readings *from* that affection rather than competing definitions. The reading is derived from the creature's real behaviour in the world, not from its name or from a modern cultural association. Ancient descriptions can supply that functional evidence; they do not by themselves supply the correspondence.

**And read a creature's parts as a body's parts.** The scale in step two is not a rule for human bodies only; it holds for humans and animals alike. For the tail, Swedenborg gives the exact anatomy: it is "the last extension of the head, because the brain extends through the spinal column into the tail," so that head and tail are united as first and last (*AR* §438). The tail is therefore the creature's absolute ultimate because it is the final extension of the head through the spine, not merely because it is an outer extremity. Where a creature carries its weapon there, what is delivered from it is delivered from that absolute ultimate. The weapon does not displace the affection: the love is the thing, and the weapon delivers what that love produces when it is provoked.

**Step four: let position supply context without collapsing its axes.** Where an element sits — high or low, above or below another element, in a deep chamber or near the entrance — modifies the reading, but height and depth do not therefore mean the same thing. Height commonly marks elevation within the degree being expressed, especially the elevation of thought and reasoning relative to what lies below it. Depth marks movement into what is more interior, and can therefore carry a composition through deeper degrees while the vertical relations within each degree remain intact. The two may converge where only one axis is available. Where a cave supplies both, they must be read separately.

One further rule governs all four steps: **you can only read what is reliably depicted.** A reading may use a feature that is present on the surface and visible to anyone who looks. It may not use a feature that is reconstructed, inferred, restored, or supplied from a parallel. Where a relief is damaged, the damaged portion is not read. Where a scholar disputes what a figure depicts, the reading either accommodates both or declines the element.

### 2.2 What a Reading Claims, and What It Does Not

This is stated positively and in advance, because it determines how everything in §6 should be understood.

**A correspondential reading claims: this is what emerges when the doctrine of correspondences is applied to this object.** That is a claim about the doctrine and about the object together. It can be checked — the elements are either present on the surface or they are not; the correspondences are either the ones the doctrine assigns elsewhere or they are ad hoc; the result is either a statement or a heap. A reading can be performed badly, and a badly performed reading can be identified as such by the criteria in §2.1.

**A correspondential reading does not claim: this is what the makers meant.** It is not a decipherment. It does not assert that the carvers of Göbekli Tepe held the doctrine, knew these correspondences, or intended this content. No archaeological finding about maker intent is offered, and none is needed, because none is claimed.

The distinction matters most where a reading and an archaeological hypothesis appear to concern the same object. An archaeoastronomical reading of a carved pillar — that its reliefs encode a datable configuration of the sky — is a historical claim about what the carvers did. It stands or falls on in-situ position, on the stability of the asterisms invoked, on the excavated date of the enclosure, and on whether the pillar was re-used from an earlier context. Those are exactly the grounds on which such claims are contested, and rightly so. A correspondential reading makes no such claim and therefore does not stand or fall on any of those grounds. The two are not rival answers to one question; they are answers to two different questions, and evidence bearing on one does not bear on the other. §6.5 works this through for Pillar 43 specifically.

What a reading also does not do is confirm itself by being coherent. Coherence is the *condition* of a successful reading, not evidence for it: a reading that produced a heap would have failed, but a reading that produces a statement has only met the bar for being a reading at all. It has not thereby demonstrated that the object was constructed to be read that way. This is stated here once, as a property of the method, and is not revisited as a criticism of any particular result.

### 2.3 The Two Tiers and Their Separation

Three practical rules keep the tiers apart in what follows.

**Archaeological claims are described before the reading and independently of it.** §§3–5 are written on ordinary archaeological grounds. They include findings that are unhelpful to the doctrinal reading — that the simplest sign forms have a good generative explanation requiring no transmission at all, that the more complex forms are regionally restricted rather than universal, that the parietal chronology is largely stylistic. Those findings stay in. A natural tier that only ever produces framework-friendly results is not functioning as a natural tier, and the comparison in §6 is worth making only because §§3–5 were not fitted to it.

**Sources are cited at their own weight, and live disputes are cited from both sides.** Doctrinal claims are cited to *Arcana Coelestia* by section number, verified against the Potts translation directly. Archaeological claims are cited to peer-reviewed literature; where a figure exists in both a peer-reviewed and a popular version, the peer-reviewed version governs. Where a proposal about a site is contested in print, both the proposal and the response are cited.

**Numbers are reported with their scope attached.** A site count carries the region and the date span it covers. A frequency carries its denominator. A chronological claim states whether its dates are direct or stylistic. This is ordinary practice, and it is stated because figures in this domain travel between a peer-reviewed thesis, a popular book, and a conference talk, and acquire different scopes along the way.

### 2.4 The Resolution of the Parietal Record

The most consequential fact about Upper Paleolithic parietal art, for any argument that depends on chronology, is that it is almost entirely undated. This is the finding of a 2011 paper in *Antiquity* by Geneviève von Petzinger and April Nowell — the researcher whose sign corpus this thesis uses, reporting on the limits of her own material:

> "The authors deconstruct the basis for dating the Palaeolithic cave paintings of France and find it wanting. Only five per cent are directly dated and the remainder belong to a stylistic framework that has grown organically, and with much circularity, as new paintings were brought to light. Following a constructive *bouleversement*, the authors recommend a new chronometric foundation based on chains of evidence anchored by radiocarbon dates. The story so far is striking: it brings many of the themes and techniques thought typical of the later painters into the repertoire of their much earlier predecessors."

Three consequences follow.

The first concerns the stylistic framework. Ninety-five per cent of the French corpus is assigned to a period not by measurement but by stylistic attribution, and the attribution framework was built inductively from the material it is used to date. That is the circularity the paper identifies. It does not make the framework worthless — stylistic seriation is a legitimate archaeological instrument and often the only one available — but it means the period labels are interpretations of the evidence, not independent measurements of it. Any argument whose structure is *sign type X appears in period P and not in period Q* is therefore an argument about a classification scheme unless the specific attributions have been anchored to dates.

The second concerns direction. Where direct dating has been applied, it "brings many of the themes and techniques thought typical of the later painters into the repertoire of their much earlier predecessors." The corrections are not random noise around the stylistic estimates; they run systematically toward greater antiquity for features previously thought late. Any developmental account that requires late features to be late is therefore working against the direction in which the evidence is currently moving, and should say so.

The third concerns correlation with external series. Where sign frequencies are correlated against climate, one series is resolved by thousands of independent radiometric determinations — Clark and colleagues used 5,704 radiocarbon, beryllium-10 and helium-3 ages to bracket the Last Glacial Maximum, conventionally to 26,500–19,000 years ago — and the other is ninety-five per cent stylistic attribution within a framework its own compiler describes as circular. A correlation between them cannot be tighter than the coarser series permits. §3.5 states the climate correlation at the resolution the coarser series actually supports.

**Where this thesis makes a chronological claim, it states whether the dates are direct or stylistic.** For most of the parietal material the honest answer is stylistic, and arguments requiring finer resolution than that are not made. This is why the argument of §5 is built on *sequence* rather than on dates within the Upper Paleolithic. The distinction between marking a found cave surface and quarrying, carving, transporting and erecting a multi-tonne pillar does not depend on stylistic attribution at all. Göbekli Tepe's enclosures are radiocarbon-dated to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B; Çatalhöyük's occupation is radiocarbon-dated; the Uruk archaic texts are stratigraphically anchored. The ordering of found-surface marking before monumental construction is secure by tens of millennia and survives any plausible revision of the parietal chronology.

### 2.5 Externalization as Compensation: The Doctrinal Principle

The doctrinal claim this thesis applies is stated by Swedenborg across a small number of passages in *Arcana Coelestia* (hereafter *AC*), and it is stated with enough precision to be compared against a material record. All quotations below are from the Potts translation and were verified against the text directly.

The claim is that **externalized symbolic systems arise to compensate for lost perception, and are therefore evidence of decline in the perceptual channel even where external capability advances**. It has four components.

The first is that when perception was direct, there was no external text at all. Of the Most Ancient Church: "In the most ancient time, when the celestial church existed, the Word was not, because the men of that church had the Word inscribed on their hearts; for the Lord taught them immediately through heaven what was good, and thence what was true" (*AC* §3432[2]). This is a strong and specific claim. It is not that early humanity had a simpler notation; it is that notation is absent where perception is present, because there is nothing for notation to do. Writing appears in the succeeding church: "at first this church had no other Word than that which had been gathered from the most ancient people... Hence it was the wisdom of that time both to speak and to write by representatives and significatives."

The second is that the shift is caused by closure, not by growth. "When internal respiration ceased, external respiration gradually succeeded... and with external respiration a language of words, or of articulate sound into which the ideas of thought were determined. Thus the state of man was entirely changed, and became such that he could no longer have similar perception... And when such determination of the ideas of thought took place, that is to say, into spoken words, they could no longer be instructed, like the most ancient man, through the internal man, but through the external. And therefore in place of the revelations of the Most Ancient Church, doctrinal things succeeded" (*AC* §608). The sequence is explicit: internal channel closes, external channel opens *in its place*. Spoken language itself is on the compensating side of the ledger. §609 states the same for instruction generally: "his internals were closed, so that he no longer had communication with heaven, except such as was unconscious. Nor, for the same reason, could he be instructed except... by the external way of sense."

The third is that the compensation proceeds by stages, and the stages are material. This is the passage that carries the argument of this thesis, and §6.1 examines it in full. Having described the loss — "when the church declined... and that perception or communication with heaven began to be lost, another state of things commenced. Then no longer did men perceive anything heavenly in the objects of the senses, as they had done before, but merely what is worldly" (*AC* §920[3]) — Swedenborg describes what was provided in its place, and names four things in order: "this was the origin of their worship upon mountains, and in groves in the midst of trees, and also of their pillars or statues in the open air, and at last of the altars and burnt-offerings which afterwards became the principal things of all worship" (*AC* §920[4]). Mountains and groves are found. Pillars and altars are made. The sequence has a hinge, and the hinge is the transition from indicating to constructing.

The fourth is that the externalized system changes further once its key is lost, and that this change has a specific form. "With the Ancient Church these were significative; for through them they learned internal things, and from them they thought of spiritual and celestial things. But when this knowledge began to perish, so that they did not know that such things were signified, and began to regard the terrestrial and worldly things as holy, and to worship them, with no thought of their signification, the same things were then made representative. Thus arose the Representative Church" (*AC* §1409[2]). The stages are named in reverse in the same section: "representatives had their rise from the significatives of the Ancient Church, and these from the celestial ideas of the Most Ancient Church." Celestial idea, then significative, then representative. In the final transition, what is lost is not the form but the knowledge of what the form is for — and the form, unmoored, is promoted to the status of the thing it once signified. This is the mechanism this library elsewhere calls literalization, and it is Swedenborg's own account of it.

Two things should be said about how this principle is used here, both of them restrictive.

It is **not used as a premise**. The thesis does not assume the doctrine is true and then read the archaeology to fit. §§3–5 establish what the material record shows on ordinary archaeological grounds, including several findings that are unhelpful to the doctrine, and §6 performs the comparison afterward. The order matters: a comparison is only informative if the compared series was described independently.

And it is **not read as a moral narrative**. Swedenborg's own framing forbids that, and §6.7 develops the point. The passage describing the closing of the internal way concludes: "if the Lord had not brought the human race into this genius, or into this state, no man could have been saved" (*AC* §608). The externalization is providential — a mercy extended when the prior mode became unavailable, not a punishment and not a failure of nerve. The people who quarried and raised the pillars at Göbekli Tepe are not, on this account, doing something lesser than the people who marked the walls of Chauvet. They are doing what remained available.

## 3. The Sign Corpus: What Is Documented

The recurring abstract marks of Upper Paleolithic parietal art are the least studied and most numerous part of the record. This section describes the corpus as it stands in the primary literature: its scope, its central finding, the structure and size of its inventory, its geographic patterning, and its distribution across time. Everything here is stated at the resolution its sources support, so that §6 has something firm to be compared against.

### 3.1 The Dataset

The foundational dataset is Geneviève von Petzinger's 2009 master's thesis at the University of Victoria, *Making the Abstract Concrete: The Place of Geometric Signs in French Upper Palaeolithic Parietal Art*. Its scope is stated in its title and its abstract: **153 sites, in France, spanning approximately 22,000 years.**

France was chosen deliberately, for what the thesis describes as its abundance of decorated sites and its natural boundaries of water and mountain ranges, which make it a coherent unit of study rather than an arbitrary slice. The geographic boundedness is a design feature, not a limitation, and it fixes what the dataset can be used to say: this is French Upper Paleolithic parietal art, and a claim about pan-European or global sign distribution requires a different dataset.

A separate and later compilation, reported in von Petzinger's 2016 popular treatment and in associated public lectures, covers **over 5,000 geometric signs from nearly 400 European Ice Age sites**. That is a genuinely continental dataset and a much larger one, and where a claim in this thesis concerns Europe rather than France it is that compilation that is meant. The two should not be merged: the 2009 thesis is the peer-reviewed, methodologically documented body of work, and the figures in it are the ones used here for anything load-bearing.

One methodological feature of the 2009 thesis matters throughout §3.5. Its figures are captioned as proportions to **period site totals** — sign occurrences are normalised against the number of known sites in each period rather than reported as raw counts. This is the correct treatment for a record in which the number of surviving and excavated sites varies enormously by period, and any frequency stated below carries its denominator with it.

### 3.2 Signs Outnumber Figurative Images

The central empirical observation of the 2009 thesis is that **geometric signs tend to outnumber figurative images** in the French corpus. The horses, bison, aurochs and lions that constitute the entire popular image of Ice Age art, and that absorbed nearly all scholarly attention for a century after Altamira, are the minority of what is actually on the walls.

The neglect this corrects has an obvious cause. Figurative images are legible to modern eyes. A painted bison is recognisably a bison, and it invites the interpretive moves — hunting magic, totemism, sympathetic ritual — that dominated twentieth-century readings. A row of dots invites nothing. It was therefore recorded inconsistently, published selectively, and in some site reports omitted altogether. Von Petzinger's contribution was in significant part archival: assembling, from published site reports of very uneven quality, a systematic inventory of material that previous recorders had treated as background noise.

This matters for what follows in two ways. It establishes that abstract marking, not animal depiction, is the dominant activity in the corpus — so that any account of what these caves were for has to account for the marks first. And it establishes that the marks are *systematic* rather than incidental: they recur, in identifiable types, across sites and across periods.

### 3.3 A Bounded Inventory, and the Question of Its Size

The inventory is bounded. The same limited set of forms reappears across sites and across long periods, rather than each site generating idiosyncratic marks. The recurring types include the line, dot, open angle, cruciform, crosshatch, ladder, penniform, negative and positive hand stencils, oval, circle, half-circle, quadrangle, triangle, zigzag, serpentiform, spiral, spanish tectiform, aviform, claviform, reniform, scalariform, flabelliform, cordiform, pectiform, and unciform, among others.

The **number** of types in that inventory is a taxonomic decision rather than a natural constant, and it should be stated as one. The same researcher's material circulates as twenty-six types and as thirty-two, depending on how finely certain forms are split — whether positive and negative hand stencils count as one type or two, whether the several tectiform variants are grouped or separated, whether a form attested at a single site is admitted as a type at all. These are ordinary classificatory choices of the kind every typology involves, and reasonable specialists resolve them differently.

The consequence is that **no inference should be drawn from the number itself.** What the inventory demonstrates is boundedness — a closed and repeatedly reused repertoire rather than an open one — and boundedness is the finding that has content. Whether that repertoire is enumerated at twenty-six or thirty-two is a fact about the enumerator.

### 3.4 Regional Structure

Certain sign types cluster geographically. **Tectiforms are concentrated in the Dordogne. Aviforms are concentrated in Quercy and the Charente.** The pattern is well attested and checkable, and it is fairly described as regional dialect.

A sign type restricted to one river basin is direct evidence of **transmission within a community**: people taught it to each other, in a place, over generations, and it did not spread beyond the network that carried it. This is what cultural transmission looks like in the material record, and its presence in the corpus is a positive finding — it establishes that at least some of these forms were taught rather than independently reinvented.

The pattern has a structure worth stating explicitly, because §4 shows the same structure recurring at global scale. **The widely distributed signs are the geometrically simple ones** — line, dot, crosshatch, ladder, hand stencil — **and the regionally restricted signs are the complex ones** — tectiform, aviform, claviform. Simple marks are the marks a hand makes with the least specification, and they recur wherever people mark surfaces. Complex marks require specification, and specification travels only as far as teaching does. The corpus therefore contains two populations with different distributional behaviour, and any account of it should treat them separately rather than as one phenomenon.

Comparative surveys of geometric marking outside Europe — in Sulawesi, in Australian rock art, in southern African assemblages, in Amazonian traditions — find the same split. The simple geometric core recurs broadly; the complex named forms of the French typology largely do not appear outside their European ranges. Estimates of overlap between the European inventory and non-European assemblages vary widely between surveys and depend heavily on how loosely a match is scored, so no single figure is reported here.

### 3.5 The Magdalenian Concentration

Sign occurrences are not evenly distributed across the Upper Paleolithic. Taking the crosshatch as a documented instance: within the 2009 dataset, the great majority of all sites bearing crosshatch marks — on the order of four-fifths — are Magdalenian.

That figure is a **share of the total**, and as a share of the total it is substantially an artifact of site abundance. There are far more known Magdalenian sites than Aurignacian, Gravettian or Solutrean ones, so a sign type in continuous use will accumulate most of its occurrences in the best-represented period whether or not anything changed. The share-of-total figure is therefore not the meaningful statistic.

The meaningful statistic is the **rate within each period** — the proportion of that period's known sites at which the sign occurs, which is the normalisation von Petzinger's own figures apply. On that measure the crosshatch occurs at roughly 3 per cent of Solutrean sites and roughly 30 per cent of Magdalenian sites, with intermediate values earlier. That is close to a tenfold increase in the rate of occurrence between adjacent periods, and it is not explained by site abundance, because site abundance is what the normalisation removes. These two rates are re-derived from the published proportions rather than read off a table in the 2009 thesis, and they have not been checked against it directly; that check should be made before the figure is leaned on.

**The concentration is real.** What it means is a separate question, and the chronological constraints of §2.4 govern how tightly it may be tied to anything external. The Solutrean and Magdalenian bracket the end of the Last Glacial Maximum, whose chronology is independently and very well constrained — Clark and colleagues bracket it to 26,500–19,000 years ago on 5,704 radiometric determinations — and the Franco-Cantabrian region functioned as a refugium into which populations compressed as ice advanced and out of which they expanded as it retreated. A rise in the rate of a mark type across that transition is a genuine observation and a genuine coincidence of timing. But the sign series is ninety-five per cent stylistically attributed, so the relationship can be stated at the resolution of *period*, not of century, and phrases implying tighter synchrony than that are not warranted.

What §3 establishes, then, is a substantial and firm foundation: a documented regional dataset with a continental extension, a dominant and long-neglected class of abstract marks, a bounded repertoire whose exact enumeration is conventional, a two-population distributional structure separating simple from complex forms, and a real change in occurrence rate concentrated around the end of the glacial maximum.

## 4. What the Natural Tier Can and Cannot Supply

The natural sciences have a great deal to say about the geometric signs, and what they say is well evidenced. This section states it at full strength, without qualification, because the two-tiered method depends on the natural tier being reported at its true weight rather than at a weight convenient to the reading that follows.

Three bodies of work bear on the corpus. Hodgson's neurovisual resonance theory accounts for why particular forms are produced and found salient. Changizi and colleagues supply a quantitative model predicting which structural configurations any sign system will converge on. Lewis-Williams and Dowson state, in the opening sentence of the paper that founded the entoptic debate, the limit beyond which none of this can go. Taken together they define a boundary with unusual precision: the natural tier accounts extremely well for **why these forms and not others**, and it has no method whatever for **what they meant**. §4.4 states where that boundary falls and what follows from it.

### 4.1 Entoptic and Neurovisual Accounts

Derek Hodgson has developed, across a series of papers from 2006 onward, an account he terms **neurovisual resonance theory**. Its claim is that the early visual cortex has evolved detectors tuned to specific low-level features — oriented edges, junctions, symmetries, parallel contours — and that marks exhibiting these features are disproportionately salient and disproportionately produced. Geometric marks are not, on this account, an early form of writing or a degraded lexicon; they are what happens when a visual system built to detect certain structures is given a surface and a marking implement.

The 2019 statement in the *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* summarises the position in its own highlights: "Neuroscans confirm relevance of early visual cortex to ancient geometric marks." "Cortical Visual Word Form area supports Neurovisual Resonance Theory." "Early engraved patterns derive from the same heuristics as letters." "Earliest geometric marks are proto-aesthetic rather than symbolic." "First geometric marks suggest neuronal recycling began in archaic hominins."

Two of these carry particular weight. The claim that the earliest marks are "proto-aesthetic rather than symbolic" locates the origin of the forms outside meaning altogether — they are produced because they are salient, before any question of what they carry arises. And the claim that engraved patterns "derive from the same heuristics as letters" explains the recurrence of specific forms across unrelated writing systems without any appeal to a common ancestor.

The theory is contested. Mellet and colleagues have challenged it, and Hodgson responded in 2020 in the same journal. It is presented here as a live position rather than a settled one.

The older entoptic hypothesis of Lewis-Williams and Dowson — that geometric signs derive from the phosphene forms generated by the visual system in altered states — belongs to the same family and has been debated for nearly forty years since its publication with open peer commentary in *Current Anthropology*. Its specific merits remain contested. Its structural contribution does not: it established that the human visual system generates a determinate, limited set of geometric forms independently of culture, and that any account of a geometric sign corpus must reckon with that generative substrate before invoking anything else.

One consequence should be stated plainly, because it is easy to elide. **A generative origin and a semantic function are not mutually exclusive.** A form may arise because the visual system produces it, and be recruited afterward to carry meaning; origin does not determine subsequent function. Alphabets are made of strokes the hand produces most readily, and they mean things. The neurovisual account explains the shape of the inventory. It does not, by itself, settle whether the inventory was used.

Two further accounts of the corpus should be recorded, since both bear on function rather than on form and neither is displaced by anything in this thesis.

The **proto-graphemic** proposal, associated with Marshack and taken up in a qualified form by von Petzinger herself, holds that the marks constitute a notational system carrying encoded information. Its strength is that it accounts for boundedness — notational systems have closed inventories — and for the systematic reuse of types. Its difficulty is the absence of the features that ordinarily identify notation: there is no demonstrated linear syntax, no evidence of combinatorial grammar, and no positional regularity of the kind writing systems exhibit. It remains a live proposal rather than a demonstrated one, and it is not in competition with anything argued here, since a reading of the sort §6 performs does not require the marks to have been notational and does not exclude it.

The **mnemonic** proposal, developed at length by Lynne Kelly, holds that the marks and the sites carrying them functioned as external anchors for orally transmitted knowledge — memory devices of the kind ethnographically attested in Australian and central African practice. Its strength is that it explains the association of marking with specific, revisited, hard-to-reach places. Its limitation is that it is an account of function and not of form: any distinctive set of marks could serve as anchors, so the model does not explain why this inventory. It sits comfortably alongside the argument of §5, and indeed supports it: an external memory anchor is by definition a thing built to hold what is no longer held internally.

### 4.2 The Generative Baseline

Hodgson's account is neurological and largely qualitative. There is a quantitative version, and it is the more consequential of the two because it makes a numerical prediction that has been tested.

Changizi, Zhang, Ye and Shimojo, writing in *The American Naturalist* in 2006, examined the topological structure of visual signs — the configurations of strokes and junctions — across more than one hundred writing systems, spanning non-linear scripts, and including symbol sets that have no historical relationship whatever. Their finding is in their title: "The Structures of Letters and Symbols throughout Human History Are Selected to Match Those Found in Objects in Natural Scenes." The distribution of junction types in human sign systems matches the distribution of junction types produced by opaque objects occluding one another in natural three-dimensional scenes.

The implication is precise. If sign inventories worldwide converge on a common structural distribution *because that distribution is what the visual system is optimised to parse*, then cross-cultural similarity in a mark inventory is the expected result under a null hypothesis of no contact and no shared meaning. Convergence is not a residue requiring explanation; it is the prediction.

This model supplies something the domain otherwise lacks: **a quantitative baseline.** Any claim that observed similarity between two sign inventories exceeds what independent generation would produce can, in principle, be measured against the Changizi distribution. That measurement has not been performed for the Paleolithic corpus, and performing it is a genuine and available research programme. §7.4 notes it as such.

The generative baseline also explains the two-population structure recorded in §3.4, and explains it economically. The forms that recur globally are the geometrically simplest — line, dot, crosshatch, ladder, hand stencil, open angle — and these are exactly the configurations the model predicts will be produced everywhere. The forms that do not recur are the complex ones: tectiforms, aviforms, claviforms, which are regionally bounded and, in the case of tectiforms, absent from the Australian corpus entirely. A transmitted inventory dispersing with a migrating population would be expected to shed elements stochastically. What the record instead shows is that the elements requiring the most specification are the ones that failed to travel, and the elements requiring the least are the ones found everywhere. That gradient is what a generative account predicts.

The honest statement is therefore this: **the simple geometric core requires no transmission to explain its distribution, and the complex forms are explained by ordinary cultural transmission within bounded regions.** No additional principle is needed to account for the shape or the spread of the inventory. Comparative surveys reporting overlap percentages between the European inventory and non-European assemblages do not change this, and in any case such percentages depend entirely on how types are individuated and matched — criteria that are rarely stated and that make the figures difficult to reproduce.

### 4.3 The Limit Stated in 1988

The paper that founded the entoptic debate — Lewis-Williams and Dowson, "The Signs of All Times: Entoptic Phenomena in Upper Palaeolithic Art," *Current Anthropology* 29:2 (1988) — opens with a methodological observation that has never been answered and is, on its own terms, unanswerable:

> "Elucidation of the geometric signs in Upper Palaeolithic art is hampered by an absence of directly relevant ethnography and by the logical impossibility of inducing meaning from numerical rock-art data."

Both halves hold. There is no descendant community whose testimony connects to Upper Paleolithic parietal practice; the interpretive bridge that anthropology relies on everywhere else does not exist here. And meaning cannot be induced from frequency and distribution data, because infinitely many meaning-assignments are consistent with any given distribution. Two thousand recorded crosshatches, perfectly mapped by site and period, constrain what the crosshatch meant not at all.

This is not a defect in the discipline's execution. It is a statement about what the available evidence can support, made by the researchers best placed to make it, and it applies with full force to every account of the signs — generative, mnemonic, proto-graphemic, shamanic, and correspondential alike. No amount of additional survey work removes it. The constraint is structural.

### 4.4 Where the Boundary Falls

The natural tier's position on the geometric signs can now be stated exactly, and it is a strong position on one side of a sharp line.

**On this side of the line**, the natural sciences supply a great deal. They explain why the inventory consists of these forms rather than others: the visual system generates and privileges a limited set of configurations, and those configurations match the junction statistics of natural scenes. They explain why simple forms recur across unrelated cultures without contact. They explain why complex forms are regionally bounded. They document, at high quality, what is on the walls, where, in what quantities, and — within the resolution limits of §2.4 — when. This is a substantial and largely secure body of knowledge, and nothing in this thesis contests any part of it.

**On the other side of the line**, the natural sciences supply nothing, and say so. Whether the marks carried meaning, what any of them meant, and whether the practice of marking expressed anything beyond itself are questions for which the discipline has no instrument. Hodgson's position is that the earliest marks are "proto-aesthetic rather than symbolic," which is a hypothesis about origin rather than a finding about function, and which he advances as such. Lewis-Williams and Dowson state the impossibility directly. The 1988 formulation is not a temporary gap awaiting better data; it is a limit on what this class of data can do.

That boundary is where the second tier begins, and the boundary determines what the second tier can be. A correspondential reading is not a competitor to the neurovisual account, because it is not an account of why the forms have the shapes they have. It operates entirely in the region the natural tier has declared inaccessible to it — and it operates there under the constraint stated in §2.2, that what it produces is a reading and not a decipherment. Nothing in §4 licenses a correspondential reading; nothing in §4 forecloses one either. The two tiers are addressing different questions, and §6 is written accordingly.

## 5. From Indicating to Constructing: The Documented Trajectory

The most substantial thing the material record supplies, and the thing that requires no decipherment whatever to observe, is a change in **the relationship between the sign and its support**.

Across the interval from the Upper Paleolithic to the emergence of writing, that relationship is transformed. Early symbolic activity places marks on surfaces that already exist and were not made for the purpose. Later symbolic activity manufactures the object that carries the meaning: quarries it, shapes it, moves it, stands it upright. Later still it encloses that object in built space. Later again it detaches the sign from any support at all, onto a tablet manufactured for nothing but carrying it.

The trajectory runs from **indicating** — pointing at meaning in what is given — to **constructing** — building a thing to hold meaning that is no longer found. It is visible in the material without any claim about what individual marks meant, and it is secure across tens of millennia, which means it survives any plausible revision of the parietal chronology discussed in §2.4.

### 5.1 Marking Found Surfaces

The defining characteristic of Upper Paleolithic parietal art is that the support is given. Caves were not excavated to be decorated; they existed, and people went into them and marked what was there. The same holds for portable art on bone, antler and stone: the material is found, and the working is applied to it.

More than that, the found surface is frequently *incorporated*. It is a commonplace of the literature that Paleolithic image-makers used natural relief, cracks, nodules and contours as constituents of the image — a bulge in the rock becomes the shoulder of a bison, a fissure becomes a back line. At Lascaux the horses of the main frieze stand with their hooves on a natural rock ledge that functions as ground. At El Castillo a tectiform sits beneath a natural crevice, with red dots above it. The wall is not a neutral substrate but a participant. This is the material signature of indication: meaning is being pointed out in what the cave already offered, not imposed on an indifferent medium.

Chronologically the practice is deep and geographically it is broad. Directly dated instances include the red disk at El Castillo, dated by uranium-series to at least approximately 40,800 years ago, and the Chauvet paintings, radiocarbon-dated to around 36,000 years ago — though Chauvet's dates have been disputed and should be reported as contested. Beyond Europe, the Sulawesi cave art dated by uranium-series to approximately 39,900 years ago establishes that the practice is not a European development. Earlier still, and outside parietal art proper, the engraved ochre from Blombos Cave in South Africa carries deliberate geometric incision at roughly 75,000 to 100,000 years ago.

These are reported here as direct dates, in accordance with §2.4, and they are the minority. The great majority of the parietal corpus — the material from which the sign inventory is drawn — is dated by stylistic attribution within the framework that von Petzinger and Nowell describe as circular. No argument below depends on the internal chronology of that corpus.

What matters for the trajectory is the invariant: for tens of thousands of years, across at least three continents, symbolic marking is applied to surfaces that were not made to receive it.

One further feature of the record deserves recording here, with its caveat attached. The European parietal tradition does not appear to begin crudely and improve. The earliest accepted material — Chauvet foremost — is already fully accomplished in draughtsmanship, perspective and the use of natural relief, with no securely dated sequence of simpler precursors leading up to it. The observation is genuine and is widely remarked. It is not decisive, because preservation is heavily biased against early open-air and portable work, and because engraved ochre and pigment use are attested far earlier and may constitute the developmental sequence in a medium that mostly did not survive. But to the extent the abruptness is real, it fits a tradition that is **transposing** an existing competence into a new medium better than one that is developing a competence within it. §7.2 returns to this.

#### A living system with no manufactured support

The stage described in this section — meaning carried by found landform, with nothing built to hold it — is not only an inference from the archaeological record or a posit of the doctrine. It is a documented human practice, and one of them is still running.

Australian songlines are sequences of sung country in which the content is keyed to specific landscape features traversed in order. The distinguishing property, for the purposes of this thesis, is not their antiquity but their **construction**: the mnemonic and its referent are the same object. No part of the system is manufactured. The land is the text, and singing the route is reading it. Several such traditions have been checked against independent physical evidence — Nunn and Reid compared Aboriginal accounts of coastlines and now-submerged land against bathymetric reconstructions of post-glacial sea-level rise and found agreement with configurations that ceased to exist some 7,000 or more years ago. The Klamath tradition describing the eruption sequence of Mount Mazama is frequently cited in the same connection; that case reaches this library at second hand and should be traced before it is leaned on.

What this is **not** is ethnographic evidence about Upper Palaeolithic Europe. The constraint stated in §4.3 stands in full: the traditions are unconnected, no testimony bridges to the painted caves, and nothing here licenses inferring what any European mark meant. The songline material is not being used as a parallel.

What it **is** is an existence proof for the mode. A reader could otherwise object, reasonably, that a symbolic system carried wholly by found landform with no constructed support is a doctrinal posit rather than a thing humans have been observed to do — and that §920's first stage is therefore unfalsifiable scene-setting. It is a thing humans have been observed to do. That establishes the availability of the mode and nothing beyond it, which is the whole of the claim made here.

### 5.2 Erecting Made Objects: Göbekli Tepe

Göbekli Tepe, in southeastern Anatolia, breaks the pattern completely, and it does so at the beginning of the Holocene — its enclosures radiocarbon-dated to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B, conventionally the tenth to ninth millennia BCE.

The site's T-shaped pillars are quarried from the local limestone bedrock, shaped, carved in relief, transported, and set upright in arrangements of standing stones. The largest exceed five metres and weigh several tonnes. Whatever they meant, they represent a categorical change in the relationship between the sign and its support. **The object carrying the meaning is now made.**

Two features of the site sharpen the point, and both are uncontested across the interpretive dispute.

The first is that the pillars stand in **open-air enclosures**. They are not marks in a pre-existing chamber; they are erected constructions in a place made to hold them. The excavation team's caution that some enclosures may have been roofed does not alter this — a roof is itself construction.

The second is that the labour is enormous and its purpose is not subsistence. Quarrying, carving, moving and raising multi-tonne monoliths requires organised, coordinated, sustained effort by numbers of people beyond a single band, and it produces nothing edible, defensible or portable. Whatever this was for, it was worth that.

It should be stated plainly what this section does and does not claim about Göbekli Tepe. It makes no claim about the reliefs at all — they are read in §6.3 and §6.4, under the terms set out in §2.2, and nothing in §5 depends on that reading. It makes no claim about astronomical function. It does not call the site a temple, a term the excavators themselves have moved away from. It claims only what is agreed by every party: that here, for the first time at scale, humans manufactured the objects that carry symbolic weight, rather than marking objects they found.

### 5.3 Building Enclosed Space: Çatalhöyük and After

The next stage in the sequence is the enclosure of symbolic activity within built domestic and ritual space, and Çatalhöyük on the Konya plain is its clearest exemplar. Radiocarbon-dated to roughly the eighth and seventh millennia BCE, the settlement consists of densely packed mudbrick houses, rebuilt repeatedly on the footprint of their predecessors, whose interiors carry wall paintings, relief modelling, and installations of animal remains — most distinctively the bucrania, the horned cattle skulls plastered and set into walls and benches.

Three characteristics distinguish this stage from the preceding one.

The symbolic object is now **installed in constructed interior space**. The bucranium is not a mark on a cave wall and not a monolith in an open enclosure; it is a made assembly fixed into a made building. The wall it is fixed to was built by the people who fixed it there.

The activity is **domestic and repetitive**. Where the Göbekli enclosures represent episodic, extraordinary effort, Çatalhöyük's symbolic installations are embedded in ordinary dwelling and are renewed as houses are rebuilt across generations. Symbolic practice has become routine and inheritable — attached to a place that is owned, maintained and passed on.

And the practice is **bounded**. Enclosure creates an inside and an outside. What was, in the parietal record, marked on surfaces available to anyone who entered the cave, is now situated within a household boundary.

The subsequent Neolithic and Chalcolithic sequence in the region continues in the same direction. Motifs that carried specific content in earlier assemblages recur in later ones — Halaf-period painted pottery is the standard example — in forms that are increasingly schematic and increasingly decorative, applied to portable goods produced in quantity. The form persists; the evidence that its makers knew what it was for diminishes. This library's [The Neolithic Transmission](The%20Neolithic%20Transmission_%20Iconographic%20and%20Taxonomic%20Continuity%20from%20G%C3%B6bekli%20Tepe%20to%20Early%20Dynastic%20Sumer.md) traces this continuity in detail and in the correct register, holding that the motifs carried "specific (though not necessarily recoverable) meaning."

### 5.4 Administering the Sign: Uruk

The support axis reaches its extreme at Uruk in southern Mesopotamia in the late fourth millennium BCE. What follows describes one development and not the state of a culture, and the distinction has to be held from the start, because the same city in the same period produced the Eanna precinct with its cone mosaics, the Warka Vase, the Warka Mask and the cylinder seals — none of them administrative, none of them detached from a made and meaningful support.

The archaic tablets from the Uruk IV and III levels constitute the earliest substantial body of writing. Their content is overwhelmingly **administrative**: quantities of grain, counts of livestock, allocations of labour and rations, lists of officials and commodities. The sign system that emerges into proto-cuneiform is a technology of accounting, and its immediate ancestry lies in the clay token and bulla systems used for recording transactions.

Three things have happened by this point, relative to the cave wall.

The sign has been **fully detached from its support**. It is no longer a mark on a meaningful surface, nor a carving on a made monument, nor an installation in a built room. It is an inscription on a portable, standardised, disposable clay tablet whose only function is to carry it.

The sign has been **standardised and made administrable**. It has a fixed inventory, a fixed set of values, a fixed direction of reading, and — critically — a training apparatus, the lexical lists, by which scribes were taught it. The system now requires professional maintenance.

And the sign has been **turned to accounting**. The first large-scale writing system is used to record who owes what to whom. This is not a criticism of Uruk; administrative writing is a formidable achievement and the foundation of the literate world. Nor is it a claim that anything was displaced. The tablet is an addition to Uruk's symbolic production and not a replacement of it, and a large part of the archaic archive is temple accounting — which makes the object an instrument of the cult rather than a successor to it.

Set out end to end, the support trajectory is this. A mark on a wall that was already there, incorporating the wall's own form. Then a monument quarried and raised, in a place cleared to receive it. Then an installation fixed into a built and inhabited interior. Then an inscription on a manufactured tablet, standardised and taught in schools. At each step the support is more made and less found; the meaning is more constructed and less indicated; the system is more explicit, more transmissible, more durable — and further from anything anyone simply saw.

That is a statement about supports, and it is the only thing §5 claims. It is not a statement about worship, and it is not a statement about how much correspondence a culture was carrying. §6.2 keeps the two apart, and §6.2 explains why they have to be kept apart.

§6 reads that sequence. It is worth stating once, before it does, that the sequence itself is not the reading. §5 is a description of the material record that an archaeologist who accepts no doctrinal claim in this library could endorse without amendment, and that is the condition under which the comparison in §6 is worth making at all.

## 6. The Correspondential Reading

This section applies the doctrine of correspondences to the trajectory set out in §5 and to two of the carved pillars at Göbekli Tepe. It does so under the terms declared in §2.2, which are restated here because they govern everything that follows: **what is offered is what emerges when the doctrine is applied. It is not a claim about what the makers intended, and no such claim is needed for the reading to be what it is.**

What the reading offers is a **structural comparison between two independently described sequences**: one set out in *Arcana Coelestia* in the mid-eighteenth century, one recovered from the material record in the twentieth and twenty-first. The comparison is worth making to the extent that the doctrinal sequence was not fitted to the archaeological one — and it demonstrably was not, since none of the archaeology existed as knowledge when the doctrine was written. §§6.3 and 6.4 then apply the method of §2.1 to two carved surfaces, and §6.5 states exactly what those readings rest on.

### 6.1 The Sequence in Arcana Coelestia 920

*AC* §920 is a single continuous passage, and its structure is an argument rather than a list. Swedenborg first describes perception, then its loss, then what was provided in its place.

The description of perception uses an example that turns out to matter:

> "The men of the Most Ancient Church had no other than internal worship, such as there is in heaven; for with them heaven was in communication with man, so that they made a one; and this communication was perception... in each object of sense they perceived something Divine and heavenly. For example, when they saw a high mountain, they perceived an idea, not of a mountain, but of elevation, and from elevation, of heaven and the Lord, from which it came to pass that the Lord was said to dwell in the highest... **and that afterwards the worship of the Lord was held on mountains**."

The final clause carries a whole history in eight words. First the mountain is *seen* — its meaning perceived directly, requiring nothing. Then, afterwards, worship is *held on* mountains: the perception has become a practice, attached to a place, requiring the place. The mountain has changed from something read into something used.

The loss is then described:

> "But when the church declined... and that perception or communication with heaven began to be lost, another state of things commenced. Then no longer did men perceive anything heavenly in the objects of the senses, as they had done before, but merely what is worldly, and this to an increasing extent in proportion to the loss of their perception... Thus was heaven separated from man." (*AC* §920[3])

And then the compensation, with its four named stages:

> "As the Lord foresaw that such would be the state of man, He provided for the preservation of the doctrinal things of faith... These doctrinal things consisted only in significative, and thus as it were enigmatical things, that is, in the significations of various objects on the face of the earth; such as that mountains signify celestial things, and the Lord... And as in these representatives they admired, and seemed to themselves even to behold, what is Divine and heavenly, and also because of the antiquity of the same, their worship from things like these was begun and was permitted, and **this was the origin of their worship upon mountains, and in groves in the midst of trees, and also of their pillars or statues in the open air, and at last of the altars and burnt-offerings which afterwards became the principal things of all worship**." (*AC* §920[4])

Four stages, in a stated order, with a hinge in the middle. Mountains and groves are found things: they exist independently of worship, and worship attaches itself to them. Pillars, statues and altars are made things: they exist because worship made them. The passage moves from indicating to constructing, and it says "at last" of the final stage — the altars and burnt-offerings that "afterwards became the principal things of all worship," which is to say that the most constructed stage became the most central.

**Source and inference key:**

| Natural object or practice | Status in this reading |
|---|---|
| High mountain | Elevation; and thence heaven and the Lord (*AC* §920[1], stated by Swedenborg) |
| Worship held *on* mountains | Historical transition stated by §920; "perception lapsed into located practice" is the thesis's inference from the before-and-after clauses |
| Grove in the midst of trees | A found living setting in §920's sequence; no independent object-level correspondence is claimed here |
| Pillar or statue in the open air | A made and erected object in §920's sequence; its role as fixed signification is inferred from the sequence, not stated as a correspondence for every pillar |
| Altar and burnt-offering | The final named practice in §920's sequence; organisation into repeatable procedure is the thesis's structural description |
| "The principal things of all worship" | Swedenborg's statement of the historical result, not a separate object correspondence |

### 6.2 The Mapping

Set the doctrinal sequence beside the archaeological one described in §5. Neither series was constructed with reference to the other.

| Stage | *AC* §920[4] | Character | Archaeological counterpart (§5) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Worship upon mountains | Found landform; meaning indicated in what is given | Marking of found cave surfaces, incorporating natural relief into the image | Found support; the wall participates |
| 2 | Worship in groves in the midst of trees | Found living setting; no construction | Continued parietal and portable marking on unmodified material across three continents | Found support; no manufacture |
| 3 | **Pillars or statues in the open air** | **Made object, erected** | **Göbekli Tepe: quarried, carved, transported T-pillars raised in open-air enclosures** | **Made support; monumental manufacture** |
| 4 | Altars and burnt-offerings, becoming "the principal things of all worship" | Built installation; ritual proceduralised and central | Çatalhöyük installations in built interiors; Neolithic altars; the sacrificial cult of the Bronze and Iron Ages, Uruk's own Eanna precinct among it | Built and enclosed; practice routinised — and continuing, not concluded |

The correspondence at stage three is the one that does the work, and it should be stated precisely so that it is not overstated. Swedenborg's third stage is "pillars or statues in the open air." Göbekli Tepe consists of carved monolithic pillars standing in open-air enclosures. The match is at the level of the *object type* and the *setting*, and it falls at the same position in the sequence — immediately after the found-thing stages and immediately before the altar stage.

**The fourth stage is not closed, and the tablet is not in the sequence.** Two errors are easy to make at this point, and earlier drafts of this thesis made both. The first is chronological. Altars and burnt-offerings do not end at Uruk. They run through the Bronze and Iron Ages and long past them, and Uruk's own Eanna precinct is a working instance of the stage, contemporary with the archive rather than superseded by it. §920 says the altars "afterwards became the principal things of all worship" — became, and remained. Stage four is the condition the sequence arrives at, not a phase it passes through on the way to something else.

The second error is categorical, and it is the more serious of the two. Every term in §920[4] is a form of worship: worship upon mountains, worship in groves, worship at pillars, worship at altars. An administrative tablet recording barley rations is not a form of worship. It therefore has no position in this sequence at all — not as a fifth stage, which the passage does not have, and not as the terminus of the fourth. The Uruk material belongs to §5's account of supports and stays there; it is not mapped onto §920 anywhere in what follows.

Three features of the match limit what can be inferred from it, and all three are stated here rather than in the Limitations section because they belong to the reading itself.

The match is **structural, not evidential**. It does not show that Swedenborg knew about Göbekli Tepe, which he did not, nor that the site is the Ancient Church, which is not claimed. It shows that a sequence he described and a sequence archaeology recovered have the same shape and the same hinge.

The match involves **no reading of the reliefs**. Nothing in §6.2 depends on what is carved on the pillars. It needs only that the pillars are made and raised, which is agreed by every party to every interpretive dispute about the site. The reliefs are read separately, in §§6.3 and 6.4, and those readings are independent of this one in both directions.

And the match is **not chronologically calibrated**. Swedenborg's churches are not periods with dates, and this thesis makes no attempt to assign them any. The comparison is of order, not of date, and any attempt to align the two series on an absolute timeline would reintroduce exactly the resolution problem §2.4 identified.

### 6.3 Reading Pillar 18: The Human Form Built

Pillar 18 stands at the centre of Enclosure D, one of a pair of monoliths some 5.5 metres tall. Four features of it are carved into the stone, visible in published photographs, and not in dispute among any party: the pillar is **T-shaped**, with a broad horizontal head above a narrow vertical shaft; it carries a **belt** in relief around the shaft, with a buckle and a hanging element; it carries **long arms** running down the sides, terminating in **hands whose fingers meet at the abdomen**; and animal reliefs are carved on the shaft, including a fox on the flank.

The composition read as a whole is a **standing human figure, abstracted to the point of anonymity and rendered at monumental scale in quarried stone.** This is not a contentious identification; the excavators themselves describe the T-pillars as anthropomorphic, on the strength of the arms, hands and belt.

Read correspondentially, and following the four steps of §2.1:

The **governing principle** is the human form itself. Everything else on the pillar modifies a body.

The **head** is the broad horizontal T-bar, and it bears no face, eyes or mouth. In the correspondential anatomy the head is the celestial, the highest and inmost part of the form: the will is seated in it, and the understanding is the clothing that clothes the will. The absence of facial features makes this interior unreadable and the figure anonymous; it does not establish that the interior itself is absent. What the construction does emphasise materially is the head's *presence and elevation*: it is the highest and heaviest part of the figure and the part that required the greatest effort to raise.

The **belt** girds the figure at the loins. Swedenborg defines a belt or girdle as a common bond: it gathers, encloses and holds inner things in connection on their lowest or outermost level, directing them toward one end (*AC* §9828; compare §9341). It therefore signifies ordering and conjunction, not a separation imposed between higher and lower regions. On this figure it binds the body's elements into one ordered human form at the level where they pass into outward action.

The **hands meeting at the abdomen** direct the arms inward and downward toward the girded centre. Hands and arms correspond to power received through truth grounded in good (*AC* §4932). Their posture modifies that power: it is gathered into the body and held at its ordered centre rather than extended outward in action.

The **animals on the shaft** modify the body they are carved upon by what they actually do. The fox is not given a fixed correspondence by the primary passages used here. Its alertness, subtle movement and life close to the ground permit a tentative reading as natural intelligence navigating the earthly plane. Those same capacities can serve prudence or deceit; the relief's presence on the flank does not by itself decide between them. The stronger reading of Pillar 18 therefore does not depend on fixing the fox to one moral sense.

Taken together the pillar reads as **the human form externalised**: a body built, in stone, at a scale no body has, with its inmost region elevated but left without distinguishing features, its outer elements gathered by a common bond, and its power held inward at the girded centre. The animals qualify that body without controlling the reading. Whatever else it is, it is a person made rather than a person perceived — and it stands at exactly the point in the sequence where §6.2 places the transition from found things to made things.

The formulation that follows from this reading, and that gives this thesis its title, is short: **the celestial person points to the mountain; the spiritual person builds a temple.** The mountain was there. The pillar had to be quarried.

### 6.4 Reading Pillar 43: A Sentence in Stone

Pillar 43 is the most iconographically dense object at the site. The elements read here are those carved on its broad face and visible in the standard published photographs: a **scorpion**, low on the surface; a **headless human figure**, with the phallus emphasised; a large **vulture**, with a wing raised and a **disk or sphere** balanced upon it; and further birds and geometric marks.

**The scorpion.** Read as an affection the scorpion is simple, and the readings Swedenborg provides are contextual readings from that affection. It is the **poisoning sensual self-love**, which has a sharp sting — thus reasoning from this affection when provoked, which is in its tail, its absolute ultimate. The several parts of that reading are not interchangeable. *AC* §195 describes the reasonings of the sensual as poison. *AR* §438 identifies the tail as the final extension of the head through the spinal column and applies that anatomy specifically to tails like those of scorpions; §439 identifies the stings in those tails as the falsifications by which the intellect is harmed. The sting is therefore reasoning *from this affection*, delivered from the absolute ultimate of the creature's head. It is not a free-standing definition of the scorpion as "persuasive falsity."

**The headless figure.** By step two, the head is the highest and inmost of the human form — the celestial, in which the will is seated and which the understanding clothes. A body without it is therefore not a body that has lost its judgment; it is a body from which the governing will, and everything that arises from it, has been taken. But the figure is not dead. The phallus is emphasised, and by function that is the organ of the natural generative life — the life that continues in the body and propagates. The reading is therefore not extinction but **severance with survival**: the inmost connection cut, while natural vitality persists and now runs from the loins rather than from above. What the anatomy states is the severance of the celestial — the will, together with the understanding that arises directly from it as its clothing — from the spiritual understanding below it, which in the Grand Man is the chest and not the head (§2.1, step two). *AC* §640 describes that severance, at the close of the Most Ancient Church, as a provision rather than a punishment:

> "When therefore the Lord foresaw that if man continued to be of such a nature he would perish eternally, He provided that the will should be separated from the understanding, and that man should be formed, not as before by a will of good, but through an understanding of truth should be endowed with charity, which appears as a will of good."

**The disk.** The surface carries a body whose head is gone and, above it, a round form held up on a wing. Read within the composition, the disk is the head. The alternative reading available in this library's earlier treatments — that the disk is the sun, and the sun the Divine Love — imports an object that is not on the surface in order to answer a question the surface has already posed, and it switches interpretive register partway through a reading otherwise conducted entirely by function. Step one requires the composition to be read as a statement before any part of it is read, and a composition that shows a head missing and a round form raised has supplied its own referent.

**The vulture.** A bird is the rational — "rational and intellectual things, of the internal man" (*AC* §40) — and the detailed reading proposed here is that the vulture descends into the ultimates to take out what should be taken out, the dead and the unclean. That describes how the image carries its meaning; the core of the correspondence within this composition is **preservation**. What dies when the celestial is severed is taken away and protected rather than lost.

The ancient sources support components of this reading, but they do not independently establish the assembled correspondence. Plutarch, reporting Herodorus of Heraclea, says that the vulture "touches no living thing" and "lives upon that which has been killed in some other way" (*Roman Questions* 93). He also says that it is rarely seen, that its young are difficult to find, and that soothsayers therefore regarded its appearance as a divine sending (*Romulus* 9.7). Horapollo says that it "sees more keenly than all other creatures," procures food from afar, and signifies compassion because of its care for its young (I.11). Neither source says that it was revered both for living on high and for caring for its young. Those source boundaries must remain distinct from the correspondential inference.

The further inference from those behaviours is that what has died is taken into the bird's care and nurtured there. The several movements do not each supply a separate meaning. Together they say that the deepest understanding which dies in the severance is preserved and prepared for new life. This is the proposed reading of what the creature does, not a statement made by Plutarch or Horapollo.

**What the preservation is for.** The final interpretive step is that taking the dead understanding away protects it from profanation. *AC* §641 does not state this correspondence and does not mention a vulture or removal. It supplies a doctrinal analogue: the things of the will "were as it were **covered over and reserved, lest anything should touch the will**. For if things of the will, that is of cupidity, had been excited, the man would have perished." The vulture is the element that makes the severance a protective provision rather than a simple loss: the deepest understanding that has died is reserved, nurtured and prepared for new life. The move from the doctrinal analogue to this reading is interpretive and should not be presented as though *AC* §641 stated it directly.

On that proposed reading, the surface produces a statement rather than a list:

> When life is governed from the lowest senses and that rule is defended by poisonous reasoning through its absolute power in the ultimates, the will is severed from the rest of the understanding, and the deepest understanding that comes from the intuition of the will dies. It is not lost: it is taken away, protected and nurtured for new life.

**A note on the sources just used.** The same ancient authors also read this bird augurally: Romulus and Remus count six against twelve in a contest over which of them founds the city (*Romulus* 9.4–5); Horapollo describes Egyptian kings sending observers to see which army the vultures faced (I.11); and Aelian describes vultures following armies by presentiment (*De Natura Animalium* II.46). These sources preserve several constructions of the bird, and their natural history is not reliable zoology. They are used here only for the specific behaviours they attribute to it, not as independent validation of the correspondential reading.

This is what emerges when the doctrine of correspondences is applied to the carved surface of Pillar 43. It is stated as such, under §2.2, and nothing further is claimed for it.

Two things about the reading are worth noting on their own terms.

The first is that it **uses every principal element, including the difficult one.** The emphasised phallus is not incidental to this reading and is not set aside by it; it is load-bearing, because the whole force of the headless figure depends on the contrast between the severed head and the persisting natural life. A reading that had to treat the phallus as noise would be a weaker reading of this object than one that requires it.

### 6.5 What This Reading Does Not Depend On

Because Pillar 43 has been the subject of a well-known archaeological controversy, it is worth stating explicitly what the reading in §6.4 rests on, and what it does not.

**It rests on:** the elements carved on the surface, as published and photographed. That is the whole of it.

**It does not rest on** the pillar's orientation, its azimuth, or its alignment with any horizon feature. It does not rest on the pillar standing in its original position — and the excavation team's observation that "many of the T-pillars... are not standing in their original positions," having been "recycled, i.e. pulled out and used elsewhere," leaves the reading untouched, because a carved surface carries its carving wherever the stone is set. It does not rest on the enclosure being unroofed. It does not rest on the radiocarbon date of Enclosure D, or on any date at all. It does not rest on identifying the animal figures with constellations, or on any assumption that Neolithic Anatolians recognised the asterisms of later Egyptian, Arabian or Greek astronomy. And it does not rest on the completeness of the excavation.

The distinction being drawn is the one set out in §2.2. An archaeoastronomical proposal — that the reliefs encode a datable configuration of the sky — is a historical claim about what the carvers did, and it stands or falls on precisely the grounds listed above. A correspondential reading makes no historical claim about the carvers at all. It says what the doctrine yields when applied to the surface. Evidence about position, roofing, stratigraphy and asterism-stability bears decisively on the first kind of claim and not at all on the second, because the second does not invoke any of it.

This also means the reading in §6.4 does not compete with the excavators' own interpretation of the iconography. Their reading — that Pre-Pottery Neolithic imagery is "especially concerned with articulation and disarticulation of the human body," and that severed heads and headless bodies together with necrophagous birds are "a well-known theme... rooted in a complex multiphase Pre-Pottery Neolithic mortuary ritual" — is an account of ritual practice, drawn from regional comparanda, and it is well founded. It is worth observing that the two accounts converge on the same subject matter: both take the pillar to concern the separation of the head from the body, and both take the vulture's role in that separation to be central. They differ in what they are accounts *of*. Theirs describes a practice. The reading in §6.4 describes what the doctrine yields when applied to the same surface. Nothing requires a choice between them.

### 6.6 Significative, Then Representative: The Loss of the Key

The reading has a further component, and it addresses what happens after the constructed stage is reached — the part of the sequence that §5.3 describes.

*AC* §1409[2] sets out a three-stage degradation with unusual precision:

> "The things thus signified were collected from their lips by their posterity and were formed by them into doctrinals, which were the Word of the Ancient Church... With the Ancient Church these were significative; for through them they learned internal things, and from them they thought of spiritual and celestial things. But when this knowledge began to perish, so that they did not know that such things were signified, and began to regard the terrestrial and worldly things as holy, and to worship them, with no thought of their signification, the same things were then made representative."

And in summary: "representatives had their rise from the significatives of the Ancient Church, and these from the celestial ideas of the Most Ancient Church."

Three states, then, in order: **celestial idea** — meaning perceived directly; **significative** — meaning known to be carried by a form; **representative** — the form retained and venerated after the knowledge of what it carries has perished. The crucial detail is the mechanism of the last transition. What is lost is not the form. The form survives intact, and is in fact *elevated*: "they began to regard the terrestrial and worldly things as holy, and to worship them, with no thought of their signification." Losing the key does not degrade the sign; it promotes the sign to the status of the thing it once signified.

This library tracks the same mechanism elsewhere under the name literalization, generally in the context of correspondential texts read as historical claims once their key was lost. *AC* §1409 states the mechanism for inherited representatives and significatives. Applying it to an archaeological graphic sequence is an extension made here, not a graphic example supplied by Swedenborg.

Read against §5, the sequence from Çatalhöyük through the Halaf assemblages supplies a possible archaeological correlate: motifs persist while becoming more schematic, more decorative and more widely applied to portable goods. Archaeology establishes the persistence and changing use of the form. It cannot establish from those facts alone that knowledge of the signification receded. *AC* §1409 supplies that as the hypothesis under which the formal sequence is read.

The proto-cuneiform sign inventory is not part of that sequence and is not attached to it here. Its ancestry is the clay token and bulla system — an accounting device from the beginning — and a grain-ration sign did not lose a correspondential key, because it never carried one. Joining the two would be an equivocation on the word *form*, treating the Halaf motif and the Uruk numeral as one lineage when they are two.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Motif retained while becoming schematic | Possible persistence of a significative as form after its internal reference is lost; the loss itself is not archaeologically observed |
| Motif applied decoratively to produced goods | Possible representative retained without its signification; decorative use alone does not establish veneration or loss of the key |

One feature of the §5 sequence bears directly on this mechanism and is worth naming, because it supplies the connection between the trajectory and the loss that the two sections otherwise leave implicit. Moving from found supports to made ones is also moving from **fixed** supports to **detachable** ones.

A mountain cannot be carried away. A grove can be felled but not relocated. A pillar can be moved, and at Göbekli Tepe was: the excavation team records that many of the T-pillars "are not standing in their original positions," having been "recycled, i.e. pulled out and used elsewhere." A clay tablet is portable by design; portability is the point of it. Detachability increases monotonically along the same sequence §5 tracks.

Detachability is not a precondition for a form to outlive its key. A fixed cave image can survive the community or practice that knew how to read it. What detachability changes is the range and manner of transmission: a portable form can circulate beyond the place, route and community that supplied its original context. Where the referent is the country and the sign is the country — the case described at the end of §5.1 — continued practice of the route continually renews the key, but abandonment can still sever it. A written tablet is the limiting case at the other end: a sign with no intrinsic relation to any support, able to travel wherever its carrier goes.

Detachability therefore facilitates decontextualisation and long transmission; it does not cause the degradation *AC* §1409 describes. It is equally what allows a text to outlive the culture that produced it. This library's [The Ancient Word: Philological Evidence for the Uruk-Ebla Origins of Genesis 1-11 and the Book of Job](../03_Biblical_Scholarship/The%20Ancient%20Word_%20Philological%20Evidence%20for%20the%20Uruk-Ebla%20Origins%20of%20Genesis%201-11%20and%20the%20Book%20of%20Job.md) argues that correspondentially dense material of the third millennium reached the Hebrew Bible by exactly this route, with the *toledot* colophons preserving the seams of the tablets it travelled on. A portable sign can lose its key; it can also carry what a fixed one could not. Nothing in the trajectory of §5 decides which occurs.

This is an observation about the sequence, not a doctrinal claim. Swedenborg names the stages and describes the loss; he does not connect them by this variable, and nothing here should be read as though he had.

One complication should be recorded rather than smoothed over. *Found* and *fixed* are not the same variable, and they come apart at the Azilian: after the Younger Dryas the great sanctuary caves were abandoned and marking shifted to small painted pebbles, which are found supports and maximally portable ones. On a found-to-made axis that is a step backward; on a fixed-to-detachable axis it is a step forward. The two axes co-vary across most of the sequence and there they do not, which is a reason to hold them apart rather than to merge them into a single story.

This has a bearing on the status of §§6.3 and 6.4, and it should be named rather than left implicit. Those readings are performed on forms whose key is not independently available. §1409 is precisely an account of what happens when a form outlives the knowledge of what it carried — and the honest position is that we stand downstream of that loss, not upstream of it. This is why §2.2 is worded as it is. A reading recovers what the doctrine yields; it does not recover what was in the carver's mind, and the doctrine's own account of how keys are lost is the reason it cannot.

### 6.7 Why This Is Not Declinism

Read carelessly, everything above describes a fall: perception lost, wisdom retreating outward, humanity ending up counting grain. That reading is available, it is emotionally satisfying, and Swedenborg's own text forbids it.

The passage that most fully describes the closing of the internal way ends on a claim that reverses its apparent direction:

> "Hence it was that this church which followed possessed an entirely different genius from that of the Most Ancient Church, and **if the Lord had not brought the human race into this genius, or into this state, no man could have been saved**." (*AC* §608)

The externalization is not a punishment and not a failure of will. It is the provision that made continuation possible when the prior mode had become unavailable. §920[4] uses the same framing: "As the Lord foresaw that such would be the state of man, **He provided** for the preservation of the doctrinal things of faith." The representatives are a rescue.

Three consequences follow for how this thesis reads the material, and each is a constraint on its own rhetoric.

**No stage is condemned, and no maker is diminished.** The people who quarried and raised the pillars at Göbekli Tepe are not, on this account, doing something lesser than the people who marked the walls at Chauvet. They are doing what remained available, with extraordinary effort and evident seriousness. The scribes at Uruk who standardised a sign system and taught it are not the endpoint of a decay; they preserved and transmitted a capacity that would otherwise have been lost with the generation holding it. Swedenborg's own comparison is instructive: the difference is "like that between the light of the sun by day, and the light of the moon and stars by night" (*AC* §4489[4]) — and moonlight is light. He adds, in the same passage, that "the good itself is the same in both, but the difference consists in seeing it in clearness or in obscurity."

**The sequence is not a moral trajectory.** It is an account of what happens to a communication channel when its interior end closes, and of what is supplied in compensation. Describing that as decline invites the reader to assign blame, and this library's standing practice is to describe structural moves and their costs without assigning guilt.

**And the direction is not necessarily irreversible on this account.** *AC* §1409 describes the preservation of form through the loss of signification; it does not describe recovery. The possibility of reading a retained form again belongs to the method of this thesis, under the limits stated in §2.2: the doctrine may disclose what the form yields without recovering what its maker consciously intended. The trajectory from indicating to constructing is therefore not treated as a closed door, but recovery should not be attributed to §1409 itself.

### 6.8 Placement in the Parietal Corpus

The readings in §§6.3 and 6.4 concern iconography: what a carved creature does, and what that yields when the doctrine is applied to it. The parietal sign corpus described in §3 does not admit that treatment, because a geometric sign does not *do* anything. A vulture scavenges; a zigzag has no behaviour to read.

But the corpus supports a different question, and it is a question the doctrine turns out to be unusually well suited to, for a reason that has not previously been drawn out in this library. The question is not what a sign depicts. It is **where the sign sits**.

#### Two axes, not one ordering

*Arcana Coelestia* speaks both of what is **inmost** and **outermost**, and of what is **high** and **low**. These relations can coincide, but they are not interchangeable wherever both are present. §3432[3] places them together in its account of one historical movement:

> "From this we can see how in succeeding time wisdom **retired from inmost things to outermost**; and that man had **removed himself from heaven, and had at last descended even to the dust of the earth**, wherein he now places wisdom."

In that passage wisdom's retirement from inmost to outermost is also a descent from heaven to dust. The two movements describe the same decline there. The sentence does not establish that physical depth and physical height are universally one coordinate, still less that a deep location in a cave must carry the same value as a high location on its wall. Discrete degrees are complete orders: a scene can move inward through degrees while retaining high and low relations within each degree.

The ordering is then stated in three registers, each with its derivation given rather than assumed.

**As elevation.** *AC* §920[1], quoted in §6.1 for a different purpose, gives it as a bare perception of height:

> "When they saw a high mountain, they perceived an idea, not of a mountain, but of **elevation**, and from elevation, of heaven and the Lord."

Not the mountain, and not a comparison to a mountain: elevation itself. In a composition, physical height can therefore carry elevation of state — often the elevation of rational or intellectual activity relative to what lies below — without determining how deeply into the composition's degrees the theme has proceeded.

**As interior and exterior.** A different relation runs the whole externalization thesis of §§5 and 6.2. The internal way closes and instruction proceeds thereafter "by the external way of sense" (*AC* §609); the Most Ancient Church perceived the heavenly *in* the objects of sense, which is the interior seen through the exterior (*AC* §920). In a cave, movement away from the entrance can carry this interior movement as depth. That depth can mark the degree at which a theme is being treated, or its continuation toward an interior conclusion. It does not assign one subject exclusively to one distance from the entrance.

**As habitat.** Three further passages give the ordering for living creatures, and give it by derivation. The first states the relation between the ancient usage and figurative speech:

> "The most ancient people **did not compare** all things in man to beasts and birds, **but so denominated them**... The sensuous things in man they called 'serpents' **because as serpents live close to the earth, so sensuous things are those next the body**." (*AC* §195)

The second gives birds and the creeping things of the waters different faculties:

> "By the 'creeping things which the waters bring forth' are signified the memory-knowledges which belong to the external man; by 'birds' in general, rational and intellectual things, of which the latter belong to the internal man." (*AC* §40)

The third gives beasts their own correspondence:

> "That 'beasts' signify man's affections — evil affections with the evil, and good affections with the good — is evident from numerous passages in the Word." (*AC* §46)

The first clause of §195 is a denial of metaphor: not comparison but denomination. Its second clause directly grounds the serpent's sensual correspondence in proximity to the ground and body. *AC* §40 directly places birds with rational and intellectual things of the internal man. These two passages support a local vertical relation: the serpent low, the bird elevated. They do not turn every creature into a term on one universal spatial ladder. *AC* §46 says that beasts signify affections; it does not assign them a middle altitude. Nor does water establish a position along a cave's entrance-to-depth axis. The correspondences remain functional and contextual rather than becoming a four-rung habitat table.

#### Why a cave

The cave is the right material because it carries both relations physically and at once, without reducing either to the other. Its floor and ceiling carry elevation. Its entrance and traversable depth carry a movement from exterior toward interior. A tablet carries neither relation intrinsically. A wall carries the first. A cave carries both.

That combination changes what repetition means. If a creature or sign establishes a theme, its appearance at several depths is not anomalous. The theme may be followed through successive degrees toward its conclusion, while its height relative to neighbouring figures continues to state the elevation at which it operates within each degree. Depth asks how interiorly the theme is being treated; height asks what is elevated or lowered within the treatment at that point. A full reading would therefore require sequence and local relation, not a global assignment of each sign type to one cave zone.

One consequence still follows before any sign is considered. The animals that dominate Palaeolithic cave art are beasts — bison, aurochs, horse, ibex, deer, mammoth — and *AC* §46 identifies beasts with affections. A body of imagery overwhelmingly concerned with beasts is therefore, under the doctrine, overwhelmingly concerned with affections. Nothing further about their altitude or depth follows merely from their being beasts.

#### What the ordering reaches

The sourced relations reach two sign forms provisionally identified as creatures, and they constrain **local vertical placement**, not cave depth. Where a form presents no creature, these passages are silent about its position. That does not make the form unreadable; it means this particular derivation supplies no placement rule for it.

| Form | Term presented | Position predicted | Bearing of the identification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Serpentiform | Serpent | Low on the local surface or below the figure it qualifies; no depth predicted | Rests on reading an undulating line as a serpent. If it is water instead, *AC* §40 supplies memory-knowledges of the external man but no local height rule |
| Aviform | Bird | High on the local surface or above the figure it qualifies; no depth predicted | Rests on reading the form as a bird in flight. This is a modern identification of an ambiguous shape and the prediction inherits its uncertainty in full |
| Penniform | Feather, or a projectile entering a beast | No secure position derived | The two identifications are both current in the literature and do not justify one placement rule |
| Tectiform | None — an enclosure, not a creature | Containment; no position derived | Leroi-Gourhan read these as traps or as huts; both are enclosures, so the enclosure reading is stable, but no habitat derivation follows and the scale gives no position |
| Cupule, spiral, quadrangle, cruciform, zigzag, and the remainder of the inventory | None | — | Outside what the habitat derivation reaches; no prediction is made about them |

Two forms carry a possible local vertical relation, and both identifications are uncertain. That is a narrow base and it should be stated as such before anything is built on it. Neither carries an entrance-to-depth prediction.

A note on the naming, since it bears on the middle rows. The terms in the standard typology — *aviform*, *tectiform*, *penniform*, *reniform* — are modern Latin coinages adopted by Breuil, Leroi-Gourhan and their successors to describe shapes to modern eyes, and were not offered by their coiners as identifications of subject matter. A name records how a form struck a twentieth-century describer; it is not evidence about what the form presents, and the identifications above are made on the shape rather than on the label.

#### What the natural tier explains, and what it does not

§4.2 sets out the generative baseline: the recurrence of simple geometric forms across unconnected populations follows from the contour statistics of natural scenes and from the response properties of early visual cortex. That account is strong, and this thesis accepts it. It explains why zigzags recur for thirty thousand years without any transmitted vocabulary.

It explains nothing whatever about **position**. Junction statistics predict that undulating lines will be produced; they do not predict that undulating lines will be placed near the floor. Neurovisual resonance predicts which forms are salient; it does not predict which register of a composition a given form occupies.

The transmission account has the mirrored limitation. It explains regional clustering — tectiforms in the Dordogne, aviforms in Quercy and the Charente (§3.4). It does not explain why a Dordogne tectiform and a morphologically distinct Cantabrian "Spanish tectiform" would occupy the same position relative to a composition, since on a transmission account those are different words in different languages.

It should be said that the premise underneath all of this — that where a sign sits carries value — is not a doctrinal assumption imported for the occasion. It is a finding of the archaeological literature, arrived at without any reference to correspondence. Sauvet and colleagues, working on the Cantabrian quadrilateral signs, argue that the signs function as **modifiers rather than labels**: a sign's value depends on what it is placed in relation to, so that a mark on an animal's dorsal line is not doing the same work as the same mark on a belly or a hoof. Clottes and Lewis-Williams, from a very different theoretical position, describe the cave wall as a participating surface rather than a neutral one, with marks sited deliberately against cracks, fissures and concavities — so that a line entering a fissure is doing something a line beside it is not. Neither account is correspondential and neither supports the ordering proposed here. Both establish the thing that has to be true before any placement argument can be made at all: that placement was operative, and that the makers were siting marks with reference to what surrounded them.

Local relational placement is therefore a variable on which the two standing natural-tier accounts are largely silent. Cave depth is a different variable and cannot be folded into it.

#### The proposition that can be checked

The corrected proposition is relational rather than zonal. Where a form can be identified with enough confidence to carry a sourced vertical correspondence, its height relative to the figure or local composition it qualifies should accord with that correspondence: rational and intellectual activity elevated, the sensual close to the ground or body. No prediction follows that aviforms should occur only in deep chambers or serpentiforms only near entrances.

Depth carries another question. If a theme is sustained through a cave, recurrence at successive depths may show that the same subject is being followed through deeper degrees or toward completion. Testing that requires the order of panels, repeated associations, and changes in the theme as the route proceeds. A frequency table assigning each sign type to one depth zone would miss the proposed relation rather than test it.

One objection has to be met here rather than deferred, because §4.3 appears to forbid exactly this. Lewis-Williams and Dowson state "the logical impossibility of inducing meaning from numerical rock-art data," and a prediction about sign distributions is a claim about numerical rock-art data. The limit they state is real and this thesis accepts it in full. But it is a limit on a direction of inference. What is impossible is to begin from counts and positions and *induce* from them what the marks meant — because infinitely many meaning-assignments fit any distribution. Nothing here proceeds that way. The correspondences are fixed in advance, from *AC* §§40, 46, 195, 920 and 3432, without reference to any cave; the distribution is then consulted to see whether it accords with them. That is deduction followed by a check, and the check can fail. Where the 1988 limit bites is on what a favourable result would mean: a distribution that accorded with the ordering would still not establish that the marks meant that, and §2.2 already declines to claim it would.

#### The evidence, and what it is worth

Leroi-Gourhan's survey remains the only systematic positional analysis of the French corpus at scale, and the structure it recovered is commonly summarised as centro-peripheral: enclosed forms concentrated in central panels with bison and aurochs, linear forms at entrances, passages and terminal fissures. That description has not been checked here against *Treasures of Prehistoric Art* directly and remains flagged. More importantly, it does not test the distinction just drawn. The appearance of related signs at both entrances and terminal fissures does not contradict a reading in which a theme can recur through several depths. His "central panel" is also a compositional category rather than a measured distance from the entrance. The distribution neither confirms nor refutes the corrected proposition.

**One correspondence applies without fixing a position.** Beasts dominate the corpus, and *AC* §46 identifies beasts with affections. That tells us the governing subject under the doctrine; it does not make beasts a middle spatial term or assign them to central panels.

**The panel-level cases cut both ways, and the count is small enough that they should all be given.**

*Relevant to local elevation.* The Axial Gallery at Lascaux uses the rock's own horizontal ledge as a ground line: horses stand with their hooves on it and painted marks occupy the wall above. This establishes a deliberate local vertical register, but the marks are not identified by the sourced correspondences above, so it does not by itself decode the register. An aviform is reported directly above and to the left of the head of the pierced human figure at Pech Merle, which accords with a rational or intellectual form elevated relative to the figure it qualifies. Serpentiforms are reported near ground level, but that report must be traced before use because no citation for it has been located in von Petzinger's text or a site monograph.

*Relevant to depth and thematic continuation.* At El Castillo, triangles and enclosed forms are reported low on walls in deep and constricted galleries. The scale derives no fixed elevation for a triangle and no fixed cave depth for any of these forms, so the case is neither confirmation nor contradiction. The Spotted Horse panel at Pech Merle carries dots above, below and within the horses. That distribution can define and permeate a theme rather than occupy one register; it is not adverse merely because the same mark crosses vertical positions. At Chauvet, the composite Sorcerer is painted on a pendant from the ceiling. A beast has no assigned middle altitude under *AC* §46, so its elevated position creates no contradiction.

*Neither for nor against.* At El Castillo, the "Corner of Tectiforms" has complex tectiforms painted below a prominent natural horizontal crevice with clouds of red dots above it. That establishes that registers were operative and that a natural rock feature was used to divide them — which is a real finding — but not which term occupies which, since the scale derives no position for an enclosure. At Font-de-Gaume, tectiforms are superimposed on the bodies of bison. That is adverse to any account placing tectiforms in a lower register, and it bears on nothing here for the same reason: the scale derives no position for an enclosure either way.

Four or five panels for, two against, out of a corpus of thousands, all of them selected because they are the ones everybody discusses. The selection hazard is the governing fact about this paragraph.

*One methodological flag on the sources.* Several of these placements reach this thesis through web-sourced summaries rather than through the site monographs. The Pech Merle aviform is cited to Lorblanchet's 2010 *Art Pariétal: Grottes Ornées du Quercy* and the Le Placard concentration to Clottes and Lewis-Williams, but neither has been checked against the publication here. These should be verified at the monograph before any of them is leaned on.

**The dataset the proposition needs has never been compiled.** There is no systematic record of sign type against local height and relation to neighbouring figures. Nor is there a route-based dataset tracking repeated themes from entrance through successive chambers. These are two different datasets because they concern two different axes. The present evidence is illustrative and selected, not a test at scale.

**A natural-tier account of local elevation is available.** The association of the elevated with valued or abstract activity, and the low with bodily or base activity, is documented across unrelated language families and is generally derived from embodiment. This thesis does not claim to discriminate between that account and correspondence. Under §2.3 they may describe the same fact at different tiers. The natural-tier literature does not, however, justify equating that vertical relation with increasing cave depth.

#### Status

Under §2.2 this remains a reading and claims nothing about what any Palaeolithic maker intended. The earlier monotonic prediction was malformed because it required height and depth to agree as though they were one axis. Once they are distinguished, the centro-peripheral distribution is no longer an adverse result, and repeated signs at multiple depths are not an anomaly requiring explanation.

What remains answerable is narrower and more exact. A local-placement study can test whether forms securely identified with rational or sensual functions occupy corresponding vertical relations within panels. A route-based study can ask whether repeated themes develop as the cave is traversed toward deeper chambers. Until those distinct studies exist, §6.8 identifies a grammar of placement and thematic depth; it does not establish a cave-wide gradient.

### 6.9 Reading the Lascaux Shaft Scene: The Same Subject on a Found Surface

Every reading performed so far in this section has been performed on a manufactured object. That is a defect in the thesis as it stands, because the argument turns on a hinge between found supports and made ones, and only one side of the hinge has been read. The Shaft Scene at Lascaux supplies the other side.

The scene lies at the bottom of a shaft some five metres deep, in the least accessible part of the cave — on the inmost/outermost axis of §6.8, the inmost point reachable. Its elements are photographed, published and uncontested as to what is present: a **bison**, pierced by a long line and with its entrails spilling from an opened belly; a **human figure with the head of a bird**, fallen, horizontal, ithyphallic, and with both hands splayed open; a **bird set on top of a staff or pole**, set into the ground and standing upright on its own base, clear of the other figures; and a large animal moving away to the left, conventionally identified as a woolly rhinoceros. The last is sometimes held to be a separate composition on grounds of technique, and the reading below is constructed so as not to require it.

Read by the four steps of §2.1:

The **governing principle** is a cause standing apart from its effects. This is not a hunting scene and not a narrative of pursuit, and the composition does not join its elements by contact. The staff stands by itself, set into the ground and touching nothing. The bison and the man are joined to each other — the bison wounded, the man going down before it. What the surface sets out is one thing established and standing, and, separately from it, what follows from it.

The **staff** is the standing term, and it is read first because the composition gives it that place. A staff signifies power, and Swedenborg supplies the rule for reading any particular one: "it is a support; for it supports the hand and arm, and through them the whole body; wherefore **a staff takes on the signification of the part which it immediately supports**" (*AC* §4876). A staff is therefore not a symbol with a fixed value but a variable that takes the value of what it holds up. In the Mosaic instances it holds up the hand and arm and so signifies the power of truth; here it holds up a bird, and birds signify "rational and intellectual things" (*AC* §40). What it states is power from the rational — and Swedenborg names that case directly in glossing Isaiah's Asshur, where staff "denotes power from reasoning and memory-knowledge, such as is the power of those who reason from memory-knowledges against the truths of faith, and either pervert them or make them of no account."

Two features of how it stands complete the reading, and both are in the composition rather than supplied to it. It is **set into the ground** — planted in the lowest place, in ultimates. And it **stands upright on its own**, in no one's hand, unattached to either figure. A power from the rational, established in the lowest place and holding there by itself, is a **ruling principle in ultimates**. That is the cause the composition sets down.

It is also, unavoidably, **made**. A staff is not found standing; it is cut, shaped and set upright, and §4876 classes it as the instrumental rather than the principal. That is this document's subject appearing inside a single element of a Palaeolithic composition, on a found wall, some seven thousand years before anyone quarried a pillar.

What follows stands apart from it, and is its effect.

The **bison** is a beast, and beasts signify affections (*AC* §46) — here an affection of the natural, and its good. It is pierced, and what the piercing produces is an opened belly with the interior coming out: the containing structure breached and what it held spilled onto the ground. What the element states is **the natural good wounded**.

The **bird-headed man** places a bird where a head should be. The head is the celestial, in which the will is seated and which the understanding clothes (§2.1, step two), and the bird is again the rational. A human figure whose head is a bird is a form whose governing seat is occupied from a lower degree — the clothing standing in the place of what it was made to clothe. And he is going down. That is the second half of the effect: under a ruling principle taken from the rational and fixed in ultimates, the natural good is wounded, and **the intellect falls with it**. He is not felled by the staff; nothing in the composition connects them, and the staff is in no one's hand. He falls before the wounded beast.

His posture carries that without needing any correspondence at all: the staff stands, the bison stands, and the man is horizontal. And, as on Pillar 43, he is ithyphallic — the natural generative life persists in a body whose head no longer performs its office. The two compositions state that persistence the same way and the loss differently: at Göbekli Tepe the head is absent and is being carried away, here it has been occupied.

The **departing animal**, if it belongs to the scene, is read by what a rhinoceros actually does: enormous physical force, armoured hide, notoriously poor sight, solitary movement. That is sensory and natural power — massive, nearly impervious, and nearly blind — leaving the scene.

> A power from the rational stands planted in the lowest place, upright and in no one's hand: a ruling principle in ultimates. Apart from it, and following from it, the natural good is opened and wounded, and the intellect goes down before it. And the great sensual power moves away.

**The parallel with Pillar 43, and what it is worth.** The two compositions have the same subject. Pillar 43 gives a governing life severed from a body that goes on living from the loins; the Shaft Scene gives a ruling principle standing in ultimates, with the natural good wounded under it and the intellect going down, in a body that is likewise still ithyphallic. Both make the persistence of natural generative life explicit, and in both it is that persistence which makes the severance legible rather than merely fatal. One is painted on a wall that was already there, at the deepest point of a cave. The other is carved on limestone that was quarried, dressed, moved and raised in the open air, some seven millennia later. Same subject; opposite sides of the hinge.

What that parallel is worth has to be stated exactly, because it is easy to overstate. It is a convergence between **two readings**, not between two established meanings, and both readings were produced by the same method from the same doctrine. A method applied consistently to related subject matter will tend to produce related results; that is what consistency means, and it is not independent corroboration. The observation that survives the caveat is narrower and still worth having: **the content the doctrine finds does not change across the hinge, while the support does.** On the doctrine's own account in *AC* §920 that is exactly what should happen — the mountain and the pillar carry the same thing, and what changes is that one was found and the other had to be built.

**Correspondential key:**

| Natural object | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| Bird fixed on an upright staff, set in the ground and standing alone | **The cause**: a ruling principle in ultimates — power from the rational (*AC* §40), a staff taking the signification of what it supports (*AC* §4876), planted in the lowest place and holding there on its own |
| Bison, pierced, entrails spilling | The natural good wounded — the containing structure breached and what it held spilled |
| Bird in place of a human head, the figure horizontal | The intellect fallen: the rational had taken the seat of the celestial, and it goes down with the good that was wounded |
| Ithyphallic figure | Natural generative life persisting through the loss (cf. §6.4) |
| Rhinoceros departing | Sensual-natural power — massive, armoured, near-sighted — withdrawing |
| Position at the bottom of the shaft | The inmost point of the cave on the depth axis (§6.8) |

## 7. Discussion

The two tiers have now done their separate work. This section states what each contributed, what kind of claim the whole amounts to, and what its limits are.

### 7.1 What the Archaeology Contributes

The archaeological tier contributes three things to this argument and declines to contribute a fourth.

What it declines is a **lexicon**. There is no method available for recovering what the geometric signs meant, and the researchers closest to the material say so directly. This is not a gap that better fieldwork will close; §4.3 argued that the constraint is structural. Any account that requires the signs to have been read — by anyone, on any framework — is asking the archaeology for something it does not have.

What it contributes, first, is **the neglected majority**. Geometric signs tend to outnumber figurative images in the French corpus, and a century of scholarship attended to the animals. Whatever the marks are, there are more of them than of the images everyone was looking at, and their systematic recording is recent. That is a real finding about the shape of the evidence and about the discipline that assembled it.

Second, **a bounded, persistent, regionally structured inventory**. A limited set of mark types recurs across a long span, and some of them cluster geographically in ways that demonstrate transmission within communities. The two-population structure — simple forms everywhere, complex forms locally — is well attested and economically explained.

Third, and load-bearing for this thesis, **the trajectory**. The shift from marking found surfaces to manufacturing the objects that carry meaning is documented, uncontested across every interpretive dispute about the sites involved, and secure across tens of millennia. It survives the chronological limits of §2.4 entirely, because it does not depend on the internal ordering of the Paleolithic material at all.

There is a fourth contribution that is easy to overlook: the archaeology **constrains the reading**. §§3–5 were described independently and include findings that give the doctrinal reading no help — the generative account of the simple forms, the regional restriction of the complex ones, the impossibility of inducing meaning from distribution data. Those findings stayed in. That is what makes §6 a comparison rather than a projection.

### 7.2 What the Doctrine Contributes

The doctrinal tier contributes something the archaeology cannot supply on its own: **a causal interpretation of why the trajectory runs in that direction**.

Archaeology can establish that symbolic practice moved from found supports to made ones. It has no resources for explaining why, beyond the standard functional narrative — increasing population density, sedentism, surplus, social complexity, and the organisational demands these create. That narrative is not wrong and this thesis does not contest it. But it is an account of *enabling conditions*, not of motive. It explains how monument-building became possible; it does not explain why anyone wanted to raise multi-tonne pillars in an open enclosure at the cost of enormous unrewarded labour, and it is notably silent on the fact that Göbekli Tepe precedes agriculture rather than following it.

*AC* §920 supplies the doctrinal cause, and the resulting reading inverts the standard account. Construction is not what capability produces when it becomes sufficient; it is what is supplied when perception fails. Applied to the archaeological trajectory, the pillar is raised because the mountain can no longer be read — because the meaning that was once seen in a found thing must now be built into a made one. This is the thesis's causal reading of the sequence, not evidence about the conscious motive of Göbekli Tepe's builders. Within that reading, the labour is the cost of a substitution rather than the expression of a new power.

The doctrine contributes a second thing, which is a mechanism by which inherited forms can survive their key. *AC* §1409's significative-then-representative sequence says that forms are not discarded when knowledge of what they signify perishes; they can instead be *elevated* and treated as holy in themselves. It does not predict increasing formal fidelity, and the Neolithic-to-Halaf sequence does not disclose whether its makers retained or lost a signification. The persistence and changing use of motifs supply a possible material analogue of the mechanism, not evidence that the loss occurred.

And it contributes a corrective to its own tone, discussed in §6.7. The framing is providential rather than declinist: "if the Lord had not brought the human race into this genius, or into this state, no man could have been saved."

There is a third contribution, and §§6.3 and 6.4 are what it looks like. The doctrine supplies **a method for reading a carved surface** — not a method for establishing what its carvers meant, which no method supplies, but a disciplined procedure that takes the elements actually present, reads each by its own function, and asks whether they compose. What comes out of that procedure on Pillar 43 is a statement about life governed from the lowest senses and defended by poisonous reasoning in the ultimates, the will severed from the rest of the understanding, and the deepest understanding that dies in that severance being protected and prepared for new life rather than lost. Whether anything corresponding to that was in anyone's mind in the tenth millennium BCE is not established and is not claimed. What is established is that the doctrine, applied under stated constraints to elements nobody disputes are present, yields that and not something else.

There is a fourth, and it is different in kind from the other three. §6.8 distinguishes two positional relations that the earlier formulation had collapsed. *AC* §195 grounds the serpent's sensual correspondence in its movement close to the earth, while *AC* §40 gives birds as rational and intellectual things of the internal man. These passages support a local vertical relation between what is close to the body and what is elevated in thought. Cave depth carries another relation: movement from exterior toward interior, through which one theme may be followed across deeper degrees. *AC* §3432[3] joins outward movement and descent in one historical process, but it does not make height and depth interchangeable coordinates wherever a composition supplies both. The result is a narrower placement proposition and a more exact account of recurrence: beasts identify the governing subject as affections under *AC* §46 without occupying a universal middle altitude, and a sign appearing at several cave depths may articulate one theme through its sequence rather than violate a zonal rule.

### 7.3 The Status of the Result

It is worth stating plainly what kind of thing this thesis has produced, because it is not one kind of thing but two, and they have different standing.

**The structural comparison in §6.2 is a claim about order, and order can be wrong.** *AC* §920[4] specifies four stages in a stated sequence with a hinge between the found and the made. The material record independently exhibits a sequence of the same shape with the hinge in the same place. That comparison had a way of failing: had monumental construction preceded found-surface marking, or had large-scale enclosed ritual architecture preceded Göbekli Tepe, the mapping would have broken. It did not break. This is a modest result and it is a real one.

Two features give it more weight than a bare coincidence. The doctrinal sequence was **specified before the archaeology existed as knowledge** — *AC* §920 was published in 1749, Paleolithic parietal art was not accepted as authentic until after 1902, and Göbekli Tepe was not excavated until 1995. And the comparison **uses no contested archaeology**: it turns on nothing the excavators dispute, on no reading of any relief, and on no date within the parietal corpus.

**The readings in §§6.3 and 6.4 are a different kind of result, and §2.2 already stated which kind.** They are what the doctrine yields when applied under stated constraints to elements that are present on the stone. They are not historical claims about the carvers, and they neither gain nor lose standing from evidence about position, date, roofing or asterism. Asking whether they are "confirmed" is asking a question they were not framed to answer; asking whether they are performed consistently, use the whole composition, and hold together in one register is the question that applies, and it can be checked by anyone with the same doctrine and the same photographs.

For the framework more broadly, one observation follows and may generalise. A doctrinal claim stated **lexically** — this form means that spiritual thing, in the makers' minds — asks the archaeology for something it does not have and cannot be assessed. The same doctrinal material stated **structurally** — this ordered sequence has this shape and this hinge — is coarser but can be checked against a record that exists. It is at least worth asking, elsewhere in this library, whether claims currently made in the first form would be stronger in the second.

What would extend the structural result? Specifying the mapping in advance for a sequence not yet excavated — stating, before the evidence exists, what stage-three material at a given site should look like — and then seeing whether it holds. That is a genuine programme and a slow one.

What can be tested on the archaeological tier is more immediate, and §4.2 named the first item: measuring the sign inventory against the Changizi contour-frequency baseline, which no one has yet done for this corpus. Beyond it sit two distinct positional datasets that the excavation literature does not currently provide — local height and relation within panels, and route-based recurrence and transformation from entrance through deeper chambers — together with the chronometric programme that von Petzinger and Nowell themselves recommend to replace stylistic dating. None of these requires the doctrinal framework to be true or false.

### 7.4 Limitations and Lacunae

The material is sorted here into what is established, what is interpretive but internally consistent, and what is genuinely open.

**Established, independent of any doctrinal commitment.** That geometric signs outnumber figurative images in the French corpus. That a bounded inventory of mark types recurs across a very long span. That tectiforms cluster in the Dordogne and aviforms in Quercy and the Charente. That simple forms are globally distributed and complex forms are regional. That placement was operative — that marks were sited with reference to the figures and rock features around them. That Göbekli Tepe's T-pillars were quarried, carved, transported and erected in open-air enclosures before agriculture. That Pillar 18 carries a belt, arms and hands, and Pillar 43 a scorpion, a headless ithyphallic figure, and a vulture with a disk. That the Lascaux Shaft Scene contains a pierced bison with spilling entrails, a fallen ithyphallic bird-headed figure, and a bird on a staff. That the support of symbolic activity moves from found to made across the sequence. A reader who grants nothing else in this thesis can grant all of this.

**Interpretive but internally consistent.** The correspondential readings in §§6.3, 6.4 and 6.9, and the stage mapping in §6.2. These are produced by a stated method from stated elements, and they can be checked for consistency — but they are readings in the sense §2.2 defines, and they are not evidence for the doctrine that produces them. The convergence between §6.4 and §6.9 is a convergence between two applications of one method, not independent corroboration, and §6.9 says so.

**Genuinely open, and the honest list is long.**

*The method has no clean miss.* This is the sharpest limitation: the procedure lacks a mechanism that would produce an unambiguous failure on any single mapping. A reading that does not resolve can generally be made to resolve by taking a different sense of an element. The constraints in §2.1 — read the whole composition, read each element by its actual function, hold one register throughout — are real constraints and they do rule things out, but they do not amount to a falsification test on an individual reading, and no claim is made here that they do.

*Stage two of the mapping is loose.* Swedenborg's "groves in the midst of trees" has no clean archaeological counterpart. §6.2 assigns it to continued found-surface marking, which is a defensible reading of "found living setting" but is not compelled by the text. Stages one, three and four are considerably tighter, and the mapping should be assessed on those.

*The stage-three match may be less unusual than it looks.* Standing stones are a widespread human practice, and "pillars or statues in the open air" describes menhirs, stelae, herms and much else. That the earliest monumental instance falls at the sequence position the doctrine specifies is the substance of the claim; the object type by itself is common enough that the match should not be treated as improbable on its own.

*The archaeological tier is coarse by necessity.* §2.4 established that ninety-five per cent of French parietal art is dated stylistically within a framework its own compiler calls circular. This thesis built on a sequence coarse enough to survive that, at a cost: it can say nothing about the internal development of Paleolithic sign use.

*Some inherited figures are unverified.* Specific numbers circulating in this library — twelve aviforms at Le Placard, a Bernifal tectiform composed from more than five hundred dots, claviforms at sixteen per cent of French sites, penniforms at twenty-five per cent — have not been checked against primary site reports. None is used here. They should be verified or removed where they appear.

*The functional account is not refuted.* That monumentality follows from population aggregation, sedentism and organisational capacity explains the same sequence without any appeal to lost perception. §7.2 holds that it accounts for enabling conditions rather than motive; a reader may reasonably hold that motive is not something archaeology needs to supply.

*§6.8's positional proposition is untested.* The earlier version predicted a monotonic gradient because it treated height and cave depth as interchangeable expressions of one ordering. That premise was incorrect. The corrected account distinguishes local elevation within a panel from movement through interior degrees along the cave's depth. Leroi-Gourhan's centro-peripheral distribution therefore does not test it, and the recurrence of a sign class at both entrances and terminal fissures is not adverse. The required local-relational and route-sequence datasets have not been compiled.

*What is not concluded.* That the geometric signs have been read. That they constitute a semantic system. That the Ancient Word is materially attested in the Paleolithic record. That Göbekli Tepe records an event or a date. That the makers of Pillar 43 intended what §6.4 reads there. What is concluded is narrower and holds: that the trajectory from indicating to constructing is documented; that it has the same shape and the same hinge as a sequence written in 1749 by someone who could not have known any of it; and that the doctrine applied to two carved surfaces yields readings that compose.

## 8. Conclusion: The Mountain and the Pillar

Across the interval from the Upper Paleolithic to Uruk, the relationship between the sign and its support is transformed. Marks are placed on cave walls that were already there, incorporating the rock's own contours into the image. Then, at Göbekli Tepe, limestone is quarried, carved, transported and raised into standing pillars in open-air enclosures — the object carrying the meaning is now made. Then at Çatalhöyük the symbolic installation is fixed into a built and inhabited interior, renewed across generations. Then at Uruk writing detaches the sign from its support altogether, standardised and taught in schools. At each step the support is more made and less found; the meaning is more constructed and less indicated; the system is more explicit, more durable, more transmissible — and further from anything anyone simply saw.

In 1749, describing what was provided to the Ancient Church after direct perception failed, Swedenborg named four things in order: worship upon mountains, and in groves in the midst of trees, and pillars or statues in the open air, and at last altars and burnt-offerings, which afterwards became the principal things of all worship. Mountains and groves are found. Pillars and altars are made. The hinge falls between the second stage and the third, and the third stage is manufactured monoliths standing in the open air. Paleolithic parietal art was not accepted as authentic until after 1902. Göbekli Tepe was not excavated until 1995.

Those are two different sequences and the thesis does not merge them. The first is about supports and runs to the tablet. The second is about worship and stops at the altar — where it has stayed, altars being the principal things of all worship for the whole of recorded history since, Uruk's own temple precinct included. A barley account is not a form of worship and has no place in the second sequence at any position.

The comparison decodes nothing, uses no contested archaeology, depends on no date within a chronology ninety-five per cent of which is stylistic attribution inside an admittedly circular framework, and compares an order rather than a content — which means it could have come out wrong and did not. It also inverts the account it sits beside. Monumentality is usually read as the moment symbolic capacity became strong enough to organise labour and raise stone. On this reading the labour is not an expression of new power but the cost of a substitution: **the pillar is raised because the mountain can no longer be read.**

Applied to the stone itself, the same doctrine yields more than a sequence. On Pillar 18 it reads a human form built rather than perceived — a body raised in quarried limestone, its inmost region elevated but left anonymous, its elements gathered by the belt's common bond, and the power of its hands held inward at the girded centre. On Pillar 43 the elements compose into a statement: life governed from the lowest senses is defended by poisonous reasoning through its absolute power in the ultimates; the will is severed from the rest of the understanding; and the deepest understanding that dies in that severance is protected and prepared for new life rather than lost. The vulture is decisive because it supplies that preservation. That is what the doctrine of correspondences produces when it is applied to those surfaces under the constraints of §2.1. It is not a claim about what anyone in the tenth millennium BCE intended, and it does not need to be one to be worth having. It required no date, no orientation, no asterism, and no assumption that the pillar still stands where it was set. It required the carved surface and a consistent reading of it — and it used every principal element on that surface, including the one that any astronomical reading must set aside.

The trajectory is not a lament. Swedenborg's own qualification is unambiguous: had the human race not been brought into this state, no one could have been saved. The externalization is a provision. The scribes who standardised the sign and taught it preserved a capacity that would otherwise have gone with the generation holding it, and the people who raised multi-tonne stone at Göbekli Tepe, before agriculture, at enormous unrewarded cost, were doing what remained available to them and doing it with evident seriousness. Nothing here diminishes them. The difference, in Swedenborg's own comparison, is between the light of the sun by day and the light of the moon and stars by night — and moonlight is light; the good is the same in both, and only the clearness differs.

And the direction is not sealed. *Arcana Coelestia* §1409 describes a form surviving the loss of its signification; the possibility of reading such a form again is the premise tested by this thesis, not a recovery process stated in that passage. Göbekli Tepe's carved limestone was buried deliberately and survived intact for eleven thousand years. That preservation makes a reading possible; it does not by itself show that the key had been lost or explain why the site was buried.

The celestial person perceived elevation and needed no mountain. The spiritual person worshipped upon the mountain. And when the mountain would no longer speak, someone quarried a stone, and shaped it, and stood it upright in the open air.

| Stage | Doctrinal | Archaeological | Strength of the correspondence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Worship upon mountains | Marking of found cave surfaces; natural relief incorporated | **Strong** — both are meaning indicated in a given landform |
| 2 | Worship in groves in the midst of trees | Continued marking on unmodified supports | **Weak** — no clean archaeological counterpart; assigned by character (found living setting), not compelled by the text. See §7.4 |
| 3 | Pillars or statues in the open air | Göbekli Tepe T-pillars in open-air enclosures | **Strong** — object type, setting, and sequence position all coincide. Uses only what the excavators themselves assert, and no reading of the reliefs |
| 4 | Altars and burnt-offerings becoming "the principal things of all worship" | Built installations at Çatalhöyük; Neolithic and later altars; the sacrificial cult continuing long past Uruk | **Moderate** — the direction and the centralisation match; the stage is open-ended and no terminus is identified |
| 5 | Significative → representative (§1409) | Motif persistence and changing use from Çatalhöyük through the Halaf assemblages | **Weak / interpretive** — a possible material analogue, but the material does not show whether the signification was known or lost; the proto-cuneiform inventory is a separate lineage and is excluded |

**What the mapping claims:** that two independently produced sequences share an order and a hinge, and that the doctrinal one was published in 1749, before parietal art was accepted as authentic (post-1902) and before Göbekli Tepe was excavated (1995).

**What the mapping does not claim:** that any sign has been read; that Göbekli Tepe is the Ancient Church; that the churches are datable periods. See §7.3.
