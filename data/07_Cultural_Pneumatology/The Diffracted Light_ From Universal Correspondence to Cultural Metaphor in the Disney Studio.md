# **The Diffracted Light: From Universal Correspondence to Cultural Metaphor in the Disney Studio**

## **I. Introduction: The Cartography of the Invisible**

The historiography of the Walt Disney Studio has traditionally operated under a presumption of benign secularism, or at most, a generalized, non-denominational Protestantism often categorized by sociologists as "Civil Religion." Within this established narrative, the studio’s output—characterized by anthropomorphic animals, landscapes that weep or rejoice in sympathy with the protagonist, and a moral universe governed by absolute laws of cause and effect—is viewed as a commercialized distillation of European fairy tales. The presence of spiritual motifs is typically attributed to the "indirect transmission" of Romanticism: the idea that Disney animators, in adapting the Brothers Grimm or Hans Christian Andersen, inadvertently inherited the spiritual DNA of those authors without necessarily understanding or endorsing the specific theological systems that birthed them.  
However, a rigorous forensic analysis of the studio's archival history, pedagogical methods, and personnel networks reveals a different reality. This report posits that the "Disney Style"—specifically the method of animating movement to create the "Illusion of Life"—is not merely a technical achievement but a secularized application of the theology of Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772). Furthermore, the evidence suggests that this influence was not accidental but the result of an "active substrate": a conscious, high-fidelity transmission of Swedenborgian concepts regarding the relationship between the spiritual (cause) and the natural (effect).  
From the specific theological coding in *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* (1948) to the "Action Analysis" pedagogy of Don Graham, the Disney Studio has historically functioned as a laboratory for the industrial application of Swedenborgian metaphysics. This report demonstrates that the studio did not just stumble upon these themes; they actively sought out consultants, teachers, and artists who possessed a "vocabulary for the invisible," thereby embedding the Doctrine of Correspondences into the very physics of their animated universe.  
Yet a critical fissure is said to have opened in the studio's modern era, the post-2010 "Revival" of *Tangled*, *Moana*, *Frozen II* and *Encanto*. The studio's growing reliance on "Cultural Trusts", advisory bodies of anthropologists, historians and cultural practitioners, has introduced a new layer between the studio and its symbols. The trusts secure cultural accuracy, and with it, the critique runs, they bring spiritual phenomena to the screen through the lens of particular traditions rather than through the universal, "vertical" causality of Swedenborg's system.  
This raises the question the present report sets out to test: Has the studio traded the universal "Science of Correspondences" for a system of "Cultural Metaphor"? The thesis under examination holds that it has: that while the cultural forms of correspondence in modern Disney films are valuable and follow similar principles, they rest on a misunderstanding of what correspondence is, translating actual spiritual causality into a culturally appropriate form that mimics it but functions as metaphor. Swedenborgian correspondence is not cultural in its essence; it is a spiritual logic that transcends culture, and the cultural part is only the form that encloses a universal causal essence.  
The thesis can be tested, because the studio has published its own reasoning. Its art books record, film by film, how the artists arrived at their images and why. The test is simple: when the modern studio gives a natural form a spiritual quality, does it rest that quality on cultural authority, or on what the thing itself does? The answer, set out in sections IV and V, is the second. In every case where the reasoning is on record, the studio argued from function and force to inner quality, exactly as Don Graham taught it to do in the 1930s; the cultures and their trusts supplied the vessel, and where they supplied content, the content was itself correspondential. The light has been diffracted, not dimmed. A prism does not change light; it shows the colours light already holds.

### **1.1 The Epistemological Framework: Swedenborg’s Semiotics**

To assess the validity of the comparison between Disney’s cinematic language and Swedenborg’s theology, one must first define the precise mechanism of Swedenborgian correspondence. Unlike simple allegory, which is arbitrary invention or poetic simile (*The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* §2), correspondence is defined as a causal, ontological relationship. It is not that a lion reminds the viewer of courage in a metaphorical sense; it is that the spiritual affection of the "power of truth" causes the form of a lion to exist in the natural world. The natural form is the "ultimate" container for the spiritual cause.  
Swedenborg’s system rests on three pillars that are particularly relevant to animation analysis:

* **Vertical Causality (Influx):** The spiritual world is the realm of causes; the natural world is the realm of effects. Life flows from the Divine, through the spiritual degrees, into natural forms. The quality of the form determines the reception of the influx. A distorted form (a villain or a wasteland) receives the same life force but twists it into evil or decay.  
* **Discrete Degrees:** Reality is stratified into distinct levels that do not merge but communicate via correspondence. The "Celestial" degree (pertaining to Love and the Will) flows into the "Spiritual" degree (pertaining to Wisdom and the Intellect), which in turn flows into the "Natural" degree (pertaining to Action and Form).  
* **The Grand Man (Maximus Homo):** Swedenborg posits that the entire heaven is structured in the form of a single human being, and by extension, human society and the church function as a "collective man." The health of this collective body depends on the proper subordination of the "natural" (feet/extremities) to the "spiritual" and "celestial" (heart/head).

In the context of animation, the animator plays the role of the "spiritual cause," infusing *anima* (life/soul) into the "natural" forms on screen. Thus, the physics of the Disney universe are inherently spiritual physics: gravity, light, color, and biology obey the emotional and moral logic of the scene rather than the laws of Newtonian mechanics. This artistic necessity aligns the medium perfectly with Swedenborg’s vision of a plastic, responsive spiritual world where affections manifest visibly as animals and landscapes shift instantaneously to reflect the interior state of the inhabitants (*Heaven and Hell* §§110, 173–175).

### **1.2 The Distinction: Correspondence vs. Metaphor**

A critical methodological distinction must be drawn between *Correspondence* and *Metaphor* (or Allegory) to understand the critique of the modern "Cultural Trust" approach.

* **Metaphor/Allegory (Arbitrary/Mechanical):** These are human-invented rhetorical devices. In an allegory, the relationship is a mechanical substitution (e.g., a pair of scales representing justice) that requires a codebook to decipher. It is "horizontal"—relating one natural object to an abstract concept based on human agreement.  
* **Correspondence (Organic/Symbolic):** This is a universal law where the natural world is a causal mirror of the spiritual world. A correspondence is "discovered" rather than "invented" and is inherent in the object's nature. It is "vertical"—relating a natural effect to its spiritual cause (*The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* §2; *The Epistemic Architecture of Post-Materialist Inquiry* §2.3).

The thesis under examination argues that modern Disney films, by relying on external cultural advisors, treat symbols as cultural metaphors (horizontal) rather than spiritual correspondences (vertical). They adopt the *form* (e.g., Maui's hair) because a specific culture believes it has power, rather than presenting it as a universal law of spiritual influx that necessitates hair as the "ultimate" of the natural.

### **1.3 The "Active Substrate" Defined**

To argue that Swedenborgian thought is an "active substrate" rather than a passive influence requires a precise definition of terms. An active substrate in institutional culture is a foundational layer of philosophy or methodology that is continuously reinforced through training, hiring practices, and creative mandates. It is not merely a historical footnote; it is the operating system upon which the institution runs.  
In the case of Disney, the Swedenborgian substrate is defined by the Doctrine of Influx and the Science of Correspondences. These theological concepts, which posit that the natural world is a theater representing the spiritual world and that all physical action is the result of spiritual influx (will and understanding), became the technical manuals for American animation. When Walt Disney's studio taught its artists, in Don Graham's words, that "the artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force" (Graham, 21 June 1937), it was demanding a shift from a materialist view of the world to a Swedenborgian one. The survival and evolution of this substrate, from the 1930s *Silly Symphonies* to the 2023 centennial film *Wish*, constitutes the central argument of this investigation.

## **II. The Architecture of the Invisible: Historical Roots of the Disney Ethos**

To understand why the Disney Studio would actively engage with a specific 18th-century theological system, one must first understand the artistic crisis that threatened the studio in the early 1930s. This was not a financial crisis, but a metaphysical one: the "Crisis of Ensoulment".

### **2.1 The Crisis of the Rubber Hose**

In the 1920s, American animation was dominated by the "rubber hose" style. Characters like Felix the Cat or the early Mickey Mouse were constructed of loose, flexible tubes. They moved with a fluid, rhythmic bounce that was entertaining but fundamentally mechanical. They had no weight, no bone structure, and most importantly, no interiority. If a character moved, it was because the animator moved the pencil, not because the character had a thought or a feeling that caused the movement.  
Walt Disney realized by the early 1930s that this style had a distinct ceiling. He envisioned feature-length films that could sustain an audience's emotional investment for eighty minutes. To achieve this, his characters needed to appear to have a soul. They needed to seem self-motivated. This required a paradigm shift from "animating movement" to "animating intentions". This shift necessitated a new physics—one where the internal will was the primary mover of the external form.

### **2.2 The Swedenborgian Solution: The Doctrine of Influx**

Emanuel Swedenborg’s theology offered a precise mechanism for this problem. Swedenborg taught the doctrine of Influx, which structures reality into discrete degrees:

* **The Spiritual (Cause):** The realm of Will, Affection, and Thought.  
* **The Natural (Effect):** The realm of Body, Action, and Form.

The mechanism is explicit: Life flows from the Spiritual into the Natural. The body does not move itself; the spirit moves the body. Therefore, the shape of the body and its actions are "correspondences" of the spirit within. Swedenborg wrote in *Divine Providence* (§103): "The body’s activity in speech or deed is only an effect from the external and internal of man’s spirit, for the body is so much obedience".  
For an animator facing the Crisis of Ensoulment, this is the Holy Grail. To make a drawing look alive, one must not draw the body moving; one must draw the affection (the spiritual cause) that results in the body moving. This alignment of artistic necessity with theological doctrine created a vacuum at the Disney Studio that pulled in Swedenborgian thinkers and methods. The studio began to actively seek out anyone who could teach this principle of "Inner Force driving Outer Form".

### **2.3 Don Graham and the Pedagogy of Forces**

The primary vector for this "active substrate" was Don Graham, the art instructor hired by Walt Disney in 1932 to retrain the studio's artists (D23 1932). Graham’s pedagogy, known as "Action Analysis," was a secularized reinstatement of Swedenborg’s doctrine of Influx. Before Graham, animation training focused on "Form"—how to draw a muscle, how to draw a joint. This led to the "rubber hose" stiffness. Graham introduced a radical new methodology: he taught that the animator must not draw the thing, but the force driving the thing.  
In his class of June 21, 1937, he told the animators: "The artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force" (Graham, 21 June 1937). A famous memo from Walt Disney to Don Graham in 1935 explicitly articulates this philosophy: "The driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character—or all three. Therefore, the mind is the pilot" (Disney, 23 December 1935). Graham put the order plainly to the animators: "it is the emotion and mood that come first, the action comes second. The action is a secondary thing - it is a result of the thought or feeling"; "it is the thought process that determines primary actions, not the action that determines the thought" (Graham, 22 February 1937). He knew he was teaching more than a technique. When an animator objected that putting it all as forces "sounds too much like philosophy," Graham answered: "It *is* a philosophy." It was, he said, "a new philosophy of approach ... not really new, but adapted to Studio uses" (Graham, 21 June 1937). He did not name its source; the studio received it as craft. The order is the one Swedenborg gives: "conatus is the all in forces, and through forces is the all in motion; and since motion is the outmost degree of conatus, through motion conatus exerts its power" (*Divine Love and Wisdom* §218). As Bill Tytla told the same class a week later, "We can't be mystical about our work ... This is a business," and yet "the ultimate thing is the mood or sensation you get into a piece of work" (Tytla, in Graham, 28 June 1937). The law the studio teaches has a documented lineage. Its nineteenth-century form is François Delsarte's Law of Correspondence: "To each spiritual function responds a function of the body; to each grand function of the body corresponds a spiritual act." Delsarte's own sources were Catholic and hermetic, not Swedenborg (Waille 2012; Waille 2015, ¶62). It was his American expositors who read his law through Swedenborg and taught it that way. Genevieve Stebbins's textbook quotes *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219 on will, forces and action, defines correspondence in the words of the New Church writer Edward Madeley ("no arbitrary relationship like metaphor or figure"), and records Lewis B. Monroe's verdict that Delsarte was "Swedenborg geometrized" (Stebbins 1901; Madeley 1883). A handbook of 1896 calls it "the law of correspondence, which was discovered by Swedenborg" (Northrop 1896). Monroe's pupil Henrietta Hovey taught it in Los Angeles from 1915 (Ruyter 2012). From Stebbins's book the law passed to Volkonsky's Russian manual, which names Swedenborg once and keeps "the manifestation of the inner man in his outward form" (Volkonsky 1913, 49, 203–204); from Volkonsky to Stanislavsky (Whyman 2008); and from Stanislavsky's *An Actor Prepares* into Graham's class, whose animators recommended it in February 1937 as "helpful reading along these lines" (Graham, 22 February 1937). Decades later Walt Stanchfield taught Delsarte by name in the studio's own classes (Stanchfield 2009). The full chain, link by link, is set out in *The Law of Correspondence in the Arts of Expression*. This pedagogy posits that the physical world (the drawing) is merely the "obedience" of the spiritual world (the character's intent). The animator does not manipulate the body; the animator manipulates the will, and the body follows.  
The Chouinard Art Institute (where Graham taught) was permeable to the broader currents of "Theosophy" and "Metaphysics" prevalent in California art circles at the time (Karlstrom 1996; Crosse 2014). Theosophy, heavily indebted to Swedenborg (via Blavatsky and earlier mystics), preached that thoughts were "things" with shape and color (Besant and Leadbeater 1901). Graham’s classes, carried forward by Walt Stanchfield, who titled one of his own lectures "The Metaphysical Side" (Stanchfield 2009), trained Disney animators to see the material world as a shell for spiritual energies. This training primed the entire studio culture to accept stories where internal moral states dictated external physical reality. When an animator drew a storm cloud in *Fantasia* or *Bambi*, they weren't drawing water vapor; they were drawing "anger" or "menace." This is the definition of Correspondence. By institutionalizing this training, Disney built a studio that thought like Swedenborgians, even if they didn't pray like them.

> **[EXTENSION]**: No document places Theosophy in Graham's classroom; this route is atmospheric. The documented route of the method is the tradition of expression. Delsarte's "law of correspondence" was taught in America through Swedenborg (Stebbins's textbook quotes *Divine Love and Wisdom* §219; Monroe called Delsarte "Swedenborg geometrized"). It passed through Volkonsky's manual to Stanislavsky, and Graham's class of February 1937 recommended Stanislavsky's *An Actor Prepares* as "helpful reading along these lines". Delsarte himself did not draw on Swedenborg; the Swedenborgian reading is his American expositors'. See *The Law of Correspondence in the Arts of Expression*, §§5–8.

### **2.4 The Appleseed Nexus: A Case Study in Intentional Coding**

The argument that this influence was purely accidental is dismantled by the production history of *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* (1948). This film serves as the "smoking gun" for intentional theological coding within the studio.  
Johnny Appleseed (John Chapman) was a real historical figure and a missionary for the Church of the New Jerusalem (Swedenborgianism): "a very extraordinary missionary of the New Jerusalem," a New Church report called him in 1817 (Society for Printing, Publishing and Circulating the Writings of Emanuel Swedenborg 1817, quoted in Harris 1949), and "a most earnest disciple of the faith taught by Emanuel Swedenborg" (Haley 1871). When Disney decided to adapt his life, they were faced with a dilemma: how to portray a man whose entire life was driven by a specific, sectarian theology without alienating a mass audience. Rather than erasing Chapman's faith, the studio engaged in a process of "active translation".  
The studio engaged Carl Carmer, historian of the "Burned-Over District" (the hotbed of 19th-century American religious movements), who is credited as folklore consultant on *Melody Time* (American Film Institute n.d.); the film had begun as an anthology of American folk heroes (Gabler 2006). The studio also corresponded with Florence Murdoch, librarian of the Cincinnati Church of the New Jerusalem. When she objected to the coonskin-capped angel, Hal Adelquist, in charge of the Story Department, replied that the studio had researched the real Chapman extensively and that "we have treated the angel with sincere respect and endowed him with a certain dignity" (Adelquist, 23 June 1948, quoted in Fryeburg New Church Assembly n.d.; Means 2011, 258–259). Through these channels, the studio answered for the film's "visual theology" to Chapman's own faith, even if the explicit sectarian labels were removed.  
The film encodes the "Blessings Three" into its closing song, which tells how "he left his blessings three" (*The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* 1948): "Love, Faith and the Apple Tree," as the studio's own album text names them (*Johnny Appleseed*, RCA Victor 1949). This is a direct translation of the Swedenborgian Trinity of Essentials: Love (the Will), Faith (the Understanding), and Use (Works) (*The Apple and the Angel* §5.2). Furthermore, the film's depiction of Johnny's "Guardian Angel" adheres to Swedenborg’s specific angelology, which teaches that all angels were once humans who lived on earth: "in the entire heaven there is not a single angel who was created such from the beginning … all, both in heaven and in hell, are from the human race" (*Heaven and Hell* §311). The angel in the film is not a distinct species with wings, but a "pioneer spirit"—a human figure in frontier garb and a coonskin cap. The studio defended him to Murdoch as true to Chapman's own belief that heavenly spirits walked among men (Means 2011, 258–259; Haley 1871 records Chapman's "frequent conversations with angels and spirits"). This specific detail shows that the studio was not merely copying generic Christian tropes but was actively engaging with Swedenborgian distinctions.

## **III. The Universal Grammar: The Science of Correspondences in the Golden Age**

Having established the historical and pedagogical "substrate," we turn to the "Golden Age" and "Renaissance" films (roughly 1937–1999) to analyze how this "Science of Correspondences" manifested as a universal symbolic logic. In these films, the correspondence between the spiritual and the natural is treated as an objective, universal law, consistent with Swedenborg’s claim that "the natural world springs from and has permanent existence from the spiritual world, precisely like an effect from its effecting cause" (*Heaven and Hell* §89): correspondences are discovered, not invented.

### **3.1 The King and the Land: Topography as Moral Mirror**

One of the most persistent and structurally significant tropes in the Disney canon is the intrinsic, causal link between the moral condition of the monarch and the physical health of the environment. This narrative device, known in comparative mythology and literary criticism as the "Fisher King" archetype, is structurally identical to Swedenborg’s correspondence of the "Land" or "Earth" with the "State of the Church" (the collective spiritual state of humanity).  
In Swedenborg’s exegesis, the physical state of the land in biblical prophecy—whether it is blooming, drought-stricken, or frozen—is never a description of agriculture but a precise diagnosis of the spiritual state of the people. Disney films replicate this diagnostic topography with high fidelity.  
**The Lion King (1994)** serves as a perfect model of Swedenborg’s *Maximus Homo* (The Grand Man; *The Animating Spirit: An Exhaustive Analysis* §6.1). The Kingdom is a body. Mufasa is the "Head" (Wisdom/Government). The Lionesses are the "Heart/Lungs" (Vital operations). The Hyenas are the "Intestines/Excretory function" (consuming waste). When Scar (the Self/Self-Love) usurps the Head, the entire Body (Land) sickens. The resulting drought is not meteorological; it is "spiritual vastation." The water (Truth) dries up because the connection to the spiritual source (Mufasa/The Sun) has been severed. The return of the true King (Truth) brings immediate rain (Influx of Truth), which restores the land instantaneously.  
**Frozen (2013)** presents a similar correspondence. Arendelle’s eternal winter is a literalization of the metaphor "frozen heart." Swedenborg describes "frigid zones" in the spiritual world, where climates vary "according to the distances of the will's affections and the consequent thought of the understanding from true love and true faith," and where "lands and waters alike are bound in ice with snow upon them" (*True Christian Religion* §185). Elsa’s fear creates a world of ice (Truth without Good). Ice, in Swedenborgian symbology, corresponds to truth that has become hard, brittle, and unyielding because it lacks the heat of love: truths without good "shine coldly, like something snowy, without heat, because they do not draw their essence from good" (*Heaven and Hell* §132). The "thaw" can only be achieved by an act of true love (Charity), which restores the liquid state (Truth flowing with Good).  
In these narratives, the "Golden Age" is not a time period but a spiritual state—a state of "Divine Order" where the natural world perfectly reflects the spiritual governance of a righteous King. The landscape is a moral mirror, providing a "universal" diagnosis of the spiritual health of the characters.

### **3.2 Animal Correspondences: The Menagerie of the Mind**

Swedenborg asserted that animals correspond to specific human "affections": "The animals of the earth correspond in general to affection, mild and useful animals to good affections, fierce and useless ones to evil affections" (*Heaven and Hell* §110). Disney films adhere to this taxonomy with remarkable consistency, utilizing animals not as biological entities but as externalized aspects of the human psyche.  
**The Horse (Intellect):** A central Swedenborgian correspondence is Horse \= Understanding/Intellect: a "horse" signifies "the intellectual faculty" (*Arcana Coelestia* §2761). In the Ancient Near East and Greek mythology (e.g., Pegasus), the horse represents power, swiftness, and guidance. Disney films consistently use the horse to represent the "Intellectual Principle."

* In *Tangled*, Maximus is the "Intellect" obsessed with the "Law." He is rigid, judgmental, and relentless until guided by Rapunzel (Affection).  
* In *Frozen II*, the Nokk (Water Horse) represents the "Natural Understanding." Elsa (the Spiritual Mind) must tame and ride the horse, symbolizing the spiritual mind's control over the natural intellect.  
* In *Sleeping Beauty*, Samson represents the "Power of Truth" that carries the Prince into battle against Falsity (Maleficent).

**The Bird (Rational Thought):** Because they fly above the earth, birds correspond to intellectual ideas and rational concepts: "birds correspond, according to their species, to the intellectual things of the natural or the spiritual mind" (*Heaven and Hell* §110; *Arcana Coelestia* §40).

* **Zazu (*The Lion King*)** represents "administrative truth" or orderly thinking. He is the bureaucrat of the mind, concerned with rules and boundaries.  
* **Scuttle (*The Little Mermaid*)** represents "false rationality"—the tendency of the sensory mind to make incorrect deductions (e.g., a fork is a "dinglehopper").

**The Dog (Natural Affection):** Dogs, among the mild and useful animals that correspond to good affections (*Heaven and Hell* §110), carry "natural good"—loyalty, friendship, and the simple affections of the natural mind. In Swedenborg the dog's sense turns on what it serves. Turned to its own appetites, it is the lowest and the unclean (*Arcana Coelestia* §§7784, 9231); faithful, it is the guard: he saw "a great dog, such as that called Cerberus," and was told that "such a dog signifies a guard to prevent man's passing over from heavenly conjugial love to the love of adultery" (*Arcana Coelestia* §5051). Disney's dogs are the faithful kind. Characters like Bruno (*Cinderella*) or Max (*The Little Mermaid*) represent the intuitive ability of the natural mind to perceive good and evil more clearly than the rational mind (which can be deceived).  
This system of animal correspondences functions as a "Universal Grammar." It assumes that the meaning of a "Horse" or a "Lion" is inherent in the animal's nature (its function), not culturally assigned. A Lion represents the "Power of Truth" because of its strength and dominance ("a 'lion' signifies truth in its power," *Apocalypse Revealed* §241), regardless of whether the story is set in Africa or England. This aligns with the Swedenborgian view that correspondences are "organic" and "discovered," not "invented".

### **3.3 Weather and Elements: The Pathetic Fallacy as Spiritual Science**

Weather events in Disney films are rarely meteorological; they are "spiritual physics" that respond to emotional and moral shifts. This utilization of the "Pathetic Fallacy" (attributing human emotion to nature) is, in the Disney context, a rigorous application of the Science of Correspondences.  
**Rain/Water:** Corresponds to "Truth" and "Cleansing." The rain at the end of *The Lion King* is the "Influx of Truth" that washes away the falsities of Scar’s reign. In Swedenborgian thought, water corresponds to truth because it cleanses, reflects, and sustains life. **Ice vs. Snow:** Ice represents truth that lacks "Good" (love), becoming hard and brittle. Snow represents natural truth that is cold but can be melted (*Heaven and Hell* §132). The interplay between ice and water in *Frozen* is a precise theological drama about the relationship between "Faith Alone" (Ice) and "Charity" (Warmth).  
The "Universal" nature of these symbols in the Golden Age and Renaissance films suggests a belief in a shared, objective spiritual reality. The symbols were treated as "Archetypes"—structures of the psyche that transcend culture. A King was a King, whether in *Aladdin* or *The Little Mermaid*, representing the same principle of Governance/Truth.

## **IV. The Modern Era: Testing the Hypothesis of Cultural Metaphor**

The modern era of Disney animation (2010–present) is where the hypothesis of "Cultural Metaphor" must be tested. Its films were made with "Cultural Trusts", advisory bodies of anthropologists, linguists and cultural practitioners, and the critique holds that the trusts changed the ground of the studio's symbols: that a symbol now holds because a culture says it does, not because it is true in the nature of things.

What would count against the studio is clear. If the filmmakers rested a spiritual quality on cultural authority alone ("Maui has hair because Polynesians say so"), the critique would stand. If they reasoned from what the thing does to what it is, the critique fails, because that is the criterion of correspondence itself: a correspondence is "validated by the functional analogy of the object itself," an allegory by a codebook (*The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* §2). The studio's art books, which record the filmmakers' own words film by film, allow the test to be run. Four films are examined, in order, beginning with the one that had no trust at all.

### **4.1 Tangled (2010): The Power in the Hair, Before the Trusts**

*Tangled*, the film that opens the Revival, adapts the Brothers Grimm and was made without a cultural trust. Its central symbol is the one that would later be attributed to Polynesian authority in *Moana*: power held in the hair.

The Grimm tale does not contain it. In the 1857 text Rapunzel's hair is "fein wie gesponnen Gold" (fine as spun gold), but it is only the way up: the prince, watching the enchantress climb it, says, "Ist das die Leiter, auf welcher man hinauf kommt, so will ich auch einmal mein Glück versuchen" (If that is the ladder by which one climbs, I too will try my luck). The enchantress cuts the braids in anger, and the healing comes at the end from Rapunzel's tears: "Zwei von ihren Thränen aber benetzten seine Augen, da wurden sie wieder klar" (Two of her tears wetted his eyes, and they grew clear again) (Grimm 1857, 1:65–69).

The studio added the power. In the film the golden hair holds the healing virtue of a flower grown from "a single drop of sunlight", and Rapunzel calls it out in song: "Flower, gleam and glow / Let your powers shine / Make the clock reverse / Bring back what once was mine / Heal what has been hurt / Change the fates' design" (*Tangled* 2010). When the hair is cut, its power is gone and its gold turns brown; the healing that remains is the one Grimm already gave her, the tear. Without any cultural adviser, the studio turned Grimm's ladder into Samson's hair. Swedenborg reads the same figure: the Nazirite's "power lay in his hair," because "the 'hair of the head' signifies heavenly wisdom in ultimate things, and also Divine truth in ultimate things" (*Sacred Scripture* §49). The power is from the sun, and "the sun of heaven is the Lord; the light there is the Divine truth and the heat the Divine good that go forth from the Lord as a sun" (*Heaven and Hell* §117). Mother Gothel, who hoards that light to keep herself young, is the self that claims what flows through another as its own possession (*The Animating Spirit: An Exhaustive Analysis* §6.2).

No culture was consulted, and the correspondence is complete. That is the baseline against which the trust films can be read.

### **4.2 Moana (2016): Samson in Mo'orea**

For *Moana*, Disney established the Oceanic Story Trust, a group of anthropologists, cultural practitioners, historians, linguists, and choreographers from islands including Samoa, Tahiti, Mo’orea, and Fiji (Robinson 2016). This body was instrumental in shifting the film's depiction of the demigod Maui. Originally designed as bald, Maui was given his hair after Hinano Murphy, a Trust member and Tahitian cultural practitioner, told the studio: "We have to put more hair on Maui’s head, because it’s very important. The mana is in the hair, the power of the demigod" (Robinson 2016).

This is the case the critique leans on, and the record does not bear it out. The consultants did not offer the rule as a local custom to be obeyed; they explained it through a universal figure. Ron Clements recalls: "When we asked some of our consultants from Mo'orea, they told us that they envisioned Maui with a mane of Sampson-like hair. His hair is his mana, they explained" (Julius and Malone 2016). The Polynesian advisers reached for the same Samson whom the studio had already given to Rapunzel six years earlier, and whom Swedenborg reads as "the Lord’s power from the ultimate things of truth" (*Sacred Scripture* §49). The tradition gave the same correspondence in its own form: a ceremonial headdress, as the art book records, "is made from real hair from the ancestors, which is imbued with mana [spiritual power]" (Julius and Malone 2016). What the trust supplied, then, is not a cultural rule replacing a universal one. It is a second witness to the same correspondence, given in its own form and translated by the witnesses themselves into the universal one.

The living ocean came from the culture in the same way. On the research trip a Fijian captain told the filmmakers, "You must speak gently to this ocean," and they learned that it "is seen as a living, sentient being with feelings and moods that needs to be treated with great respect" (Clements and Musker, in Julius and Malone 2016). The directors made it a character to convey, in Musker's words, that "the ocean connects us. The land and the sea are one and the same" (Julius and Malone 2016). The perception is old. Swedenborg holds that the knowledge of correspondences was once "the chief of knowledges" (*Heaven and Hell* §87), cultivated across the ancient world, where people "worshiped in gardens and groves, according to the different kinds of trees in them … for gardens and groves signified wisdom and intelligence" (*True Christian Religion* §§202, 205). A people who still speak gently to the sea have kept something of that knowledge; in the Word, "'seas'" signify "a collection of knowledges" (*Arcana Coelestia* §28).

The studio's own part was what Graham trained it to do: look at the thing. "We didn't want to mess with the physics, like light passing through water or the patterns waves create on sand," says production designer Ian Gooding; "we tried to honor how amazing the ocean is in reality." The design rules "evolved from what we noticed in nature," says Andy Harkness: "we stylized what was already there." The effects team went into pools and the sea, "shooting lasers to measure the amount of light diffusing through the water—all to understand it better" (Dale Mayeda, in Julius and Malone 2016).

### **4.3 Frozen II (2019): Water Has Memory**

*Frozen II* centres on the concept that "water has memory." The filmmakers collaborated with the Verddet, a Sámi expert group set up under an agreement between Walt Disney Animation Studios and the Sámi Parliaments and Saami Council to guide them through Sámi culture, history, rights and society, so that the depiction of the Northuldra people would be accurate (Sámi Parliament of Finland 2019). The art book records what that collaboration produced: the producer thanks "the indigenous Sámi people with whom we collaborated to design the final costumes for Honeymaren, Ryder, and Yelana" (Del Vecho, in Julius 2019).

The idea at the centre of the film did not come from the trust. The producer, Peter Del Vecho, describes how the team reached it: "Elsa's magic comes from and is a part of nature. Her powers are connected to water. As we looked into the science of water, we learned about the concept that water has memory. Water dissolves other substances and absorbs part of everything that passes through it, so in a sense it really holds our history. All the water we have is all the water we ever had or will have. It was incredibly powerful to stand on a giant glacier and realize it held history" (Julius 2019). This is a correspondence reached by functional analogy, from what water does to what it is, and it arrives where Swedenborg had placed it: "It is a very common thing in the Word for 'waters' to signify knowledges [cognitiones et scientifica]" (*Arcana Coelestia* §28). In the film Olaf states it as a fact of nature, and Iduna's Northuldra lullaby gives **Ahtohallan**, the glacier of truth, its form: "a river full of memory" (*Frozen II* 2019). The journey to Ahtohallan is a journey inward to the "internal memory" where the truth of one's life is stored, and what Elsa finds there exposes the dam her grandfather built: the works of the self, laid across the flow, are brought to light by the memory of the waters.

The rest of the art book shows the same training at work in every department:

* **Truth to materials.** "There has to be a truth to materials for audiences to really believe in what's depicted on-screen," says head of environments Sean Jenkins. To build Ahtohallan the team worked out how glacial ice handles light: "Red is absorbed the fastest, then green, then blue" (Julius 2019).
* **Force known by its effects.** Of the wind spirit, Gale: "It's not anthropomorphic; it's nature, it's wind. We only 'see' it by how it causes other things to react, like hair or fabric rippling" (Julius 2019). Graham told the animators in 1937 that "the artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force" (Graham, 21 June 1937); the studio then wrote software for exactly that, driving leaves, grass and hair from Gale's path (Black, Correy and Fiske 2020).
* **The inner state governs the outer event.** "What is the emotional tone of this sequence, and how can effects support that? It's not just about what would naturally happen in the real world" (Dale Mayeda, in Julius 2019). This is the memo of 1935: "the mind is the pilot" (Disney, 23 December 1935).

### **4.4 Encanto (2021): The House as the Family**

In *Encanto*, a cultural trust of Colombian experts advised the filmmakers on clothing, food, architecture and customs (McGowan 2022). The house, **Casita**, is alive. It responds instantly to the emotional state of the family. When the family's relationships (spiritual bonds) fracture, the house (physical container) cracks.

This is perhaps the purest example of "Vertical Causality" in the modern era, and the art book shows that the filmmakers built it as such. "Early on, the filmmakers landed on the idea of the physical house as a literal representation of the family and their emotional connections. If the family is happy, the house is healthy, but if the family is going through struggles, the house too will crack." The gifts were assigned the same way: "the powers assigned to each member didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality," says Jared Bush (Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022). Not arbitrary, dictated by the inner state: that is the definition of correspondence against allegory, stated by the filmmakers themselves. Co-director Charise Castro Smith gives the reason: "we can somehow process and sense our emotions better when they are externalized that way" (Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022).

The culture supplied the form. Colombia gave the notion of "Encantos," places in nature filled with enchantment: "In Colombia, we are surrounded by sacred lands that feel magical, and we coexist with them, not questioning their existence," says the team's guide, Alejandra Espinosa. From it the filmmakers "crafted the idea of a house and town that magically emerges from the earth as the response to a prayer made in a moment of desperation"; "The land grants her a miracle when she begs for a way to escape the violence" (Castro Smith, in Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022). The literary tradition supplied the lens. The directors write that "while we always say Encanto is not true magical realism, it is absolutely inspired by the great literary traditions of writers like Gabriel García Márquez, Isabel Allende, and Jorge Luis Borges," whose "stories aren’t fantasy, but reality as told through a unique lens" (Bush, Howard and Castro Smith, in Reyes Lancaster Jones 2022); García Márquez's *One Hundred Years of Solitude* is named as the film's inspiration (Grønn and Svenhard 2022). One of those writers had read Swedenborg closely: Borges, "a pioneer of Magic Realism," counted him among his influences (Swedenborg Society n.d.).

The "Miracle Candle" represents Divine Influx, providing energy for the "gifts" (Uses). When the family loses sight of the source of the gifts (Love/Charity) and focuses only on the performance, the light fades. The Swedenborgian structure is intact: Use (Gifts) must be grounded in Love (Candle/Abuela's intent), and the miracle is granted in answer to prayer, from the land, at the moment of greatest need.

## **V. The Verdict of the Record: Correspondence, Not Metaphor**

### **5.1 The Criterion Applied**

*The Hermeneutics of the Mundus Imaginalis* (§2) defines the difference between **Correspondence** and **Allegory/Metaphor**:

* **Correspondence:** A causal link. The natural object *is* the spiritual reality in a lower form. It is "discovered" and "organic." Validated by the functional analogy of the object.
* **Allegory/Metaphor:** An arbitrary invention. A "mechanical substitution." Validated by a codebook.

Measured against this criterion, the four films give one result:

| Film | Symbol | How the studio arrived at it | Ground |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| *Tangled* | Power in the hair | Added to Grimm by the studio, with no trust; cut, the power is gone | The nature of the symbol (Samson) |
| *Moana* | Mana in the hair; the living ocean | From the Mo'orea consultants, who explained it as "Sampson-like hair"; the ocean from Fijian sailors; the physics of water honoured | A second witness to the same correspondence, translated by the witnesses into the universal form |
| *Frozen II* | Water has memory | "As we looked into the science of water"; from what water does | Functional analogy |
| *Encanto* | The house is the family | "Didn't feel arbitrary, but were dictated by emotions and personality" | Inner state to outer form |

In no case does the studio rest a spiritual quality on cultural authority alone. The method the 1930s studio built (look past the form to the force, know the invisible cause by its effects, let the inner state drive the outer world, ground the image in what the thing does) is the method its artists describe in their own words eighty years later. The thesis of Cultural Metaphor does not survive the record.

### **5.2 What the Cultures Gave**

The trusts did change something, and it is worth stating precisely. They supplied the outer forms: the costume of the Northuldra, the headdress of the *taualuga*, the food and architecture of the Madrigals. Where they supplied content, the content was the perception of a living nature, the ocean that must be spoken to gently, the sacred lands that are not questioned, the hair that holds mana. That perception is not a cultural metaphor. It is what Swedenborg describes as the remnant of the ancient science, once cultivated "in many of the kingdoms of Asia, especially in the land of Canaan, in Egypt, Assyria, Chaldea, Syria, Arabia, Tyre, Sidon, and Nineveh," where "the hieroglyphics of the Egyptians … were nothing, but correspondences" (*True Christian Religion* §§201–202), each people holding it in its own form:

* **Egypt** \= Scientifics (Memory) (*Arcana Coelestia* §1462).  
* **Assyria** \= Rationality (*Arcana Coelestia* §119).

Disney's "Cultural Trusts" can therefore be seen not as a betrayal of correspondence but as an (unconscious) recovery of the "Ancient Word": the specific correspondential languages that peoples outside the modern West have kept. The witnesses themselves show the unity beneath the forms when the advisers from Mo'orea explain mana by Samson. This is the constant state in variable form.

### **5.3 The Risk That Remains**

The critique was right about one thing, though it placed it in the studio rather than in the audience. The danger lies in reception. A viewer may stop at the cultural form and see "Sámi Mythology" or "Colombian Magic" rather than "The Physics of the Spirit," an anthropological curiosity ("Look what the Sámi believe") rather than a spiritual reality ("Look how the soul works"). The studio's method does not invite that reading, since every one of these correspondences is built to be felt as true. But a culture of reception that treats every spiritual perception as one belief among others will reduce it to that, whatever the film intended.

## **VI. Case Studies Across the Eras**

To illustrate the continuity, we compare specific symbols across the eras.

| Symbol | Golden Age | Modern Era | What the Record Shows |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **The King** | **Mufasa (*The Lion King*)**: Represents "Divine Truth" and Universal Law. His rule affects the cosmos directly. | **Chief Tui (*Moana*)**: Represents "Tradition" and "Safety." His rule is social; the cosmic power lies with Te Fiti, the ocean and Maui. | The cosmos still answers to the moral order: the land dies when Te Fiti's heart is stolen and lives when it is restored. The locus of the correspondence has moved from the king to the living world, not out of the film. |
| **Water** | **Rain (*The Lion King*)**: "Influx of Truth" cleansing the land. | **The Ocean (*Moana*)**; **Ahtohallan (*Frozen II*)**: a living ocean; "a river full of memory." | Water becomes a "who" as well as a "what." The memory of water was reached from its function ("as we looked into the science of water"); its life from a people who still speak to the sea. |
| **Hair** | **Samson (the Word)**: strength in the hair. | **Rapunzel (*Tangled*)**; **Maui (*Moana*)**: healing power in the hair; "Sampson-like hair. His hair is his mana." | The same correspondence, reached by the studio without a trust in 2010 and confirmed by the Mo'orea consultants in 2016. |
| **The House/Castle** | **Beast's Castle**: Dark/Gothic to Gold/Alabaster. A mirror of the soul's regeneration. | **Casita (*Encanto*)**: A living family member. It cracks with family stress. | **High Fidelity**. "A literal representation of the family and their emotional connections." House \= Mind, stated by the filmmakers. |

## **VII. Conclusion: The Diffracted Light**

The evidence confirms that the "active substrate" of Swedenborgian thought—the operational logic that "inner force drives outer form"—remains the foundational physics of the Walt Disney Studio. From Don Graham's classrooms to the code of the *Frozen II* wind spirit, the studio continues to treat the natural world as a theater representing the spiritual world.

The question this report set out to test was whether the modern studio, working through Cultural Trusts, had traded universal correspondence for cultural metaphor. The studio's own record answers it. When its artists explain why a symbol carries the meaning it does, they never answer "because this culture believes it." They answer from function (water holds history because it carries everything that passes through it), from force (the wind is seen only in what it moves), from the inner state (the house cracks because the family does; the gifts are "dictated by emotions and personality"). That is the pedagogy of 1935 and 1937, and it is the criterion of correspondence itself.

**The Nature of the Change:** The shift from the Golden Age to the Revival is not from spiritual causality to cultural metaphor. It is from **Implicit Universalism** to **Universalism in Particular Dress**.

* In the Golden Age, Disney presented its correspondences (Kings, Rain, Animals) as if they were the "Laws of Physics." They required no cultural explanation; they just *were*.  
* In the Modern Era, the studio still reasons from the same laws, and clothes them in a culture. *Water has memory* because of what water does, and the Northuldra's lullaby gives it a voice. *Maui has power in his hair* as Samson had, and the Polynesian witnesses said so themselves.

**Final Verdict:** The "Invisible Influx" persists, and it has not been camouflaged. In the Golden Age the Swedenborgian logic was hidden in plain sight as the "magic of animation." In the Revival it is hidden in the same place, and the cultures the studio consults have turned out to be fellow witnesses to it rather than its replacement. This report does not claim that the modern filmmakers knew Swedenborg's doctrine. Its claim concerns the method. What the studio teaches its artists is the core of the science of correspondences, the science Swedenborg rediscovered: read the inner cause from the outer form, and ground every image in what the thing does. That method entered the studio in the 1930s, when, as section II traces, Swedenborgian thought was present at its beginnings. That presence most likely gave the studio its shape, its character and its magic, and the impulse of the method came from Swedenborg. The artists receive it as craft, not as doctrine, and that is its strength. They learn the living core of correspondence, which works in any culture, rather than a key of fixed meanings, some of them religiously loaded. This is why the method can meet a Polynesian or a Colombian perception of living nature and recognize it. The light has passed through the prism of culture and been diffracted into its colours. A prism does not change the light; it shows what the light contained.
