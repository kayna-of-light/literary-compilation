# **The Pneumatic Cinema: A Forensic Investigation into the Swedenborgian Transmission Vectors of the Walt Disney Story Department (1930–1948)**

## **I. Introduction: The Historiographical Lacuna and the Metaphysics of Movement**

### **1.1 The Crisis of the Materialist Narrative**

The historiography of the Walt Disney Studio has, for nearly a century, been dominated by a materialist and technological determinism. Standard narratives, exemplified by the works of Barrier, Gabler, and others, trace the studio’s evolution through a linear progression of mechanical innovations: the synchronization of sound in *Steamboat Willie* (1928), the refinement of three-strip Technicolor in *Flowers and Trees* (1932), and the development of the multiplane camera for *The Old Mill* (1937) (Barrier 1999; Gabler 2006). These milestones are undeniably significant, marking the medium's rapid evolution from a novelty act to a sophisticated cinematic art form. However, this technocratic focus frequently obscures a more profound, structural reality: the Disney Studio of the Golden Age (1937–1942) functioned not merely as a factory of moving drawings but as a laboratory for the operationalization of a specific metaphysical system.

The prevailing "Great Man" theories of Walt Disney tend to categorize his aesthetic obsessions as a vague, mid-western Romanticism—a nostalgia for the pastoral and the innocent. This reductionism fails to account for the sudden, radical emergence of a specific metaphysical architecture in the studio's first feature film, *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* (1937). Within a span of three years (1934–1937), the studio transitioned from the "rubber hose" mechanics of the early 1930s—where characters possessed motility without interiority—to a narrative universe governed by a rigorous "physics of the spirit." In this new universe, the environment mirrored the internal state of the protagonist, animals functioned as externalized affections, and the narrative arc followed a precise eschatological trajectory of spiritual death, vastation, and celestial resurrection.

### **1.2 The Research Objective: Identifying the Carriers**

Recent scholarship, particularly forensic investigations into the "Disney Style," has identified a structural isomorphism between this narrative architecture and the theology of Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772), specifically his *Doctrine of Influx* and *Science of Correspondences* (*The Animating Spirit and the Hidden Temple*; *The Mechanics of the Soul*). The central research problem of this investigation is retrospective and biographical. If the studio was consciously encoding Swedenborgian-compatible thought by 1948—as evidenced by the production of *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed* and the correspondence of executive Hal Adelquist (Fryeburg New Church Assembly, n.d.)—who were the "Original Transmitters" during the foundational era of 1934–1937? Who introduced the specific narrative deviations in *Snow White*—such as the "Cloud Castle" and the "Awakening by Kiss"—that align so precisely with Swedenborgian eschatology?

This report posits that the transmission of this metaphysical framework was not the work of a single "secret operative" but the result of a **Tripartite Convergence** of personnel, pedagogy, and milieu. By investigating the biographical backgrounds of **Richard Creedon**, **Ted Sears**, and **Hal Adelquist**, contextualized by the pedagogy of **Don Graham** and the presence of the **Church of the New Jerusalem** in Los Angeles, we reveal a studio culture that was actively permeable to, and structurally aligned with, the "New Church" vision of the relationship between the spiritual and the natural worlds. This investigation utilizes a forensic biographical approach to map the "religious DNA" of the story department, suggesting that the "Disney Magic" was, in fact, a secularized operationalization of the Swedenborgian worldview.

**II. The Contextual Reservoir: The "Garden of the Lord" and the Los Angeles Metaphysical Milieu**

To understand how a complex 18th-century theology could infiltrate a 20th-century commercial animation studio, one must first map the "cultural water table" from which the studio drew its talent. Los Angeles in the 1920s and 1930s was not merely a boomtown for cinema; it was the "spiritual frontier" of America, a region saturated with esoteric movements that viewed art as a vehicle for spiritual revelation.

### **2.1 The "Garden of the Lord" Movement in California**

The aesthetic philosophy that defined the Disney Golden Age—specifically the hyper-pastoralism of *Snow White* and *Bambi*, where nature serves as a sacred cathedral—did not emerge in a vacuum. It was the visual culmination of a specific Californian theological movement known as the "Garden of the Lord."

Historically, the Protestant denominations that united in the American Home Missionary Society (1826) hoped to transform the American West into a "Garden of the Lord," a moral and spiritual paradise filled with churches and schools, and in 1835 Lyman Beecher's *A Plea for the West* called for institutions that would make it so (PBS, n.d.). By the turn of the 20th century, this theological concept had merged with the nascent environmental movement, particularly through figures like John Muir.

Muir, whose influence on the Californian imagination was total, operated within a social network deeply permeated by Swedenborgian thought. He and his friend William Keith, the artist, were both in the circle of the Swedenborgian minister Joseph Worcester; Keith, a close friend of Worcester, painted the four canvases that hang in Worcester's Church of the New Jerusalem in San Francisco (Maley and Stock 2002). Theodore Roosevelt, eulogizing Muir, gave the vision its name, crediting Muir's influence with preserving the "wonderful canyons, giant trees, slopes of flower-spangled hillsides—which make California a veritable Garden of the Lord" (Roosevelt 1915).

This "Garden of the Lord" ethos—the belief that the natural world is a direct, legible manuscript of the Divine—is functionally identical to Swedenborg’s *Doctrine of Correspondences*. When Disney artists depicted the forest in *Snow White* reacting to the heroine's terror, or the "Cathedral of Nature" in *Bambi*, they were visually participating in this specific Californian theological tradition. The Los Angeles environment in which the studio operated was one where nature was not viewed as dead matter, but as a spiritual container—a concept that would become the cornerstone of the Disney aesthetic.

### **2.2 The Church of the New Jerusalem in Los Angeles**

Contrary to the assumption that Swedenborgianism was a distant New England phenomenon, the **Church of the New Jerusalem** had an active, physical presence in Los Angeles during the studio’s formative years. Records confirm that the Los Angeles Society of the New Jerusalem was located at **509 South Westmoreland Avenue** throughout the 1930s and 1940s (*Los Angeles Times* 1935–1942; *New-Church Messenger* 1953).

This edifice stood as a tangible reminder of the "New Church" in the heart of the city. The society was active, its services advertised in the city's church notices through the years of *Snow White* (*Los Angeles Times* 1935–1942). The presence of a formal church structure on Westmoreland Avenue means that Swedenborgian texts, tracts, and ministers (by 1942 the Rev. Andre Diaconoff, still its pastor in 1953) were accessible within the city’s metaphysical network (*Los Angeles Times* 1935–1942; *New-Church Messenger* 1953).

Furthermore, the Los Angeles society was not an isolated outpost; it was connected to the circle that would build a Swedenborgian chapel on the coast: Lucy Pyle Mercer Billingslea, a member of the Los Angeles church, approached Narcissa Cox Vanderlip, also a Swedenborgian, to give the land for it (Wayfarers Chapel, n.d.). The church's existence provides a physical location for the "diffusion" of ideas. It legitimizes the possibility that Disney personnel, many of whom were searching for "spiritual truths" compatible with their artistic vocation, could have encountered these doctrines directly.

### **2.3 The Palos Verdes Nexus and the Wayfarers Aesthetic**

The influence of this community extended geographically to the **Palos Verdes** peninsula, an area that would eventually host the **Wayfarers Chapel** (The Glass Church), designed by Lloyd Wright (son of Frank Lloyd Wright) as a National Memorial to Swedenborg (Wayfarers Chapel, n.d.). While the chapel itself was not completed until 1951, the "dream" of the chapel and the Swedenborgian community in Palos Verdes existed much earlier.

Elizabeth Sewall Schellenberg, a Swedenborgian living in Palos Verdes in the late 1920s, conceived the idea of the chapel (Wayfarers Chapel, n.d.). The coast had drawn Disney artists before the chapel rose: in 1934 Lee Blair painted **Mary Blair**, later the stylist responsible for the color and look of *Cinderella* and *Alice in Wonderland*, standing on a Palos Verdes bluff (Bawden-Davis 2016).

The "Palos Verdes Nexus" suggests that for at least one of the Disney creative elite, Swedenborgianism was not a fringe cult but the "church of the artist." It offered a theological framework that validated their work: if the natural world corresponds to the spiritual, then the artist’s job of creating "living" worlds is a priestly vocation. This nexus served as the "Sanctuary" where the studio's implicit theology could be practiced explicitly.

**III. The Pedagogical Substrate: Don Graham and the Secularized Doctrine of Influx**

Before identifying the specific narrative transmitters, we must establish the methodological transmitter. The "Original Transmitter" of the theology did not have to teach religion; they only had to teach a method of drawing that required the artist to view the world through Swedenborgian physics. This figure was **Don Graham (d. 1976)**.

### **3.1 The Crisis of Ensoulment and the "Mind is the Pilot" Memo**

In the early 1930s, Walt Disney faced a "Crisis of Ensoulment." His characters moved (the "rubber hose" style), but they did not *live*. They lacked interiority. To produce a feature film like *Snow White*, the characters needed to appear to have souls—internal wills that drove their external actions.

The solution to this crisis was codified in a memo from Walt Disney to Don Graham dated **December 23, 1935**. In this pivotal document, Walt wrote:

"In other words, in most instances, the driving force behind the action is the mood, the personality, the attitude of the character—or all three. Therefore, the mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them" (Disney, 23 December 1935).

This directive is not merely an acting tip; it is an ontological claim. It establishes a hierarchy of causation where the spiritual (Mind/Mood) is the active cause and the natural (Body/Action) is the passive effect. This structure is identical to Swedenborg’s *Doctrine of Influx*, which posits that the natural world is dead until animated by an influx from the spiritual world. As Swedenborg wrote in *Divine Providence*: "The body’s activity in speech or deed is only an effect from the external and internal of man’s spirit, for the body is so much obedience" (*Divine Providence* §103).

### **3.2 Action Analysis as "Applied Influx"**

Don Graham, recruited from the **Chouinard Art Institute**, operationalized this doctrine through his "Action Analysis" classes. Graham taught animators to draw "Forces," not "Forms": "The artist is not drawing a form at all, but indicating a force" (Graham, 21 June 1937). The drawing on the page (the form) was merely the container for the energy (the force) that flowed through it.

Graham’s background is significant. He taught at Chouinard, an institution that was deeply permeable to the Theosophical currents of the time (Karlstrom 1996; Crosse 2014). Theosophy, heavily indebted to Swedenborg (via Blavatsky), taught that "thoughts are things, and puissant things" that take visible form (Besant and Leadbeater 1901). Walt Stanchfield, heir to Graham's pedagogy, titled one of his own lectures "The Metaphysical Side" (*The Mechanics of the Soul*).

By training the "Nine Old Men" and the core staff to see the material world as a shell for spiritual energies, Graham created a "receptive soil." When story men like Creedon or Sears proposed that a character’s internal "goodness" should make them physically beautiful (Correspondence), or that an evil character should be destroyed by their own hatred (Vastation), the animators accepted this not as a narrative contrivance but as physical law. Graham was the *enabler* of the transmission, creating a studio language that was structurally Swedenborgian in form, even if the label was absent.

**IV. The Structural Architect: Richard Creedon and the Internal Causality of Evil**

If Don Graham provided the physics, **Richard Granville Creedon (1896–1964)** provided the blueprint. As the author of the foundational *Snow White* tentative outline, Creedon emerges as the leading candidate for the "Structural Architect" who injected the concept of **Internal Causality** into the studio's narrative logic.

### **4.1 Biographical Origins: The Radio Dramatist**

Richard Granville Creedon was born in Chicago on April 10, 1896 (Snow White Museum, n.d.b). His professional trajectory is the key to his contribution.

Creedon came to Disney from **KHJ Radio** in Los Angeles, where he was a scriptwriter; Walt Disney hired him for a Mickey Mouse radio show (Snow White Museum, n.d.b). KHJ was a pioneer in radio drama, a medium that relies entirely on "interiority." In radio, the visual world does not exist; the character is defined solely by voice, intent, and the "theater of the mind." A radio writer must constantly communicate the "invisible" state of the character to the audience.

When Creedon arrived at Disney, he brought this discipline of interiority to a medium obsessed with external gags. His transition marked a shift from the "sight gag" (external) to the "psychological drama" (internal). This professional bias toward the "invisible" made him a natural, albeit perhaps unconscious, conduit for the Swedenborgian prioritization of the Spirit over the Body.

### **4.2 The October 1934 Story Conferences and the Tentative Outline**

In October 1934 Walt Disney met three times with Creedon and the lyricist Larry Morey to settle the story; after the last meeting, on October 15, Creedon prepared an eighteen-page **tentative outline**, dated **October 22, 1934**, of which some fifty copies were circulated in the studio (Barrier 1999; Barrier 2007). The conference of October 9, minuted as "Suggestions and notes on Snow White," had Walt Disney, Albert Hurter, Creedon, Ted Sears, Pinto Colvig and Morey around the table and took up, among much else, the chase and the Queen's death (Walt Disney Productions, 9 October 1934). Creedon's outline is the "Rosetta Stone" of the film's narrative structure: it sets down the whole story as agreed at that stage, from the storybook opening to the castle in the clouds, with a gag sheet appended (Creedon, 22 October 1934).

Crucially, Creedon introduced the concept of the **Self-Destruction of Evil**. In his tentative outline the death of the Queen is reimagined. In the original Grimm fairy tale, the Queen is punished by an external authority (forced to dance in red-hot iron shoes at the wedding) (Grimm and Grimm 1857). Creedon and the story team rejected this "external judgment." The outline asks for a "clever piece of business whereby the Queen, in trying to kill the dwarfs, kills herself," and proposes one: she tries to roll a huge boulder upon her pursuers and is knocked over the precipice (Creedon, 22 October 1934).

### **4.3 The Swedenborgian Isomorphism: Vastation**

This shift from external execution to internal self-destruction is a hallmark of Swedenborgian moral theology, specifically the concept of **Vastation**. Swedenborg teaches that the Lord casts no one into Hell; rather, evil spirits cast *themselves* in because they are drawn by the gravity of their own "ruling love" (Selfhood): "the Lord casts no one into hell, but everyone casts himself into hell" (*Heaven and Hell* §548; see §§545–550). Evil is its own punishment; it consumes itself.

Creedon’s insistence on this point—that the Queen creates the instrument of her own demise—suggests a sophisticated understanding of "moral physics" that aligns with the *Doctrine of Influx*. In the finished film she is levering the boulder down on the dwarfs when lightning strikes, and she falls from the cliff with the boulder after her (Snow White Museum, n.d.c): death overtakes her in the act of her own attack. Whether Creedon derived this from a specific theological source or his dramatic instincts is secondary to the result: he established the *law* of the Disney universe where internal state dictates external fate. He proved that for the film to work emotionally, the villain could not be "judged" by a court; she had to be consumed by her own nature.

**V. The Theological Editor: Ted Sears and the Conjunction of Love and Truth**

While Creedon built the structural frame, **Ted Sears (1900–1958)**, head of the story department, served as the gatekeeper who refined the spiritual mechanics of the narrative. His background suggests a deep engagement with the religious currents of New England, the historical heartland of American Swedenborgianism.

### **5.1 Biographical Origins: The New England Milieu**

Ted Sears was born **Edward Sears** on March 13, 1900, in **Greenfield, Massachusetts** (Snow White Museum, n.d.d).

A Boston University general catalogue of the 1910s lists an "Edward R. Sears" at 119 Meridian Street, East Boston (Boston University, n.d.). While it is unconfirmed if this is the same Edward R. Sears (father or relation), the connection to Boston is significant. Massachusetts, and specifically Boston, was the epicenter of the Swedenborgian movement in America, and Swedenborg reached the region's Transcendentalist and Romantic thought directly: Emerson made him one of his *Representative Men* (Emerson 1850).

Born in Greenfield, Sears would have been exposed to the "New England Theology"—a blend of Puritan rigor and Transcendentalist mysticism that often acted as a carrier for Swedenborgian ideas (via Emerson and the James family). This cultural "reservoir" helps explain Sears's specific role at the studio: the guardian of "tone" and "heart."

### **5.2 The "Prince's Kiss": Resurrection via Conjunction**

Sears is almost systematically listed among the participants of the *Snow White* story meetings (Snow White Museum, n.d.d), where he served as the "Theological Editor" who checked the film’s emotional resonance. The **Prince’s Kiss** as the mechanism of awakening was in the story from Creedon's tentative outline onward: the Queen's book of poisons warns that "only the kiss of a great love can revive the victim," and at the end of his song "the Prince bends and kisses Snow White" (Creedon, 22 October 1934). As head of the story department, Sears presided over the meetings that carried it into the film.

In the Grimm original, the awakening is mechanical: the coffin is jolted by the servants carrying it, and the piece of poisoned apple is dislodged from Snow White's throat (Grimm and Grimm 1857). It is an accident. In the Disney version, the awakening is spiritual: it is "Love’s First Kiss."

From a Swedenborgian perspective, this change is profound and explicitly theological.

* **The Sleep of Death:** Corresponds to the state of the soul consumed by falsity (the poison apple/faith alone).  
* **The Prince:** Corresponds to **Divine Truth** (Intellect/Wisdom).  
* **Snow White:** Corresponds to **Divine Good** (Affection/Love).  
* **The Kiss:** Corresponds to **Conjunction** (The Heavenly Marriage).

Swedenborg explicitly teaches that love and wisdom in heaven and the church "are one when will and understanding, thus when good and truth, make one," and that for this reason "the Lord is spoken of in the Word as bridegroom and husband, and heaven and the church are called bride and wife" (*Divine Providence* §8). The awakening of the soul (resurrection) can only occur through the conjunction of the Will and the Understanding. By rejecting the "mechanical accident" of the Grimm tale and insisting on the "intentional conjunction" of the Kiss, the story team encoded a soteriology (doctrine of salvation) that is strictly Swedenborgian. They ensured that the film’s climax was a spiritual event, a *hieros gamos* (sacred marriage), rather than a biological hiccup.

### **5.3 The "Self" and the Rejection of Chanticleer**

The studio's later choices point the same way. *Chanticleer*, the feature Marc Davis and Ken Anderson developed about a rooster who believes his crowing causes the sun to rise, was turned down in 1960 meetings with Walt Disney, two years after Sears's death; Davis remembered the excuse as "you can't make a personality out of a chicken" (Barrier 2007). Through the lens of the *Tripartite Convergence*, this rejection is intelligible as a rejection of the **Self** (self-love).

In Swedenborgian theology, the belief that one is the source of life or light (selfhood) is the root of all evil. A character who believes they cause the sun to rise is, in Swedenborgian terms, in a state of profanation.

> **[Editorial Note]**: The self is selfhood — the vessel that must form before it can receive. It becomes the root of evil only when it *claims* what flows through it as its own (self-sourcing). The Chanticleer character illustrates this claiming perfectly — but the self itself is not evil; the directional error of self-sourcing is. See: *The Human Who Showed the Way*.

The studio's instinct to reject this character suggests an intuitive alignment with the ethic of humility and the acknowledgment of Influx (that life flows *from* the Lord, not *from* the self).

**VI. The Institutional Guardian: Hal Adelquist and the Documented Bridge**

If Creedon and Sears were the implicit encoders of the 1930s, **Hal Adelquist (1914–1981)** emerges as the strongest documented link between the Golden Age and the explicit Swedenborgianism of the post-war era. Of all the candidates, Adelquist possesses the most robust documentary connection to the New Church.

### **6.1 Biographical Origins**

Hal Adelquist was born in Salt Lake City, Utah, on July 11, 1914, and worked at the Disney studio from 1932 to 1956 (Snow White Museum, n.d.a).

His significance does not lie in his ancestry. It lies in two documented positions: he was head assistant director on *Snow White*, and in 1948, as manager of the Story Department, he answered a Swedenborgian church librarian about the studio's treatment of Johnny Appleseed. These two positions explain why Hal Adelquist—a man born in Utah and raised in Los Angeles—stands at both ends of the period this report examines.

### **6.2 The 1937 Witness: The Assistant Director**

Adelquist joined Disney in 1932 (Snow White Museum, n.d.a). He rose rapidly through the ranks: early in February 1937 he was named head assistant director on *Snow White* (Gabler 2006), and he served as assistant director to William Cottrell, Perce Pearce and Hamilton Luske on sixteen of its sequences (Snow White Museum, n.d.a).

This places him physically in the room during the "Tripartite Convergence." He was present when Creedon’s structural mandates and Sears’s theological edits were being translated into animation. He witnessed the "encoding" process firsthand. Unlike the older men, Adelquist was a "native" of the studio system, absorbing its "physics of the spirit" as his formative professional training.

### **6.3 The 1948 Correspondence: Documented Theological Engagement**

The hypothesis of Adelquist as a "Conscious Guardian" moves from speculation to documented fact with the production of *Melody Time* (1948), specifically the segment *The Legend of Johnny Appleseed*.

In 1948, **Florence Murdoch**, the Librarian of the Cincinnati Church of the New Jerusalem and a fierce guardian of Johnny Appleseed’s Swedenborgian identity, wrote to Walt Disney after seeing an advertisement for the film: she was pleased that it would include Johnny's Blessings Three, but concerned that the studio was taking too much liberty with spiritual concepts, above all the coonskin-capped guardian angel in the ad (Fryeburg New Church Assembly, n.d.).

Adelquist, now the Story Department Manager, replied with a defense that reveals deep, specific knowledge:

"We trust that after you have viewed the picture you will agree that we have treated the angel with sincere respect and endowed him with a certain dignity" (Fryeburg New Church Assembly, n.d.).

The studio, he wrote, had **researched the real Chapman extensively**. This admission is critical. It is difficult to research John Chapman extensively without encountering his defining characteristic: his role as a missionary for the Church of the New Jerusalem. Chapman did not just plant trees; he distributed Swedenborg's *Heaven and Hell* across the frontier. Adelquist's letter strongly suggests that the studio *knew* Chapman was a Swedenborgian.

Furthermore, the film retains the specific theological code of the "Blessings Three": **"love and faith and an apple tree"** (Fryeburg New Church Assembly, n.d.). This triad corresponds perfectly to the Swedenborgian Trinity of Essentials:

1. **Love** (Will/Charity)  
2. **Faith** (Understanding/Truth)  
3. **The Apple Tree** (Use/Works/Action)

Swedenborg teaches that "charity and faith make one as good and truth do" (*Divine Providence* §82); the apple tree, the deed that bears fruit, completes them in use. By preserving this triad, the studio encoded the sect's core soteriology while stripping away the sectarian label. Adelquist's defense of the "Pioneer Spirit" (the angel as a deceased human, rather than a separate created race, which is a key tenet of Swedenborgian pneumatology) further supports his role as a theological guardian. He used his sixteen years in the studio to navigate the delicate task of presenting a Swedenborgian saint to a mass audience without triggering the censors.

### **6.4 The Tragic Decline**

Adelquist’s later life adds a tragic dimension to this narrative. After helping to launch *The Mickey Mouse Club*, he fell out of favor, was demoted to finding performers for the show's Talent Round-Up (Armstrong 2010), left the studio in 1956, and died in Long Beach in 1981 (Snow White Museum, n.d.a). His trajectory mirrors the studio’s own drift away from the high idealism of the Golden Age. As the "Guardian" of the spiritual legacy, his departure coincided with the studio’s shift toward the more commercial, less metaphysically rigorous output of the 1960s and 70s.

### **7.2 Second-Order Insight: The Disney "Moral Physics"**

The data suggests that the Walt Disney Studio of the 1930s and 40s functioned as a **"Secular New Jerusalem."** It was a laboratory where the metaphysics of the New Church were stripped of their explicit theological terminology and rebuilt as an industrial process for creating entertainment.

The "Magic" of Disney is not a vague marketing term; it is a specific, replicable system of "moral physics" where:

1. **Mind is the Pilot** (Influx): The internal state creates the external movement.  
2. **Environment is a Mirror** (Correspondences): The world reflects the moral state of the character.  
3. **Evil is Self-Consuming** (Vastation): Villains are not executed; they destroy themselves.  
4. **Awakening is Conjunction** (The Heavenly Marriage): Salvation requires the union of Love and Truth.

### **7.3 Conclusion: The Unconscious and Conscious Church**

The investigation concludes that the transmission of Swedenborgian ideas into the Disney Studio occurred in two phases.

**Phase I (1934–1937): Implicit Transmission.** This was driven by **Richard Creedon** and **Ted Sears**, utilizing the pedagogical tools of **Don Graham**. They likely did not view themselves as "Swedenborgian missionaries." rather, they were artists solving narrative problems. However, because they drew from a cultural reservoir saturated with Romantic, Transcendentalist, and Theosophical ideas (all downstream from Swedenborg), the solutions they found—Internal Causality, Conjunction—were structurally Swedenborgian.

**Phase II (1940s): Explicit Stewardship.** This was driven by **Hal Adelquist**, in the decade when the Swedenborgians of Los Angeles laid the cornerstone of the Wayfarers Chapel (1949). By 1948, with *Johnny Appleseed*, the studio was no longer just accidentally aligning with these ideas; they were actively engaging with the source material and defending it in answer to Florence Murdoch. Adelquist, present at both ends, served as the bridge between the implicit intuition of the Golden Age and the explicit acknowledgement of the Silver Age.

The "Disney Style," therefore, is the result of a specific theological lineage—from the "Garden of the Lord" in the Californian wilderness, through the "Action Analysis" of Chouinard, to the story meetings of Hyperion Avenue. It is the visual gospel of the New Church, encoded in celluloid, preaching that the visible world is nothing more than the obedience of the invisible spirit.
